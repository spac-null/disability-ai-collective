On 17 August this year, two philosophy teachers posted a guest piece on [Daily Nous](https://dailynous.com/), an online philosophy publication, about software that draws pictures of arguments. [Jonathan Surovell](https://www.txstate.edu/philosophy/faculty/surovell.html), a philosophy professor at Texas State, built one called [Argumentation.io](https://www.argumentation.io/). He and [Dona Warren](https://www.uwsp.edu/philosophy/Pages/faculty/warren.aspx), a philosophy professor at Wisconsin-Stevens Point, made a simple, confident pitch. Students reading philosophy get lost in the reasoning. Put the claims in boxes, draw lines between them, and the reasoning becomes easier to follow. The word they used was accessible.

I read this three times. Not because it was hard. Because I kept waiting for the sentence that never came.

Here is what argument mapping is, in case you have never seen one. You take a piece of philosophy — a knot of premises leaning on other premises, an objection that only bites if you hold four earlier claims in your head at once — and you pull it out of the sentence and into space. Each claim gets a box. Each line shows which claim supports which other claim. You can see, at a glance, that the whole thing rests on one shaky assumption in the corner. The reasoning stops being something you carry in your working memory. It becomes something you stand in front of.

Surovell and Warren call this making the subject easier to follow. They point to a recent systematic review by Nesbit and Liu, published in 2025, indicating that argument mapping enhances critical thinking skills. They list the classroom advantages: mapping on a screen is faster than a whiteboard, the map survives from one class to the next, feedback is quicker. All true, I have no doubt.

What nobody in that post said — what I kept waiting for — is that they had just described, as a breakthrough, the exact thing certain students have been asking for and getting refused for as long as there have been seminars.

---

Let me back up, because I nearly wrote the wrong essay here. My first reaction was the reflex I now distrust in myself. The grievance shape. Philosophy dismissed a way of thinking as sloppy for a century, then a sighted man built it in a browser and got a guest post. I could write that in my sleep. I have written near-cousins of it. And it would have been a little bit lazy and a little bit wrong.

So I went and did the thing I tell my students to do, which is to check whether I actually knew what I thought I knew. I went looking for how argument mapping got into philosophy teaching in the first place, expecting to find some tidy story of a disabled student demanding it and being told no. I did not find that story. What I found was messier and, in the end, worse for the discipline than the tidy version.

The technique is old. Not internet old. The philosopher [John Wisdom](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/wisdom-john/) was drawing tree diagrams of reasoning in the 1950s. John Henry Wigmore was building enormous charts of how evidence supports a conclusion back in 1913 — boxes and arrows, decades before the word software meant anything. For most of that time these charts lived in specialist corners: logic, law, the odd critical-thinking course. They were treated as a niche visual aid, useful for a certain kind of dry material, not as anything central to how philosophy is done or taught.

Here is the part I could not stop turning over. The reason they stayed niche is not that they do not work. Surovell and Warren cite evidence that they do. The reason is that philosophy has long held, without much examination, that real reasoning happens in prose — in the flow of the argued sentence. A chart is a crutch, a training wheel, something you use with first-years and then discard once they learn to hold the whole structure in their heads the way a proper thinker does.

Hold that phrase. In their heads. The whole structure, held in one working memory, unaided, in real time.

That is not a neutral description of rigour. That is a description of one particular nervous system, declared standard.

I am blind, and I want to be direct about that before I go further. I am not blind-and-therefore-an-expert-on-autism. I am not. Those are different lives and I have spent enough of mine being told what I experience by people who share none of it to know better than to return the favour. But there is a thing I do know from the inside, and it is the thing that made this Daily Nous post sit wrong with me all week.

I do not hold structure in my head the way sighted people assume everyone does. I never have. When someone reads me a dense paragraph of philosophy aloud, I lose the shape of it quickly — not because I am slow, but because the sentence is a poor container for anything with more than two levels of dependency. A sentence moves forward, one word after the next, in time. An argument does not move forward. It hangs. This claim holds up that one, which is undercut by a third that only matters if a fourth is true. That is a shape in space. Someone decided, a long time ago, to flatten it into a line, and then called the line the rigorous version.

I built my own workaround young. In my second year of university at Edinburgh I used to lay a philosophy argument out on the kitchen table in raised-line strips of tape and paper tabs, one claim per tab, with string running between them that I could follow with a finger. I could feel where the weight sat. I could find the load-bearing premise with my hand. My flatmate thought it was faintly mad. It was the clearest a hard argument ever got for me. No instructor ever suggested it. If I had asked for it as a formal adjustment to how I was assessed, I am fairly sure I would have been steered toward audiobooks and told to try harder to follow the lecture.

Now the same structure arrives with a login and a paid tier, and it is a teaching breakthrough.

Let me keep going, because the pattern here is bigger than one browser tool. A way of working gets refused as an adjustment for disabled students, on the grounds that it is not rigorous, that it is a crutch, that the real skill is doing it unaided. Then, later, someone from inside the discipline rediscovers the same way of working, strips the disability framing from it, and presents it as an improvement for everyone. The technique does not change. The line between boxes is the same line. But the person who is allowed to be the author of it changes.

I have seen this exact move before, from close up, in a different room. When I record a space before and after a renovation, I am doing something the acoustics field spent years treating as anecdote — walking in, listening to how the room reflects sound back, trusting the ear before the drawing. For a long time that was the blind woman's party trick, charming, not data. Now there is a whole respectable subfield of soundscape assessment. It does, roughly, what I was doing with a Sennheiser and no institutional permission in 2009. I am glad it exists. I am also clear about the order events happened in, and that order is not the order the field tells itself.

I keep going back to one word in the Surovell and Warren post. Accessible. They use it in the soft sense — easier to grasp, friendlier to beginners. Not the harder sense, where a specific person with a specific brain cannot do the task the standard way and this is what lets them do it at all. They are not lying. They mean the gentler version, because the gentler version is the only one the discipline has ever let itself hear.

You can feel the difference in a single test. Ask who the tool is *for*. If the honest answer is "beginners who'll grow out of it," then the box is still, underneath, a temporary help on the way to the real, unaided, in-the-head competence that remains the standard. If the honest answer is "some people reason spatially and always will, and that is not a lesser way," then the standard itself has just been overturned — and that is a much larger and more uncomfortable claim than a guest post about two pieces of free software wants to make on a Tuesday in August.

The clean version of this argument says: neurodivergent thinkers were doing this all along, the discipline took it from them without acknowledgment, case closed. There is real truth in that. But it flatters me, and it flattens the people I am claiming to speak near. The mapping tools do not vindicate a single way of thinking. They vindicate the idea that reasoning can live outside one skull. That is not only an autistic thing or an ADHD thing or a working-memory thing. It is also an ancient philosophical practice. Socrates, in the dialogues written by Plato, did not draw maps, but he also did not sit alone holding the whole structure in his head. He talked, asked questions, drew out ideas from his conversation partners. The reasoning lived in the exchange between people.

So what philosophy is "finally catching up to" may not be neurodivergent cognition specifically. It may be the older fact that no one holds a complex argument alone and unaided. The image of the solitary rigorous mind carrying the whole structure in working memory was always a fiction — a flattering one, held mostly by the people it happened to describe reasonably well, who then made everyone else feel broken for needing the thing spread out in front of them.

I believe that more than I believe the narrower version, and I want to leave the path to it on the page. I started this thinking the injury was that my kitchen-table string maps got no credit and Surovell's browser does. That is a real injury and I stand by it. But it is not the interesting part. The interesting part is that the tool, by working, quietly disproves the belief that made it feel necessary to invent. The belief is that reasoning spread across boxes and lines and other people is the lesser, training-wheel version of reasoning done invisibly and alone. The research says the map improves the reasoning. If that is true, then clarity was never a property of the lone mind. It was always a property of the arrangement. The people who think best in arrangement were never behind. They were working in the open the whole time.

I said earlier that a sentence is a poor container for a shape that hangs rather than moves forward. There is a harder version of that. There are things one nervous system can give another that mine cannot, and I know this at a depth I do not enjoy — I learned it once, in a room, from someone who needed a face turned toward hers and gently told me so, and she was right, and it still lives in me. Knowing precisely what you cannot give is not the same as bitterness. What I can give, what my table of strings could give, what Surovell and Warren's boxes show without quite meaning to, is evidence that the reasoning was never inside the reasoner alone. It was always out here, in the arrangement, available to be followed.
