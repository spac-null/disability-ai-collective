Among the projects gathered from the University of Ljubljana's Academy of Fine Arts and Design, one arrives dressed as a courtesy. Zoja Čepin's Multisensory Exhibition Experience is described as a system that "makes artworks accessible beyond sight," developed through interviews and testing with blind and partially sighted people. In that framing it reads as an accessibility add-on: a tool for a specific group of visitors, fitted to a gallery that otherwise stays exactly as it was. Something bolted on, thoughtfully, at the edge of an untouched room.

Look at how it actually works. Each booklet translates an artwork into three progressively detailed tactile layers, allowing visitors to move from composition to key features at their own pace. The whole comes first. Then the parts. Then the finer parts. The visitor decides when to move from one layer to the next.

That sequence is not a substitute for looking. This is where the courtesy reading breaks. A tactile version of an artwork is easy to picture as the same encounter routed through the fingers — the glance made touchable, one sense standing in for another. But composition-then-detail, taken at a tempo the visitor sets, is not the glance in another form. It is a different way of taking in an artwork altogether. The work is not delivered whole and instant. It is built up, in order, over time the visitor controls. The order is part of the meaning now. So is the pace.

Once you see that, the ordinary gallery encounter starts to look like a decision rather than a given. The standard encounter assumes that meaning arrives all at once. You stand in front of a painting and the whole thing is there before you, composition and detail together, and the pace of that arrival is set by the display — by how the object is hung, lit, framed for the eye. The layered booklet does none of this. It refuses the all-at-once. It insists on a sequence and hands the clock to the visitor.

And in refusing it, the booklet names it. The conventional encounter had a structure all along — composition first, then detail, understanding assembled in an order — but the speed of sight hid the assembly. Vision seems to give you everything simultaneously, so the building-up disappears into an instant, and the instant passes for the natural shape of looking at art. The tactile layers slow that instant down until its parts separate. What looked like a single act turns out to have joints. The booklet shows the joints.

The project carries this further, and the source lets it. The result, it states, is "a richer, more inclusive encounter with art for everyone." That word — everyone — is the design reaching past the visitors it was tested with. The proposition is that the hidden structure, once made explicit and adjustable, is not only useful to those who cannot rely on sight. If composition-then-detail at a chosen pace is a clearer way to take in a work, then making it available might change the encounter for any visitor at all. The claim is not that touch helps some people see. It is that the ordered, self-paced structure is better understanding, full stop, for anyone who uses it.

That is the design's own extension of itself, and it should be marked as such, because the source does not carry it across the line into evidence. Interviews and testing with blind and partially sighted people are recorded as having happened. What those people experienced, what they concluded, how many took part, what changed for them — none of this is in the source. The material describes a project and its claims, not measured outcomes. No installation in an actual museum is stated. So "for everyone" stands where the design placed it: as a proposition drawn from the logic of the object, not a result that has been shown. The gap is not a flaw to be papered over. It is the exact edge of what can be said.

But notice what does not depend on that claim being proven. Whether or not the layered booklet ever demonstrably enriches the encounter for a sighted visitor, it has already done something to the standard encounter simply by existing beside it. It has made a hidden assumption visible. The claim "for everyone" may or may not hold; the exposure holds either way.

Whether or not the "for everyone" claim is ever borne out, the project has already made the hidden assumption visible: that the standard gallery encounter treats meaning as arriving all at once, at the display's pace, through sight — and that this was a structure of understanding all along, not a fact of art.
