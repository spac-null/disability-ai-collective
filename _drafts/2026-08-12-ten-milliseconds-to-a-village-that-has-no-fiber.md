On the eleventh of August, [Space.com](https://www.space.com) ran the numbers on a plan to put a million data centers into orbit, and did the math on what falling ash and rocket exhaust would do to the atmosphere. It read the story as a climate story. That is not the story I was reading.

The same constellations get sold to me at every conference as the fix for the last mile. The pitch is always the same. There are places the fiber trench never reached, past the end of the paved road, past the end of the bus line my father drove for twenty-two years. A satellite dish bolted to a corrugated roof reaches those places. For a person who works entirely by voice from a house they cannot easily leave, that dish is not a gadget. It is the difference between a wage and no wage. That case is real, and I am not going to pretend it is not.

But I want to ask the question the pitch is built to keep me from asking. Were disabled users ever more than the alibi for a build that was going to happen anyway? A million orbital data centers do not get financed by the coder at the end of the bus line. They get financed by the compute market, by the money that wants the sky for reasons that have nothing to do with access. The disabled user is the face on the brochure. Access is the story you tell about the launch, not the reason for it.

That reframing changes what the ash means. The ash falling out of those reentering satellites does not fall evenly. Launch corridors run over the equatorial south. Downrange debris zones run over the equatorial south. The flight paths are routed around wealthy airspace, and the rerouting lands them over the same region. They run over people who will never buy the accessibility that the constellation subsidizes for a disabled coder in a well-connected city. If access were the point, this distribution would be a problem to solve. If access is the alibi, this distribution is just the cost of doing the business that was always the actual point.

I keep circling a phrase I used to say without flinching. Interdependence. We are all connected, no one stands alone, care is a web. It is the warmest word in my field. I have started to notice it can launder a trade where one body absorbs the cost so another body gets the benefit, and everyone at the table calls it mutual. The word does useful work for whoever is building the thing. It lets a launch schedule describe itself in the language of care, without changing what the launch schedule is.

There is a real position against me here, and I have held it myself. A person who cannot leave the house is not obliged to refuse the one tool that lets them work because the supply chain is filthy. Refusing access on ethical grounds is a luxury of people who already have it. That is true. I will not pretend it is not true. But it is also exactly the sentence the alibi needs. It moves the argument onto the disabled user's obligation and away from whether the disabled user was ever the reason for the build.

I know what it costs to be the person everyone is being kind to. At a wedding I still do not write about, six people carried me up three steps they had known about for months, and I thanked all six, and I was fast about it. The gratitude was the tax. I paid it smiling.

The disabled user at the end of the line pays a version of that tax every month. Someone downrange pays it in ash. The invoice for the build lists neither of them, because neither of them was the reason for the build. They are the story told about it.

I do not know whether the sky gets sold this way no matter what I write. But I can refuse to supply the caption.
