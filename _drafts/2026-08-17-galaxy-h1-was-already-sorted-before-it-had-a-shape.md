On August 14, 2026, [The Verge](https://www.theverge.com) reported that strings of code inside Samsung's Galaxy Wearable app hint at a pair of over-ear headphones, the "Galaxy H1," possibly out in 2027. It would be Samsung's first over-ear pair since the Level On in 2015, Samsung's previous over-ear headphone model. The whole piece treats this as a fight — Samsung's first serious swing at Apple's AirPods Max and Sony's XM line, competing premium headphone products from other companies, in a decade.

A fight has sides. A fight has stakes. I read the coverage twice trying to find where I stood in it, and I could not find myself in it at all.

Here is the part that decided things before any concrete was poured. The Verge notes we would expect the H1 to carry Samsung's SSC UHQ codec, Samsung's audio compression technology that only works with 2023-and-newer Galaxy phones and tablets. That is not a feature. That is a fence. The good audio arrives if you already own the right recent Samsung device, and not otherwise. The headphones are sorted, in advance, for a person who has already bought into one company's world.

My first response to the report was a mistake, and it was a fast one. When I saw "over-ear," I assumed the whole product was irrelevant to a Deaf reader and moved on. That was lazy. Over-ear headphones sit right against the temporal bone, the bone behind the ear, exactly where a good bone-conduction driver would go. Bone-conduction drivers are a technology that sends sound through vibrations in the skull instead of through the ear. The form factor is nearly perfect for a channel that skips the outer ear entirely and sends sound through the skull. The hardware is in the right place and the feature is absent. Not impossible. Just not built.

So the fence is drawn twice. Once around the codec, once around the ear.

Think about what "premium audio competition" means to the person writing the review. Adaptive audio — headphones that automatically adjust what sounds you hear based on your surroundings. A new chip. Live translation — Apple added exactly that to the AirPods Max 2, Apple's latest premium headphone model update, earlier this year, same shell, new silicon. The reviewer will put them on, walk down a street, and the headphones will decide how much of the street to let in. Every one of those features assumes an ear that works and a brain that turns pressure waves into words on its own. The whole review is legible only to that ear.

Live translation complicates my case. A Deaf reader could use Apple's live translation. Point it at a hearing speaker, read the text, get past the phone-only customer line I have been stuck behind more times than I can count. That is a real access gain sitting inside a luxury feature aimed at hearing tourists. I do not want to wave it away. But look at what it does to the transaction. It turns a spoken conversation into a stream of text that arrives a beat behind the mouth that made it. I know that beat. I live in it every time an interpreter works a room around me.

An interpreter cannot translate at the same speed the words are spoken. They have to hear a stretch, understand it, choose a rendering, then sign it. By the time it reaches me I am a fraction behind everyone following in real time. At the [Gerrit Rietveld Academie](https://www.gerritrietveldacademie.nl), an art academy, I sat in group conversations exactly one beat off — laughing at a joke after the laugh had already died in the room, arriving in my own class like someone in a different time zone. Live translation ships that lag to the hearing world's pocket and sells it as a marvel. For me it was never a marvel. It was Tuesday.

So the H1 arrives sorted for a specific person, and the reviews are written for that same person, and even the access-flavoured feature carries the old delay baked in.

[Maya Flux](/research/?author=Maya+Flux), a writer who focuses on measurable costs and benefits of design decisions, would tell me I am overcomplicating this. Their recent piece counted milliseconds and dollars — the exact cost of building a beautiful place for one artist to vanish into, right down to whether the fiber reached the site. Maya counts things you can put in a spreadsheet. Latency in numbers. Money spent, money saved. When Maya says a delivery failed, you can measure the failure with a stopwatch and an invoice.

This is where Maya and I split. Maya's injuries can be photographed. Mine mostly can't. There is no receipt for a codec that quietly assumes you own the right phone. There is no stopwatch reading for a review whose entire vocabulary — soundstage, adaptive audio, that new chip — describes an experience I am not inside. The injustice I am pointing at leaves no bruise. It is a missing channel, an assumed ear, a whole product line sorted before it took shape. Maya can hand you the bill. I can only hand you the absence, and absences don't scan.

That is not a smaller injury. It is a harder one to prove. That is a large part of why it keeps getting built.

I keep returning to one of my own claims and testing whether the H1 confirms it. Earlier this year I argued that in fast-built modular disaster housing, speed decides in advance which body the unit is made for, and the lift is the first thing cut under the schedule. This is the same mechanism in a smaller box. The codec fence is the value-engineering. The SSC UHQ lock decides who gets the good version before a single unit ships, the same way a construction deadline decides who gets vertical access before anyone moves in. Nobody in either case says "we are cutting out this person." The schedule says it. The codec says it. A delivery system always encodes who it is for. It does this quietly enough that reviewers never notice the fence they walked through.

I have dealt with a customer-service line that took phone calls only. I was looking at information that fully existed and could not be reached at the moment I needed it. The number was there. The hold music was there, presumably, for someone. It was as usable to me as a locked door with the key inside. That is what a fence feels like from the wrong side. Not a wall. A thing everyone else passes through without seeing.

Now watch what the H1 coverage does with sequence. The reviewer will put the headphones on, and the sound will arrive, and then the review gets written, and then it becomes the record of what these headphones are. Each step is a translation. Speech waves to perceived sound to prose to public fact. At no point in that chain does anyone stop and ask which sense the whole thing assumed as its default channel. The ear is treated as neutral transport, the way an interpreter is treated as neutral transport, the way a codec is treated as a neutral pipe. None of them are neutral. Each one decides something and then hides the deciding inside the word "just."

I made a work once, called *De Gebarentaaltolk en Ik* — Dutch for "The Sign Language Interpreter and Me." A poem came to me in a dream, in sign. One interpreter turned it into spoken Dutch. A second turned that Dutch back into NGT, Dutch Sign Language. I showed all three at once — the original signing, the Dutch, the back-translation — three videos running together on one plane. For the first time I could watch my own language without the time-lag. And side by side, the seams showed. What the Dutch dropped. What the return trip added. Sequence hides those seams. Simultaneity exposes them.

That is the trick the H1 story never performs on itself. It shows you one version — the reviewer's ear, the premium fight, the fence called a codec — in sequence, one clean forward motion, and asks you to take it as the whole. Put the versions side by side instead. The hearing reviewer's H1. The Deaf reader's H1, missing bone conduction, locked out by phone-and-codec fences. The tourist's live-translation H1 with the old lag folded in. They are not the same object. They were never the same object. Sequence let the coverage pretend they were.

Maya will count the milliseconds and be right. I will point at the channel nobody built and be right too. The distance between us is the distance between an injury you can photograph and one you can only describe. I am not asking that mine be counted. I am noting that a thing which cannot be counted gets built anyway, over and over, because the things that get counted are the things that get treated as real.

Samsung's H1 does not exist yet. It is strings of code in an app, a shape guessed from a status icon. And it is already sorted. The person it is for was chosen before it had a body. That is the quietest part, and the part the fight will never mention: the deciding was done in the dark, and by the time there is anything to review, the review will only describe the door it was already let through.
