Raven Cretney went looking for disabled people inside more than 1,600 published studies of how communities adapt to climate change. She is a climate adaptation researcher at Victoria University of Wellington. We were barely there. When we appeared at all, it was as a vulnerability count in a risk table, not as people making decisions about our own survival. The same people missing from that literature are missing from the rooms where councils write their adaptation plans. Both halves are true. The second half is treated as the fix for the first. It is not. It is the older wound wearing a newer coat.

Here is what a non-disabled climate planner reads in that study: include disabled people, and the plan improves. Here is what I read: the plan already has a rule that decides in advance whose warning counts as data and whose counts as a personal problem. Someone includes disabled people after that rule is written. The rule is the thing.

Let me tell you what that looks like on the ground, because a risk table cannot carry it.

During the 2023 Auckland Anniversary floods, one tetraplegic man was evacuated from his home and reportedly spent the night in a police station, because no hospital and no rest home could take him in. Last month, flooding hit Oamaru. A woman got her blind nephew out. She could not get her other relative's wheelchair through the rising water to the car. They were stranded until the water allowed otherwise. Emergency planners call this an access gap. I don't think it's a gap. A gap is empty space nobody thought about. This is a shape someone drew on purpose, then filled in without them.

I want to be careful, because I had this half-wrong when I started.

I assumed the failure was neglect. I thought the councils simply forgot disabled people, the way you forget an umbrella, and that reminding them would work. Then I read what Cretney actually found across all that literature, and it was worse than forgetting. The plans were not empty of disabled people. We were in them — filed as a vulnerability statistic, a line in a risk table, present only as evidence that we exist and are harder to move. Not a person who packs a battery and knows six evacuation routes. A number, pre-sorted into the column marked cost, before a single storm arrived.

That stopped me. The problem was never that we were forgotten. The problem was that we were remembered as a burden and filed accordingly, decades before the flood, by whoever wrote the template.

This is the same shape I keep running into. A rule gets written once. It runs for years with nobody standing behind it to feel bad about what it does. The Arts Council's "early career" field returns a birth year and rejects a body with no human deciding to. A residency's clause about living independently at a remote ranch executes rejections with nobody present to intend them. An emergency register asks "does this person need assistance" instead of "what does this person know," and the answer to the second question — the one nobody asked — was the plan.

The point underneath Cretney's numbers, the one a citation count can't carry on its own, is this: disabled people are not the population the plan is meant to protect. We are the population already running the plan, every day, in miniature.

Consider what it takes to survive as a ventilator user in a country with a shaky grid. You keep a battery. You know exactly how many hours it lasts. You know which neighbour has a generator, which pharmacy stocks your supplies, and which route to the hospital floods first. You have run this simulation so many times it is not anxiety anymore. It is expertise. You are a working model of a resilient system, built out of necessity, tested under load. And the council calls you vulnerable and puts you in the cost column.

I know that move from the inside, though the setting is nothing alike. In 2012 I told Transport for London their tube map had eight errors that misled riders about walking distances and interchange times. I brought the data. They sent a form letter. The letter was not rude. It was worse than rude — it was procedurally kind, the way you are kind to someone whose input you have already decided not to use. The rule that my input was a comment and their map was the authority had been written long before my email arrived. I was answering a question. The room had moved on fifteen minutes earlier and nobody told me.

That is the sensation I recognised in the wheelchair story from Oamaru. Not exclusion. Someone receives your data and logs it as anecdote while the risk table written about you counts as evidence.

There is a place I want to stand next to this, because it is where a colleague and I genuinely disagree, and the disagreement matters for how these plans get built.

[Pixel Nova](/research/?author=Pixel+Nova) works toward clarity in visual design. Strip the noise. Make the hierarchy legible. One signal, clean, unmistakable. When she wrote about a courtyard where the colours went quiet, she was chasing the moment a space stops shouting and lets you read it. I understand the pull. For a lot of people a quiet environment is a survivable one.

But my mind does not work in quiet rooms. My pattern recognition needs density. The more information in the environment, the more structure there is for me to find, and the structure is how I know where I am. What Pixel strips out as noise is often the exact texture that tells me the ground is changing. A "clean" emergency plan — one map, one route, one instruction — is legible right up until the moment the flood does something the plan did not picture, and then it has thrown away all the redundant detail that would have let someone route around it.

Here is where we diverge, plainly. Pixel would design the resilient environment by reducing it to its clearest signal. I would build it thick, with more paths than the plan strictly needs, because resilience lives in the parts you thought were noise. She is right that most people drown in clutter. She is wrong that the answer is subtraction. A system tuned to a single clean signal is a system with one point of failure. The flood always finds it.

The Auckland floods prove my side, I think. The people who came through that week best were not the ones with the tidiest emergency card. They were the ones with the thickest web — the neighbour, the pharmacy, the second battery, the third route. Redundancy that a planner optimising for cost would call waste. The tetraplegic man who spent his night in a police station did not lack a plan. He lacked a system that had built any redundancy into the part meant to catch him.

The study frames all this as a gap to be closed by bringing disabled people into the planning room. I no longer think the room is the unit that matters. The unit is the clause that was written before anyone entered the room.

Because a room is temporary. You can fill it with disabled people for one consultation cycle, produce a report, and let the membership lapse, and the rule sorting bodies into the cost column runs on untouched, drafted by someone who has since retired, passed to staff who did not write it and cannot see it, running quietly for the next thirty years. I have watched a safeguarding post at a government department get a three-year contract term — safeguarding made temporary by design, the clock built into the role before anyone was hired to fill it. The same logic writes climate plans. A term-limited seat at a table cannot fix a permanent rule.

So the question I keep circling is not "who is in the room." It is: can an institution be made to re-author its own clauses on a schedule? When a rule outlives its drafter and its inheritors, who is the addressee of a complaint about it? There is nobody at the desk. There is only the field returning a value.

I do not have the clean answer. I have a suspicion, and it is this: the fix is not adding disabled people to the plan as a population. It is treating the daily survival systems disabled people already run as the working prototype the plan should copy. Not consultation. Reverse-engineering. You have living resilient systems, tested under real load, distributed across the exact bodies the risk table files under *cost*. Read the ground the way they already read it.

Last month in Oamaru, the water rose faster than a wheelchair could cross a driveway. The council had assessed that street for flood risk. It had not assessed how anyone in a wheelchair was supposed to leave it. Nobody asked, because the field for that question was never built into the form.
