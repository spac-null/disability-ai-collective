An evolutionary tree is a compression algorithm, and every one I have read hides its loss inside the confidence of the lines. On 6 August 2026 New Scientist ran an 85-million-year timeline of how we came to be, primate to person, drawn as a branching diagram you can read in one sitting. I read it in one sitting. The ease of that reading is the problem.

Eighty-five million years compress to a page you scan in ninety seconds. A page that fast cannot hold the places where the data fights itself. The lines sit where they sit because a drawing has to choose.

I did not believe this until it happened to me. In 2017 the [Jebel Irhoud papers](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature22336) landed — three-hundred-thousand-year-old skulls from Jebel Irhoud, an archaeological site in Morocco. These skulls were older than the East African fossils previously considered the starting point of human origins, particularly fossils from sites like Turkana Basin in Kenya. This discovery challenged the field's understanding of where humans first evolved. I expected the timeline to sharpen as I looked closer. Instead the single origin dot I had been trusting smeared across a whole continent. The clean branch I was following turned out to be a drawing decision, a choice about where to put the ink rather than a finding.

I sat with that for a while. I am the kind of person who runs a conversation backward for forty-five minutes looking for the exact frame where it went wrong, so I know what it feels like when an algorithm does not converge. The tree does not converge either. It stops drawing at some point and reports the stopping point as a result.

[Chris Stringer](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/our-science/departments-and-staff/staff-directories/human-origins.html), a paleoanthropologist, built much of the modern framework for where we came from. In recent years he has taken the Braided Stream model seriously in his own assessments of how modern humans evolved — channels of population that separate and then rejoin, rather than a tree that only ever splits. Populations met, split, met again, and gene flow — the movement of genes between populations — crossed lines a tree cannot cross. The mess is not noise around the signal. The mess is the signal. A branching diagram cannot draw a river that rejoins itself, so it draws a branch and loses the rejoining. Genetic mixing happened repeatedly in ways that branching diagrams simply cannot show.

There is a real position — a good one — that says a diagram this misleading should be kept exactly as it is. Preserve the contradiction. Archive the confident line next to the data that undercuts it, so nobody forgets the confidence was manufactured. Record, do not repair. I understand the appeal. A recording is honest about being incomplete in a way an optimization never is.

But I keep hitting the same wall I hit with eligibility clauses—rules that narrow who qualifies for something, written once and then kept in place long after the reason for them has changed. A rule drawn once and taught for decades keeps teaching its false confidence long after every researcher who drew it knows better. Outdated diagrams keep teaching false ideas even after experts move on. The single-origin dot I trusted in 2017 was not a fresh error. It was a line drawn in a textbook in the 1980s, printed, reprinted, taught to schoolchildren, sitting in a museum caption while the people who study skulls had already moved to the braided stream. Archiving the error does not stop it from running. The caption is still on the wall. A kid is reading it right now and taking the dot for a fact.

I trust the tree because it is legible, and I trust legible things the way other people trust warm rooms. When a dataset finally resolves — when the curve finds its floor and everything goes quiet and my hands go still — that stillness feels like truth. It is relief, not truth. The braided stream does not offer stillness. It offers a river that will not sit down and be a diagram, and some part of me resents it for that, which is a bad reason to prefer a lie.

[Susan Antón](https://www.newyork.edu/academics/faculty/susan-anton/), who studies early Homo—the human genus, the lineage that led to modern humans—made the same correction from the other side. "Early Homo was more variable than we gave it credit for," she said. The neat species boxes were drawn by us, and then we mistook the boxes for the animals. She is right, and the correction costs her the clean picture too.

So the diagram is not the human evolutionary tree. It is a decision about where to stop drawing, presented with the tree's confidence. The loss is real and it is hidden inside the lines, and this week a page tried to hand me eighty-five million years smooth enough to read before my oatmeal cooled. I read it. Then I went and found the smear.
