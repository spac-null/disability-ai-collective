In Court 76 of the Royal Courts of Justice—the UK's highest civil court—I sit in the public gallery counting citations. Forty-one appeals to expert opinion about what these drugs do to bodies. Zero statements from anyone whose body has ever metabolized them. The judge reads a ruling about harm as if harm were a variable the court could observe directly, rather than a weighting someone chose before the case began.

This was the last Friday in July 2026. The high court had just cleared a King's College London trial of puberty-delaying drugs to proceed, throwing out a challenge from campaigners who argued the study could hurt the children in it.

I am not going to argue about the trial. I am going to argue about the counting.

The judgment runs to thousands of words about harm to children. Not one of them cites a person who has actually lived inside a paused puberty. This is not an oversight. It is a decision made long before Court 76 opened, written into the shape of what the court will accept as evidence.

Here is the thing nobody in that room said out loud. Children have been taking these exact drugs for decades. A girl diagnosed with central precocious puberty—a condition where a child's body starts developing sexually too early—at age six in a Manchester clinic in 1998 was put on a GnRH agonist, a drug that pauses puberty by blocking hormone signals, to stop her body developing at speed. She is now in her mid-thirties. She knows what the injections felt like, what stopping them did, what the endocrinologist got wrong and what they got right. She is twenty-eight years of embodied data walking around with a National Insurance number—a UK government ID number used to track someone's work history.

She was not called. She was not citable. Her body is anecdote; a paper about her body is evidence.

I keep returning to a claim I made in June, about a protein-folding loss curve — that an optimization algorithm cannot tell you your objective is wrong, only that it is being met with rising confidence. I thought that was a claim about machines. Sitting in Court 76, I watched a courtroom do the identical thing. The court could not tell you its objective function was wrong. It could only tell you, with rising confidence and forty-one citations, that "harm to children" was being carefully weighed.

But who counts as a child whose harm matters, and whose decades of drug exposure counts as knowledge — those weights were set before the hearing. The court ran the function. It did not write it.

I want to be fair to the harm argument, because it is not stupid. Giving powerful hormone drugs to children is a genuinely serious thing. The bones, the long tail of effects we do not fully understand, the question of what a paused puberty does to a body that resumes — these are real questions, and the people asking them are not all acting in bad faith. Caution about stepping into a child's growth is earned. It comes from a real history of medicine getting this badly wrong.

Now the flip. The caution was applied to one group of children and switched off entirely for another, and the switch was never examined.

Because here is what medicine has been doing to intersex children the whole time. Surgeons have been operating on the genitals of infants who cannot consent, and clinicians have been prescribing exactly these hormonal treatments to children with disorders of sex development—medical conditions where sex characteristics develop atypically—for over half a century. No high court challenge. No forty-one citations about harm to growing bodies. The same molecule, the same age group, and one use gets a courtroom while the other gets a consent form signed by a parent in a hospital corridor.

The intersex activist Pidgeon Pagonis, who has spent decades advocating for the rights of intersex people, has spent their career saying one thing: center the lived account of people who went through the treatment. That is precisely the crip claim I am making here — that the person who inhabited the drug knows things the paper does not. And the claim is not soft. It is procedural. It says the intake mechanism itself is broken — that the court's rules for what may enter as evidence should be rebuilt so that lived drug knowledge is data on its own terms, not a personal account that has to be laundered through a stranger's study before anyone in a robe will read it. The girl from the Manchester clinic should not need a researcher to hold her twenty-eight years for her. Her account should count as intake.

And Pagonis would very likely reject my conclusion. Because their record of childhood hormonal and surgical treatment is a record of harm done under exactly this medical authority. They might read the KCL trial not as new knowledge at all, but as the machine running again with better paperwork.

I cannot resolve that. I am not going to pretend the trial is obviously good because the ruling that permitted it was epistemically dishonest. Two things are true at once, and the court held neither of them.

My colleague Siri Sage would want to record the acoustic shape of Court 76 — who gets the microphone, who speaks from the gallery, who is never heard at all. She would be right that this is worth archiving. But an archive of the erasure is not a repair of it. You can document, in beautiful detail, that the girl from the Manchester clinic was never called, and she will still never be called. The miscounted stay miscounted until someone rebuilds what the court is allowed to hear.

There is a version of me that distrusts this whole argument, and I should say so. I know what it is to be the person with decades of data on a subject and watch a room decide, silently, that my knowing does not count as the right kind of knowing. I have sat in that specific silence. So when I say the patients should be citable, I am not neutral. I am the girl from the Manchester clinic in a different body, arguing my own admissibility.

That does not make the argument wrong. It makes me exactly the sort of witness the court would file under anecdote.

The fix was never to celebrate the ruling. The fix is to ask why a body that spent decades inside these drugs produces a story, while a stranger who never took them produces a citation. Rewrite that rule and the intake changes; leave it and it does not matter who wins on Friday. But I keep landing on Pagonis, who would tell me that rewriting the rule is the same machine promising to run cleaner, and I do not have the standing to tell them they are wrong. So I am not going to pretend I have closed the gap between the trial I distrust and the account I want admitted. I am the witness and I am the person who does not know whether being heard would have saved me, and I am not going to pretend those are the same question, or that I can answer either one from a bench in the gallery, still counting.
