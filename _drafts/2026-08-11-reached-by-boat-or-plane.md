An arts foundation called [Iolair](https://www.iolair.org) spent this summer building a place for one artist to disappear into. On August 10, 2026, the architecture magazine [Dezeen](https://www.dezeen.com) published photographs. Seattle-based architectural studio [GO'C](https://www.gocstudio.com) wrapped a small building in ebony-stained cedar on Orcas Island, in the Salish Sea, on a site that was once an apple orchard. The building is 1,600 square feet. It holds a gallery that turns into a studio and a sleeping room. The island, the write-up notes plainly, is reached by boat or plane.

I read that last line three times. Not because it surprised me. Because of how little work it was doing — a fact stated the way you'd state the ceiling height, weight-bearing and neutral, a thing everyone would agree was simply true and simply fine.

Here is what I want to know, and did not know when I started. What does a building sound like when it has been designed around the idea that getting there should be hard?

Iolair is an arts residency program that brings artists from around the world to live on the island, work in solitude, and — the foundation's word — draw on the island's "spirit." The architects were asked to hold two things at once: privacy for the artist, and room for the public to come and look. GO'C put it plainly: the building had to "provide privacy for the resident artist, yet also accommodate public visits and exhibitions." That is a real design problem. They solved it with an L-shaped plan that wraps a courtyard, so the private end and the public end never have to meet in the middle.

That plan is honest about who the building is for. Two kinds of person: the one who lives there, and the ones who visit. Neither of those people arrives with any friction the building has to answer for, because the friction happened earlier — on the water, in the air — before anyone reached the cedar.

I want to be careful here, because I nearly wrote the wrong essay. My first draft was about how the building has no ramp and I could have filled a thousand words with the missing ramp. Then I remembered a morning in Amsterdam, in March 2019, standing in the entry hall of a concert building I'd been hired to assess for acoustics and accessibility. Everyone kept apologising for the stairs. I told them the stairs were the least of it. The problem was the hall itself. It was six metres of polished stone. It swallowed the low end and threw back the high end, so a walking stick tapping the floor came back as a bright useless click that told you nothing about where the walls were. A blind person walked into that hall and went instantly, acoustically, deaf to the space. The ramp they eventually built let you in. The room still refused to tell you where you were.

That morning corrected me. Access is not the last hundred metres. Access is the whole route, and the route to Orcas Island begins at a ferry terminal on the mainland, in a queue of cars, in a schedule that does not bend.

So let me stay with the route, because the route is where the sound lives, and the sound is where the assumptions hide.

A ferry is not a neutral corridor. It is a sequence of very specific acoustic events, and you learn to read them if that is how you build the world. The terminal hall is all hard surface and public address: a sailing announcement bounces off tile until the vowels are all that survive. The steel ramp onto the vessel rings under a cane in a way concrete never does. The engine room hum comes through the soles before it reaches the ears. Then the boat pushes off and the whole soundscape changes register, because now you are moving and the water is doing something the terminal never did. I have recorded ferry crossings with a professional microphone in the Firth of Forth and in the Dutch Wadden Sea. The recordings are almost useless as documents and completely reliable as feeling. You always know, from the sound alone, the exact second the land lets go of you.

Now put a working artist in that sequence who cannot make the crossing on the terms the crossing is offered. Not one kind of person — many kinds. A four-hour journey with two transfers isn't romantic; it's a week of recovery that costs working time. Some artists cannot drive, and "reached by boat or plane" quietly assumes a car at both ends. Some need medication that requires refrigeration and a pharmacy within reach. The point is not that these people can't get to Orcas Island. Plenty could, with enough money and help. The point is that the building's founding idea treats the difficulty of arrival as a feature. Solitude, remoteness, communion with the island's spirit — all sold as gifts. The hardness of getting there is sold as part of the art.

That is the assumption I want to pull apart. Not that the building excludes anyone on purpose. Remoteness has been made romantic, and romantic remoteness is only romantic to a body that experiences remoteness as choice rather than as a wall.

Think about the artist who reads a residency brochure and sees the photo of a jetty. For plenty of disabled artists, that jetty is a stop sign — not because the jetty is malicious, but because the person who chose that photo has never once been stopped by a jetty, and so doesn't know it was a decision they made.

Nobody who designs the retreat has been stopped by the retreat. So the stopping becomes invisible, then it becomes natural, then it becomes spiritual.

The idea that the artist needs to withdraw from the world to make real work is not old craft wisdom. It is a nineteenth-century invention, roughly contemporary with the railway, and it hardened into orthodoxy exactly as cities filled up and the countryside became a thing you visited rather than lived in. The retreat sells solitude as the precondition for art. But solitude was never the precondition. Access to solitude was. The monastery, the writer's cabin, the island studio — every one of them required a body that could get there and stay there, and every one of them quietly wrote off the bodies that couldn't as bodies that were not really serious about art.

Back to the cedar building, because I owe it precision, and precision means admitting what's good.

The courtyard is entered through a gate nearly three metres tall, galvanised steel, designed and fabricated by local artist Zackarya Leck. I want to be honest that this gate complicates my whole line of thought. A steel gate that size, worked by hand, is not a smooth featureless barrier. Steel that has been hammered and welded has a voice. It rings differently along its length depending on thickness and join, and a heavy gate on a real hinge announces itself when it swings — the low groan, the settle, the latch. If you arrive at that courtyard through the gate, you will hear the threshold before you touch it. The building has, without meaning to, given its entrance an acoustic signature that a blind visitor could actually use. It did this by accident, because a local craftsman made something with his hands instead of ordering a flat aluminium panel from a catalogue.

I did not want the gate to be good. It is good. The most legible part of the approach wasn't intentionally designed that way.

Which brings me to a disagreement I keep having with people who work in access, and who I think are mostly right.

Their view says the fix is information. Put the ferry timetable and the transfer distances and the surface types and the door widths on the foundation's website, so a disabled artist can decide in advance whether the residency is possible. Make the invisible route visible. Assess it, write it up, sign it. There is a version of this building's failure that is entirely a failure to tell people things, and the answer is to tell them.

I understand why that view wins. It is buildable. You can write the document this afternoon. But it accepts something I can't accept. The route stays exactly as hard as it always was. The only thing that changes is that now you can read about that hardness in advance. That is not access. That is a warning label. A blind artist does not need a better description of the six metres of polished stone that will render her deaf to the room. She needs the room to not do that. The building is where the decision was made — in cedar and steel and an L-shaped plan and a jetty — not in the absence of a paragraph on a website.

I'll go further, and this is where I part from people I respect. Making the route legible can quietly make the wall permanent. Once the foundation has written up the four-hour journey and posted it online, an artist who attempts the crossing is treated as someone who chose it with full information. The foundation posts the difficulty; the artist reads it and applies anyway; the exclusion becomes a personal decision. The building gets to keep its romance intact. The hard arrival is now a thing the artist opted into rather than a thing the design imposed.

I don't have a clean answer to this and I'm not going to pretend I do. Disabled artists genuinely cannot plan without knowing the route, and no amount of my complaining about romantic remoteness feeds anyone or gets them across the Salish Sea. But I keep coming back to the sound of that concert hall in 2019, and the way everyone in it wanted to talk about the stairs and the signage and the printed guide, and nobody wanted to talk about the fact that the room itself was built to be silent about where its own walls were. The document is the stairs. The room is the building. We keep fixing the stairs.

Here is the thing I did not resolve. I don't actually know whether the artist Iolair chooses will be someone who could never have made the crossing, or someone for whom the whole romance of the secluded island is exactly right, and I'd be arrogant to decide that for them from Amsterdam. What I know is narrower and harder. The building was conceived, in its founding idea, as if the difficulty of reaching it were a gift. And there is a whole class of body for whom that difficulty is not a gift, has never once been a gift, and cannot be reframed into one by any brochure photograph of a jetty at golden hour.

There's a private version of this I don't put in the essays, usually. Years ago someone I loved needed something from me in a moment of real distress and told me, gently, that she needed a person who could give her the one thing I could not give — to read her face across a room. She was not wrong. There are things I cannot give. I have spent a long time being precise about that, and being precise did not make it stop.

I think about that when I read the phrase "in communion with the island's spirit." There is a communion the island's spirit offers that some bodies simply cannot receive on the terms it is offered, and no amount of my insisting otherwise changes it. But that is exactly the sentence the retreat never says out loud. It never says: this communion is for some bodies and not others, and we have decided which. It says: reached by boat or plane. Neutral. Weight-bearing. Fine.

The oculus on the northern end of the building is a large circular window meant to pull daylight deep inside. The foundation's name, Iolair, is Gaelic for eagle, and the architects called the window an eagle's eye. An eagle sees a mouse from a mile up. It also cannot, under any circumstances, tell you what the water sounds like the second the land lets go of you.
