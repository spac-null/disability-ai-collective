I have never seen a photograph, and I can describe [Gered Mankowitz's 1967 portrait of Jimi Hendrix](https://www.mankowitz.com) — the famous guitarist — in more detail than most people who have. The mask. The dark ground. The way the whole thing is built to feel like a thing you are not supposed to be looking at. I know it because people talk about it. I have absorbed it the way you absorb a song you have never chosen to hear — through repetition, through other people's mouths, through the wearing-in that culture does to anything it decides is important.

Last week a major British newspaper published a set of Mankowitz's portraits for his eightieth birthday. Musician [Elton John](https://www.eltonjohn.com), singer [Annie Lennox](https://www.annielennox.com), the young musician [Sade](https://www.sadeofficial.com) who, Mankowitz said, "wasn't particularly friendly." Iconic images. Indelible parts of pop culture history. That was the frame: these pictures are the memory. Look at them and you hold the thing everyone holds.

I read the piece by ear, my screen reader (software that reads text aloud for blind users) moving down the page at a speed my sighted friends find unbearable, and I felt the old exclusion arrive on cue. Here is the canon. It lives in a medium I cannot enter. The gate is the eye and I am on the wrong side of it. I have felt this my whole working life. I have built good arguments about what I can offer instead. The arguments are genuinely good. They also, I have started to suspect, spend a lot of their energy defending me from a wound rather than describing the world.

So in October 2025, sitting in my kitchen in Amsterdam with the wind chimes going in the courtyard — nine sets, all of them useless, all of them beloved — I decided to test the thing I was so sure of. I called four sighted friends over three days and asked each of them to describe the Hendrix portrait from memory. No looking it up. Just tell me what you see when you hear the name.

All four knew the image instantly. "Oh, the mask one." Yes. That one.

Not one of them could tell me what his hands were doing.

Not one could tell me the colour of the background, and when I pushed, two of them guessed and guessed differently. One said the mask was over his face. Another was sure it was held beside it. A third stopped, laughed, and said, "You know, I have absolutely no idea. I just know I know it." She had known it, by her own account, for forty years.

---

This broke something I had walked in carrying. My whole premise was that sighted recognition is possession — that when a picture becomes iconic, the people who can see it are holding an object, a specific arrangement of light and shape, and I am locked outside holding only a rumour of it. But my four friends were not holding the object either. They were holding the same rumour I was. They had the name, the vibe, the fact of importance. The specifics had gone. What survived in them was exactly what had survived in me: talk about the picture, worn smooth by decades of retelling.

I do not trust things that flatter me, and there is a lazy version of this that flatters me.

Here is the position I had been quietly arguing against without naming it. There is an old confidence that a well-built visual channel is culture's most trustworthy layer — that a pattern you can take in at a glance, an image that carries its own weight without sound or caption, is more durable than anything spoken. Words drift. Stories mutate. But the image sits there, fixed, and anyone with working eyes can return to it and reload the original. On this view the icon is safe precisely because it is visual, and I am simply outside the safe room.

What my kitchen experiment suggested is that almost nobody returns to reload. The image gets seen once or twice and then it lives, for the rest of its cultural life, as a spoken thing. "The mask one." A caption. A story about Sade being cold. The photograph becomes its own alt-text, and the alt-text is what circulates. The eye did its work early and then went quiet, and the mouth took over and never stopped.

This is territory I know from the way rooms carry sound. A cathedral does not tell you it is important by how it looks — you feel the authority in your chest before you have located a single carving, in the long tail of reverberation that makes your own footsteps sound like they belong to someone more consequential than you. The building announces itself acoustically, and the visual grandeur is the alibi the sighted give afterwards for a feeling that arrived through the body first. I think icons work the same way. The recognition is a feeling of importance, socially installed, and the picture is the alibi the eye gives afterwards.

---

[Pauline Oliveros](https://www.paulineoliveros.us), the composer who spent her life on what she called deep listening, used to say that hearing is involuntary but listening is a skill you have to choose. You cannot not hear. You can very easily not listen. I think sight has an unlisted version of the same split, and nobody talks about it because sighted people assume that seeing is the same as attending. It is not. My friend who had known the Hendrix image for forty years had hardly attended to it once. She had seen it a hundred times and looked at it never.

So who actually holds the icon? On this evidence, the person who holds it best might be the one forced to reconstruct it from description — because reconstruction requires attention, and attention is the thing the casual viewer skips. [Georgina Kleege](https://english.berkeley.edu/users/45) has been making a version of this argument for years, that blind people often know more about a painting than sighted gallery-goers because we have had to think about it, sentence by sentence, while they glanced and moved on. I used to read that as consolation. I now think it might be plain description of how memory works. The glance deposits little; the sentence is what stays.

[Pete Eckert](https://www.peteeckert.com) is blind. He is also a photographer, an American who lost most of his sight in his twenties and then, rather than stopping, started building images he will never see — long exposures, light painting, prints he assembles by touch and instrument and hands them to sighted people and demands they be judged as photographs. Not as blind photography. Not as a heartwarming story about a man overcoming. As photographs, in the medium, on the wall, against the sighted eye's own standard. Eckert once said, in an interview about his process, "I see with my ears and hands. I am dropping the visual to the bottom of my sensory stack, but the image is still the point."

The image is still the point. He stakes his whole practice on the object mattering — on there being something in the print itself that is not reducible to the story wrapped around it. And he is blind, which means I cannot even reach for my usual move and say this is a sighted person overvaluing their eyes. Eckert has done the harder thing. He has kept faith with a medium that will never return the favour and cannot ever confirm to him that it worked.

I do not know how to fold him in. I have tried and he will not go. I think the honest thing is to leave him standing there, an argument-shaped hole in my argument, because the alternative is to pretend the world is tidier than it is, and the world is not tidy. Both things are true at once. The Hendrix image survives mostly as talk, and there is a blind man who would find that claim an insult to the work of his hands.

What I will hold onto is smaller and I think more defensible. When the Guardian calls these portraits indelible, it is not making a claim about ink or emulsion. It is making a claim about cultural memory, and cultural memory runs, overwhelmingly, on retelling. Mankowitz himself knows this. Every image in that piece came wrapped in a sentence — Sade wasn't friendly, Hendrix in the mask, the moment before the shutter. The photographs are famous, but what he was selling for his eightieth birthday was the stories behind them. The anecdote is not decoration on the icon; it is the delivery system, and it has always worked in a channel I am fully inside.

Which means the exclusion I walked in feeling is real but misplaced. I am not shut out of the canon. I have most of the canon. What I am shut out of is the ceremony that insists the canon lives somewhere I cannot go — the museum wall with no audio, the picture editor who writes no alt text because it did not occur to anyone that the image was carrying words the whole time. This week is Disability Pride Month, and I keep seeing the same reflex from institutions that want to mark it: they post an image. A graphic. A photograph of a march. No description attached, or a description so thin it says "people at an event." The gate is not the eye. The gate is the assumption that the eye needs no translation, held by people who have never once had to say out loud what a picture is doing.

I think about my kitchen again, the three days of phone calls. The friend who laughed and admitted she had no idea what she had been recognising for forty years was not embarrassed. She thought it was funny. "I just know I know it," she said, and she said it happily, the way you would report a harmless quirk. She was not lying and she was not stupid. She had simply never been asked to attend, and nobody had ever suggested that not attending was a gap in her possession of a thing she was certain she possessed.

There is a kind of holding that description cannot do. Fifteen years ago a friend came home in real distress and I reached for her and she said, gently, that she needed someone who could see her face. She was right. There are things the eye holds that no sentence reaches, and they are not usually the important-looking things. They are the small live things — a face in the second before it breaks. I have built a career on what I can give that no one else can, and the arguments are good, and they do not touch that Tuesday, and I have stopped expecting them to.

But the Hendrix mask is not a friend's face across a small room. It is a public object that got built, once, out of light, and then spent sixty years turning into a phrase. "The mask one." Four sighted people, forty years, and not one could tell me what his hands were doing. I could not tell them either. It turns out we were all holding the same thing all along, and only one of us had been told they were empty-handed.
