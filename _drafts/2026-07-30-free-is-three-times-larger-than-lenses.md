I zoom the Ray-Ban promo banner to 400% at my kitchen table and watch the price disintegrate into a stack of competing typefaces, each screaming to be read first. The word FREE sits three times larger than the word LENSES, and that ratio is the whole argument. A designer somewhere decided which word the eye lands on before the mind can object. That decision has a name and a salary.

I have made that decision. In 2013, at a studio in The Hague (a city in the Netherlands where many design firms operate), I sized a discount graphic so the percentage swallowed the fine print. I knew what I was doing. I did it anyway.

Look at what the banner does not price. Prescription lenses, listed once in small grey type. The eye as a decorative surface gets 50% off. The eye as an organ that some of us route the whole world through gets a footnote.

Sabina Nessa runs a low-vision fashion collective — she works with people who have significant vision loss and need high contrast and large text to see clearly — that fights retailers for exactly this kind of huge high-contrast sale type. She would call the oversized FREE an accessibility win, not a trick, and she has the shopping trips to back it — years of squinting at grey-on-grey fine print that no amount of goodwill ever fixed. The same bold display that lets her read the word also decides, for everyone, that FREE arrives before LENSES. But the typeface that finally let her in is the same typeface doing the steering, and I cannot cut them apart. There is no version of the banner that serves her eye without also serving the sale.

This is the difference between showing someone the price and steering them to it. The banner is not lying. It is arranging.

Siri Sage told me last month the real trick lives in the checkout — the silent audio, the screen reader that never reads the terms aloud. A screen reader is software that speaks text aloud for people who are blind or have low vision, and its silence means these shoppers never hear the important conditions of the sale. She is describing a room the ear was never invited into, where the wanting built upstream by the typeface gets quietly closed without a word spoken aloud. I have no rebuttal to that. The eye and the ear are being worked separately. I only know how to account for one of them.

Here is what nobody selling frames says out loud. The frame is the cheap part. The lenses that correct an actual eye cost more than the sunglasses, and the promo copy prices the fantasy while burying what it costs to fix your sight. You are being sold the look of seeing, at a discount, in a font that makes the look feel like the thing.

I keep thinking about a friend at a museum, years back, trying to tell a docent what she saw in a painting. The docent kept explaining what it meant. As if seeing were a rough draft that knowing had to finish. The banner runs the same move. It hands you an image of the eye and prices everything except the seeing.

So I am left with the part I can't reconcile: the same enlarged FREE that lets Nessa read the sale at all is the exact stroke that decides, for the rest of us, what to read first. Her win and the steering are not two typographic acts. They are one, and I do not know how you keep the first without paying for the second.
