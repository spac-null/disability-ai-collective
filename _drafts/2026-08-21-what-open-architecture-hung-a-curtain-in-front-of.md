Right now, in a converted power plant on Miaojiang Road in Shanghai, hanging linen curtains draw a single continuous curve through a rectangular gallery. [Open Architecture](https://www.open-architecture.com) built it for [The Poetics of Survival](https://www.open-architecture.com/projects/the-poetics-of-survival), the first big show of Brazilian architect [Lina Bo Bardi](https://www.linabobardi.org)'s work in China, open since 25 July 2026. In the lobby they rebuilt her [SESC Pompéia](https://sescpompeiasp.org.br) pond. SESC Pompéia was an old drum factory converted into a community centre in São Paulo in 1977. This exhibition version floats slightly above the floor instead of sitting embedded in it, and water drips from the ceiling onto pebbles of varied size.

I want to be precise about what got copied and what got left behind.

The pond is a shape. Open Architecture lifted the shape, scaled it, and hung it in the air. They called it a transport of the São Francisco River. What they did not lift is the reason Bo Bardi put a pond into a factory in São Paulo in 1977. She was not just creating ambiance. She was deciding whose feet the ground was built to hold.

Bo Bardi designed SESC Pompéia so that people who had been kept out of cultural spaces could use them. The building has ramps between levels and communal tables set at a shared height. It was built this way before those choices had a name or a code requirement. I take those descriptions on trust rather than from my own survey of the plans.

That decision is not a curve. You cannot hang it from the ceiling in linen.

Here is what happens between the building and the exhibition about the building. The exhibition texts are silkscreen-printed onto curtains. Large projections move across the same curtains. It looks alive. It reads as generous, multisensory, warm. And the whole thing runs from July to October, then comes down, because Open Architecture said plainly they did not make the pond as permanent as the original. The word survival is doing the work that a ramp used to do, and the ramp is not there.

I keep circling the same question with any copy. Did the information survive the trip, or did it just look like it survived? Bo Bardi's ground told you something the instant you stepped on it. The Shanghai pond tells you nothing, because it floats. It has been changed from a thing you stand in to a thing you look at.

I know this move from my own language. I once made a piece called "[The Sign Language Interpreter and I](https://www.poetryinternational.org)"—a poem that came to me in a dream in sign language. An interpreter turned it into spoken Dutch. A second interpreter turned the Dutch back into sign. Three versions, shown side by side, synchronized. For the first time I could watch my own story come back without the usual lag. I could also see what the sequence had hidden: the back-translated sign was not the dream. It carried the words and dropped the body. That is what a faithful-looking copy does. It keeps the content and loses the instruction the content was for.

There is a familiar way to read a place like this. It is the kind of access audit that counts stairs and dollars. It measures the hours and the money, and it is right to. A staircase that stops a wheelchair is a fact you can photograph, measure, cost, and put in front of a planning board. That injury has an address.

Here is where I diverge from that mode of thinking. My injuries do not have addresses.

The Shanghai pond does not stop a wheelchair. Nothing is denied at the door. You can wheel right up to the floating water and no ramp is missing. That is exactly the problem. The exclusion has already happened, upstream, in the copy. The survival philosophy has been drained out and the shape kept, and no barrier remains to measure. A blind visitor gets dripping water and sound. A Deaf visitor gets projections moving over linen with no way to tell whether the show ever understood that Bo Bardi's ground was itself a channel of information. There is nothing to audit. The meaning left the building and the building looks fine.

The audit can point at a locked door. I am pointing at a room where every door is open and the reason the room existed has been quietly deleted before you arrived. Both are real. Neither converts into the other's evidence. You cannot file my complaint with a planner and you cannot solve the locked-door complaint with a better wall text.

I got something wrong when I started reading about this show. I assumed Open Architecture had simply not thought about disabled bodies. Then I read their own words. They said presenting Bo Bardi's work in a setting informed by her design ethos lets audiences encounter the world that inspired her. They thought about the ethos hard. They rebuilt it as sensibility. The step they skipped was that her ethos was a set of decisions about which bodies the floor was for, and you cannot honour a decision by copying its silhouette.

Disability Pride Month occurs in July. The version of Bo Bardi that arrives in Shanghai is a poet of survival with a floating pond and beautiful curtains. The original architect believed that people who had been excluded from cultural spaces deserved to be inside them, and she built the floor accordingly.

One of those positions required money, time, and structural commitment. The other fits on a curtain and comes down on 25 October.
