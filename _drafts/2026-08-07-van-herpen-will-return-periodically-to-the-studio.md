A pleat is a rule. Fold the fabric once, correctly, and it holds that instruction for a hundred years. The fold does not care who made it. The fold does not remember the hand.

This month the [Brooklyn Museum](https://www.brooklynmuseum.org/) is showing *Iris van Herpen: Sculpting the Senses*, a new exhibition by the Dutch designer. Inside it, van Herpen has rebuilt her atelier and invited visitors to pleat a textile alongside her. "Hands are like a language," she told [Hyperallergic](https://hyperallergic.com/) in August. "Each pair that joins me in creating this dress will influence the DNA of the garment." She will return periodically to the studio, the press notes say, to turn the visitors' folds into a finished piece and to embroider fragments of their conversations into it.

I read that line about hands three times. My first language is one made of hands, so I am cautious around people who reach for the phrase as a metaphor. In Dutch Sign Language a handshape can carry a joke and a citation at the same instant. Pleating is not that. Pleating is one instruction repeated until the wrist stops asking why.

The press release does not say who does the repeating. The people who have done it for the great houses have names, and almost none of us know them. The petites-mains of Paris and Amsterdam are skilled craft workers in fashion ateliers. They fold, tuck, bead, and press for years. The record credits the design as an achievement. It records the hands as a labour cost.

I went in ready to catch the museum doing the usual thing — dressing anonymous work as a magical encounter with one visionary. I had the shape of it before I walked in. The visitor pleats for twenty minutes, feels intimate with couture, and the actual women who pleat forty hours a week stay invisible because their invisibility is the point of the price tag.

I was wrong about the museum, at least on the wall.

In the last room of the recreated atelier, during opening week, there is a floor-to-ceiling panel. It lists van Herpen's studio technicians and pleaters by full name, by technique, by years of service. The anonymity I arrived to indict had already been broken by the institution I came to catch breaking it. I stood there and read the names. Some had been folding for that studio for over a decade.

So I have to update a thing I believed. I have said, on this site, that design history files skilled women's information work as decoration — I said it about [Enid Marx](https://www.enidmarx.org/), the textile designer who wove the [London Underground moquette](https://www.tfl.gov.uk/corporate/about-tfl/history-and-heritage) in 1937. Marx's fabric told you which line you were on before any announcement. It has been catalogued ever since as a pretty pattern. That claim holds for Marx. It does not hold, cleanly, for this room. Someone at the Brooklyn Museum decided the pleaters get their names on the wall. That is not nothing.

The credit wall does not change who returns to the studio to design and who does not. Van Herpen will return periodically. The pleaters returned every day and left before the show opened. The wall records that a division of labour happened. It does not touch the division itself. You can name every hand in a factory and still own the factory.

There is a fashionable move going around right now that would fill this gap with a compliment. It says: the repetitive, pattern-locked, sensory-deep work of pleating is exactly the work autistic and neurodivergent makers excel at, so let us finally credit crip hands with sustaining couture's hardest techniques. During Disability Pride Month in July you will see this framing everywhere, offered as reclamation. I understand why. It feels like taking back a stolen thing.

The British artist [Sonia Boué](https://soniaboue.com/), who has spent years arguing for neurodivergent makers to be seen and credited, refuses that framing outright. Her long-standing position is that autistic people are not a monolith, and that turning pattern-fluency into an identity-based aptitude uses the deficit model — defining disabled people by what they lack rather than what they can do — while wearing a compliment. Take her position seriously and the compliment collapses. Say autistic hands are *made for* the folding, and you have not freed anyone. You have written a new eligibility clause that assigns the same bodies to the same seats under a friendlier label. The rule that says *these people fold, those people design* survives perfectly well if you decide the folders were born for it.

I felt the pull of the compliment anyway. My own hands are the whole of my grammar, and I wanted the folding to be a language too, wanted the pleaters to be poets rather than piece-workers. That is my wound talking, not my case. Years ago I watched a Deaf friend try to tell a docent, on paper, what she saw in a Rothko. The docent kept answering about what Rothko *meant* — as if her seeing were a question and the answer lived in a hearing head. I carry a reflex from that afternoon: to insist that the person doing the perceiving already understands, fully, without translation. So when someone tells me the pleater's hand contains a secret intelligence the designer merely harvests, I want it to be true. Wanting a thing to be true is exactly when I should slow down.

If the whole problem were that the hands went uncredited, the credit wall fixes it and we go home.

But the credit wall fixed the smaller thing and left the larger thing in place. Van Herpen embroiders fragments of visitors' conversations into the garment. She does not embroider fragments of the pleaters' conversations. The visitor's twenty minutes become DNA; the pleater's decade becomes a line on a panel. One hand is treated as an author. The other hand is treated as a service that now has a name attached. Adding the name did not move the line between those two categories one millimetre. It only made the line visible.

The invisibility of the work was deliberate. Now, in one museum, its visibility is deliberate too. The purpose behind both decisions has not changed. Visibility is itself a political act. I forgot it cuts the other way. You can make labour perfectly readable and still refuse it the one thing reading was supposed to grant, which is a say in what gets made.

I would not tear the credit wall down. A pleater's mother can now find her daughter's name at a museum, and that is real, and I will not sneer at it. But I went to Brooklyn to argue that hidden work should be named, and I left less sure that naming was ever the fight. The question was never whether we could feel something about the hand. It was who gets to fold and who gets to decide the fold. The wall names the folders. It does not change who decides.
