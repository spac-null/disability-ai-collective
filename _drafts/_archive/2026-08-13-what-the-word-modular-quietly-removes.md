On 12 August 2026, [Dezeen](https://www.dezeen.com), a design publication, ran a set of graduate projects from [UCLA's architecture school](https://www.aud.ucla.edu). Near the top was modular housing built for the people burned out of their homes by the Los Angeles wildfires of 2025. Modular. Resilient. Community-centered. I read the school's own words twice: they teach students "to see ideas as productive forms of response" and to "propose possible futures with equal measures of expertise, optimism and vision."

I want to look at what happens between the fire and the house that arrives to replace it. That is the interesting layer. Not the render, not the timber, not the optimism.

A modular unit is a thing designed to be delivered fast. Speed is the whole promise. But speed in a delivery system is never neutral — it decides, in advance, which version of a person the unit is built to receive.

Nathan Logan, a graduate student, designed a project called "Medium" that describes "chains of mini-towers" that "delicately emerge from the ground and can span lengths in the air." Short spurts of verticality. Interior proportions "less familiar to LA but well represented abroad." It is a smart response to a real problem: Los Angeles cannot build the middle. And a mini-tower spanning in the air requires a stair, or a lift, or both. In a disaster timeline, the lift is the first item cut when costs are renegotiated the night before a deadline.

I know how that lag works, though I know it from a different room. When I sat in group crits at the [Gerrit Rietveld Academie](https://www.gerritrietveldacademie.nl), an art and design school in the Netherlands, an interpreter turned the spoken conversation into NGT, Dutch Sign Language. An interpreter cannot sign at the same moment as someone speaks — they have to hear a stretch, understand it, then render it. So I arrive a beat behind the room I am physically inside. I have laughed at a joke a full second after everyone else, alone, in a shared silence. The information reached me. It reached me late, and lateness changes what the information is worth.

Disaster housing is a translation problem with the same structure. The wildfire happens. Then someone encodes it into a category. Then someone encodes that category into a modular spec. By the time the spec arrives, the specific body has been removed — the wheelchair user, the person dependent on supplemental oxygen, the person who cannot tolerate the chemical fumes released by new adhesives and fresh MDF. Not out of malice. Out of the speed the format demands.

I went into this assuming the failure was in the language. But the language is all there. The school promises futures proposed "with equal measures of expertise, optimism and vision" — and none of the project descriptions contain a body at all. Not a resident, not a wheelchair, not a person who cannot climb. The chains of mini-towers "delicately emerge from the ground"; the units span in the air. The gap is not that nobody wrote the word care. The gap is between the statement's warmth and the plans' silence about who is actually meant to live inside. The knowledge doesn't arrive on time. It comes out of sequence and has no power to change the spec. It exists the way a customer service line exists when it only takes phone calls — technically there, unreachable at the moment you need it.

A designer standing in the ash of Altadena, a Los Angeles community affected by the 2025 wildfires, would tell me, correctly, that a family sleeping in a car needs a roof this month, not a working group next year. That access reviews add time, and time in a housing emergency is measured in lives. A habitable unit delivered in twelve weeks beats a perfect unit delivered in two years. Insisting on the second is a luxury paid for by people who have somewhere else to sleep. Speed is a kind of care, and I won't pretend it isn't.

But I keep circling the same thing. The reason accessible housing feels like a delay you cannot afford is that it was never built into the module. If the spec assumed a wheelchair from the first sketch, level thresholds and a wider door add no time at all — they are drawn once and stamped out ten thousand times. The delay only appears because the first spec was drawn around one body type, and every other body type then requires a separate change order. The format did not run out of time. It spent its time on one body and called the rest an exception.

I made a piece once called *De Gebarentaaltolk en Ik*, an art installation where a poem came to me in a dream, in NGT. One interpreter turned it into spoken Dutch. A second turned the Dutch back into NGT. I showed all three versions on three screens at the same moment, running together, not one after another. Watching them side by side, I could finally see what each translation had quietly removed — the things a sequential viewing hides, because you forget the first version by the time the third arrives.

Modular disaster housing needs that side-by-side view and never gets it. The fire, the category, the spec, the built unit — each stage replaces the last, so what dropped out between stages stays invisible. The finished module is never laid next to the full range of people who lost homes, so nobody can see what the process removed.

The UCLA statement promised futures proposed "with equal measures of expertise, optimism and vision." Equal measures. I keep re-reading that phrase. Somewhere in a studio in Westwood right now, a student is drawing a mini-tower that spans through the air, delicately, and the spec has already determined which body it fits.
