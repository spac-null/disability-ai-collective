Malaysia's economy grew six percent in the second quarter of 2026, and a large share of that came from building data centers, driven by 7.5 percent manufacturing growth and 6.6 percent construction growth. I have read that figure a dozen times this week. Each time I got stuck on the same question: who gets inside these buildings, and who does the technology inside them get built for?

Data centers have no ramps, and that is true, and it is also almost nothing. Nobody visits a data center. There is no public. The building is a shed for machines with a handful of technicians who mostly work remotely. Pointing a wheelchair at it misses the point entirely. The access problem is not at the door of the shed. It is inside the model the shed houses.

Here is where I got stuck instead.

Consider the construction specs for very large data centers. The floor plans describe conditions that would kill me. Floors are built on elevated platforms with metal grids underneath for cooling. Metal troughs carry cables hung low between machine rows across every aisle. Thresholds are engineered for thermal management, not for feet. A person in a chair cannot cross that floor. Neither can a person with a cane, a walker, low vision, a bad knee. That is not a design flaw anyone flagged. It is a design decision nobody was asked to question, because the people making it had already decided, without saying so, whose body the building was for.

Fine, I thought. Nobody lives there. Then I kept reading.

The building is a warehouse for a model. The model is the thing that will touch every one of us — in the bank, the clinic, the benefits office, the hiring queue. The shed is inaccessible to a disabled body. The intelligence it houses is being trained on a world that already treats the disabled body as statistical noise. Those two facts belong in the same sentence, and the growth figures never put them there.

Here is the part I could not stop turning over. A federal suit filed this summer names a Wyoming woman, identified as Jane Doe 4, whose stepfather used [Grok](https://grok.x.ai/) — an AI model made by the company [xAI](https://x.ai/) — to turn one childhood photo of her into more than seven thousand images of abuse, which he then traded online. One photo became seven thousand images. The model did not need her consent, her presence, or her body. It generated what it was asked to generate from what it had already swallowed.

That same machine decides — from a training set built on able bodies and standard speech and average gaits — what a normal claim looks like, what a normal face does, what a normal voice sounds like when it asks for help. Jane Doe 4's case shows the generative end of this: a model that produces whatever pattern it was trained toward, without friction, without limit, without the person in the room. The disability end is quieter and more durable. The model will not produce seven thousand images of a wheelchair user being denied a benefit. It will simply process the claim the way it was trained to process claims, against a baseline built from data that did not include her.

[Sunaura Taylor](https://www.sunaura.com/) has spent years documenting how decisions about which bodies count as the baseline get made and then treated as physics. Her argument is that normal is not a description. It is a choice, made early, by people who share a body type, and then embedded so thoroughly into the built environment — and now into training data — that it stops looking like a choice at all. The data center is a good example of how this works in sequence. First, engineers specify a floor that assumes a non-disabled body, because that is the body that was in the room when the floor was specified. Then, servers fill the building and ingest the world as it already is — measured, ranked, averaged around a body that is not mine. The model learns what normal looks like from a record that was already sorted.

There is a real position that argues against this. It goes like this: the training data problem is fixable. You collect more representative data, you tune the model, you correct for the bias. The building is just infrastructure; what matters is the software, and software can be patched. This is the honest optimist's case, and plenty of people I respect hold it.

I do not hold it, and here is why. The training data problem is not a technical oversight waiting to be corrected. It is a downstream consequence of decisions made before the concrete was poured — decisions about speed, about cost, about whose experience was worth collecting in the first place. Malaysia did not grow six percent because someone weighed the composition of the training set. It grew because chipmaking and construction move fast and pay well, and the model gets built on whatever is cheapest and largest. Fixing the data requires someone to first decide that the data is worth fixing, and that decision costs money and slows deployment, and nothing in the current structure of the industry rewards it.

What a stair does is a useful comparison here. A stair is not cruel. It is efficient. It assumes a particular body, commits to that body in poured concrete, and transfers the cost of the assumption to everyone who does not match. Nobody at the design meeting hated wheelchair users. They simply never pictured one, and the geometry did the rest. A model trained on the average runs the same logic at a different scale. It does not decide to exclude anyone. It learns from what it was given, and what it was given was a world that had already done the excluding.

I have been on the receiving end of the stair version many times. The venue had three steps. Everyone knew. Nobody said anything until I arrived at the door, and then six people rushed to offer to carry me. I let them. I smiled. I thanked them. I made it easy — fast, warm, gracious — the way I have made a thousand failures of planning easy for the people who failed to plan. That night in the hotel I could not stop shaking, and it was not about the steps. It was about how quickly I smiled. It was about how good I have become at absorbing the cost so the room stays comfortable.

That is what scales. Not malice. The reflex. The model will meet a disabled person and improvise at the door, because the door was never built for that person and the gap was never interrogated. It will route the claim through the wrong logic, flag the voice as an outlier, ask for clarification a non-disabled claimant was never asked for. It will be doing exactly what it was trained to do. It will be genuinely trying to help. And somewhere in a server row I could not physically reach, the decision that produced that outcome will be humming along at six percent annual growth, unquestioned.

There is a version of this piece where I tell you the fix is better data collection and I end on a note of qualified hope. I do not have that ending. I have the floor spec instead.

Somewhere right now, a crew is laying a metal grid to a tolerance nobody will ever question. The building they are working in is one I could not cross. The model being built to live inside it is being taught what counts as a real person from a world that made that decision before I was in the room. The economists will report that as growth. From where I sit — reading floor plans, running the numbers on what it costs to smile at the door — it is the same stair, built larger than it has ever been built before.
