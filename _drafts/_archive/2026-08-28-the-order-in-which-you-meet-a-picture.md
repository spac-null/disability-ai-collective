Zoja Čepin's multisensory exhibition system is a way of showing an artwork through touch and sound. It pairs layered tactile booklets with audio description, and it was developed through interviews and testing with blind and partially sighted people. It is built to slot into existing displays without changing them: simple to produce, affordable, and, the project states, easy to adapt for permanent or temporary exhibitions. A visitor can take the booklet home. That last detail matters, but the booklet is where the interesting work happens.

Here is how one booklet works. It translates an artwork into three progressively detailed tactile layers, allowing visitors to move from composition to key features at their own pace. So the encounter has a shape. You meet the whole arrangement first. Then you move inward toward the parts. And the speed of that movement is yours.

Notice what this is not. It is not a straight swap of touch for sight, one sense standing in for another as a whole-for-whole substitute. The booklet does not hand you the picture all at once in a different medium. It hands you the picture in an order. Composition first, then detail. The three layers make that order into a thing you can feel: a sequence the object holds and releases in steps, rather than an impression delivered in a single instant.

That is the part worth stopping on. The booklet makes the reading order of the artwork explicit. It separates the overall shape of the image from its features and puts them in distinct layers, and by doing so it names a stage that usually goes unnamed. Whoever builds the layers decides what is understood first. The choice to lead with composition is a choice, and here it is visible, because it has been given its own layer before detail arrives.

Set that against an ordinary framed picture on a wall. There, composition and detail reach the eye together. The sequence, if there is one, is collapsed into a single glance, and it is not chosen — it just happens, all at once, unmarked. The booklet does the opposite. It pulls the stages apart and lets you walk through them one at a time. What the wall folds into one moment, the booklet lays out as steps you take.

I should be plain about what this is and is not. This is a reading of how the design behaves, not a documented fact about the encounter. It is also not a claim about how eyes or minds work. Whether sighted looking actually runs composition-then-detail is not something the design proves, and I am not asserting it; the point is only about what the booklet isolates. The source names three progressive layers and self-paced movement, and it names interviews and testing with blind and partially sighted people. It does not say which artworks were used, or how an image was divided into layers, or what the testers reported the sequencing did for them, or whether the tactile layers or the audio carry the structure. Those are real gaps, and I am not filling them.

Within those limits, though, one thing holds. The booklet takes the order in which you meet an image and turns it into something you can see being built and can move through at your chosen speed. It treats the sequence of encounter as a variable, not a given. That reframes what the design is for. The obvious description — access as a supplement, a version of the artwork added on for people who cannot see it — describes the reach of the system but not its most particular move.

By splitting an artwork into ordered layers and handing over the pacing, the system turns the reading order of an image, normally collapsed into a single unchosen glance, into something explicit and controllable.
