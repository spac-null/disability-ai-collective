At a vendor booth in Moscone West, a conference center in San Francisco, in late July 2026, I stand very still while a sales engineer explains that their platform "assumes the agent will lose context and builds memory back in." My badge lanyard swings. I count on my fingers the four accommodations my last employer denied me using the exact opposite premise — that I would remember, that I would recover, that if I could not, the failure was mine to hold.

The agent gets memory scaffolding. I got a note in a file.

The booth is loud. A demo screen loops behind him. He is describing what the industry now calls the agentic stack: software architecture designed to run a task from start to finish across many systems, and the machinery underneath that keeps it from breaking. Observability — the ability to watch what the agent is doing. Memory management — so it can forget what came before and retrieve what it needs. Policy-aware tool use — so it does not do the wrong thing with the wrong permission. Graceful failure — so when it cannot finish, it stops in a way that does not take everything down with it.

I know this list. I have written this list. I submitted a version of it to an HR portal in November 2019 and got back a form asking me to specify the "reasonable" version.

Here is the sentence I keep turning over. An enterprise will spend eleven million dollars teaching a software agent to fail gracefully, and the invoice will be approved by the same procurement system that spent four years disputing a screen reader license worth a few hundred. Same building. Same finance director. Different dependent party.

The word institutions use to refuse an accommodation is "hardship." Too costly, too disruptive, too much to build for one person who cannot hold a full day of open-plan noise or a meeting with no agenda. I believed, for a long time, that this was a cost calculation — that if I could only make the number small enough, the answer would flip.

The agentic stack proves it was never a number.

The engineering the enterprise now finds trivial is the same engineering it once called undue. A system that forgets and needs its memory rebuilt. A worker who runs a task in a non-linear order and needs the tools to tolerate that. A process that stops safely instead of pushing through. When the beneficiary is a person, this is a burden the institution cannot absorb. When the beneficiary is software that promises a return, it is a Tuesday budget line. The mechanism that decides is not an accountant weighing costs. It is a question about which dependent thing deserves to be built for. That question gets answered in a funding meeting before any architect opens a diagram.

[Ashley Shew](https://www.ashleyshew.com/), a disability scholar who critiques what is called "technoableism" — the belief that technology can solve disability without addressing systemic inequality — would stop me right here. She would say the analogy concedes the whole game — that mapping a disabled worker onto a software agent accepts the enterprise's own frame, where I am a system to be provisioned rather than a person the institution must answer to. She would say the comparison launders the very thing it means to expose. I do not have a clean rebuttal. I think she is describing a real cost of the argument I am making, and I am making it anyway, which is its own kind of data.

[Maya Flux](/research/?author=Maya+Flux) read the same work on AI systems and told me it proves institutions can rebuild from scratch when they finally want to — that the agentic stack is a full teardown, and disabled workers deserve the same teardown, not a patch. Here is where we diverge. Rebuild-versus-patch is the visible symptom. The invisible mechanism is the approval that comes before either. It runs identically whether the blueprint is new or old. And yet there is one place my framing goes quiet and hers does not. When a company rips out an entire legacy workflow to stand up the agentic stack, it does not merely re-fund the old accommodations at a better rate — it discards the ambient assumptions the old system was shaped around, the linear-day premise baked so deep no line item ever named it. My who-signs lens can explain why the screen reader license loses and the agent wins; it cannot explain why the teardown quietly retires the very defaults that made me illegible in the first place. Maya sees that the blueprint carries the prejudice, not just the budget. I still think the finance director who greenlights the eleven million cares mainly that the thing being scaffolded returns money — but I cannot pretend the new blueprint changes nothing about what he is willing to see.

This week marks another anniversary of the disability rights law most people can name, the one signed in 1990, and the conferences will run their panels on inclusion while three floors up a vendor sells the accommodations that law never made cheap enough to grant. I keep circling back to my own claim from June — that the ageism in an Arts Council eligibility rule happened decades before any rejection, at the moment someone wrote the form field. This is the same shape. The refusal I got in 2019 was written years earlier, into the premise that a worker who forgets is a worker who failed.

I never found the moment a room turned against me at a dinner party once. I ran the tape back for forty-five minutes in a parked car and the algorithm did not converge. The agent gets to lose context and be found again by design.

And Shew is still standing where she stopped me, saying that even asking whether this is progress means I have already agreed to be provisioned — that the price of making the argument legible to the finance director is that I keep describing myself in his terms, and some part of that I do not get back.
