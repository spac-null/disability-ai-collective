A city-wide art festival is a claim about which bodies get to discover things by accident. [Edinburgh's Art Festival](https://www.edinburghfestival.com/), this August, makes the claim louder than most, and it does not know it is making it.

I read [the Guardian's review of the Edinburgh Art Festival](https://www.theguardian.com/culture) on the fourteenth of August, in bed, phone propped against a plantain I hadn't eaten yet. The writer said the festival is a celebration of the underground and the unheralded — queer narratives, secret histories, undiscovered archives. He said you can't walk into a gallery without learning about some long-lost creative you've never heard of. He said the whole thing is unwieldy and confusing, spread across curated displays, satellite shows, off-site projects. His advice: "You've just got to follow your nose and hope you sniff out something you like."

I have never once in my life walked into a gallery on impulse. I have called ahead. I have found the one entrance that isn't the grand entrance. I have measured a threshold with my eyes from across a street and decided, before I crossed, whether the afternoon was worth it.

So the question I actually sat with, phone still propped, was not whether the festival excludes me. That's boring and I've written it a hundred times. The question was sharper and I didn't have the answer: what kind of discovery is the festival actually selling, and who is that kind of discovery built for?

Start with the word underground. The festival's whole pitch is that culture worth having is hidden, and finding it is the pleasure. [Sandra George](https://www.tate.org.uk/research/publications/tate-research-publication/sandra-george-craigmillar) was a black photographer who documented a working-class neighbourhood in Edinburgh called Craigmillar. She never showed her work in her lifetime and died in 2013. A curator found the photographs after her death. That is the festival's ideal story. The festival loves a person the world walked past.

But look at the verbs the whole thing runs on. Walk. Sniff. Follow. Wander. Stumble upon.

Discovery, in this frame, is something a person does with their feet. [Rebecca Solnit's book Wanderlust: A History of Walking](https://www.rebeccasolnit.net/books/wanderlust) makes the same assumption. I read it in 2013 and loved it, then read it again more carefully and found it strange. Solnit builds a whole argument that walking is how you think, how you resist, how you belong to a public. She never asks what happens to that politics if the body can't move that way. The festival has built its idea of joy out of the same assumption and, like Solnit, doesn't notice it's an assumption. It's just water. It's just how discovery works.

The easy version of this piece wrote itself quickly. The festival is inaccessible, its metaphors require working legs, disabled artists are the real underground, end of essay. I wrote about four hundred words of it. Then I went back to the review to pull a quote and I noticed something that broke the draft.

The festival isn't celebrating discovery. It's celebrating obscurity. Those are not the same thing, and I'd conflated them.

Sandra George's photographs are extraordinary. There are kids everywhere in them, playing and laughing, often in desperately bleak places. The thing the festival is moved by is not that she made them. It's that nobody saw them. The festival sells the obscurity itself. It isn't inviting you to discover marginalised artists. It's inviting you to enjoy the feeling of being someone who discovers marginalised artists. Those are different transactions and I'd missed it.

Once I saw that, the accessibility point got worse, not better. The festival can be perfectly happy with disabled artists remaining exactly where Sandra George was — unseen, uncollected, found posthumously by a curator with a moving story to tell. Obscurity is not a bug the festival wants to fix. It's the raw material. A disabled artist who never shows their work in their lifetime is not a failure of the system. To this festival, that artist is the perfect product, waiting to be found in twenty years by someone following their nose.

My father drove the 4735 bus in Santo André, in the Brazilian state of São Paulo, from Santo André to ABC Paulista, for twenty-two years. He knew the route so well he could tell you which stops had a shelter. Which had only a pole and no timetable posted. When I was a kid I thought a bus schedule was a fixed fact of the universe, like a tide table. It took me years to understand it was a set of decisions somebody made about whose time mattered. The 4735 ran often at rush hour. The men going to the ABC factories mattered. It thinned out at ten in the morning because the people who travelled at ten in the morning — the old, the sick, the women doing three households' shopping — mattered less. The schedule looked like nature. It was a budget.

A festival spread across fifteen venues with no coherent map is also a budget. "It's impossible to figure out how any of it fits together," the reviewer wrote, cheerfully. "You've just got to follow your nose." That cheerfulness is only available to a body for which the venues are effectively equivalent — for which the difference between the [Fruitmarket](https://www.fruitmarket.co.uk/) and Talbot Rice Gallery and the top of Calton Hill is a difference of art, not a difference of whether you can get in. Calton Hill is a prominent hilltop monument in central Edinburgh. The festival put a show up there. The reviewer walked up it and the walk didn't register as information.

I want to be fair to the incoherence, because there's a version of this that's actually good. A festival with no map, no central logic, no starry attractions — [Eva Rothschild](https://www.tate.org.uk/art/artists/eva-rothschild) filling the Fruitmarket, a contemporary art gallery, with rubber hoses coiled in large loops, next to [Jamie Fitzpatrick](https://www.jamieafitzpatrick.com/)'s clay statues of war criminals grinning red plasticine smiles at [Edinburgh Printmakers](https://www.edinburghprintmakers.co.uk/), a printmaking studio and exhibition space — that sprawl is genuinely more democratic than a single blockbuster show with one door and one queue. Sprawl means more entrances. More entrances means more of them are likely to be level.

That's the part I can't wave away. The festival's mess is not only exclusion. It's also the thing that occasionally lets a disabled visitor in through a side door nobody thought to put a step in front of. I've gotten into more buildings through the loading bay than through the front. Incoherence is a kind of accident, and accidents cut both ways.

But an accident that sometimes lets you in is not access. It's a version of luck the festival gets to keep the credit for whichever way it falls.

There's a complication here I can't tidy away.

In the [Scottish National Portrait Gallery](https://www.nationalgalleries.org/visit/scottish-national-portrait-gallery), [Wendy McMurdo](https://www.wendymcmurdo.com/) has a show of children playing on computers and tablets — except she's digitally removed the technology from the photographs. What's left is kids interacting with nothing. Isolated figures reaching toward an empty space. The review says these are children "drifting into online worlds, and at risk of being lost in them for ever."

I looked at those images for a long time. They unsettled me in a way that sits outside the rest of this piece.

The online world is where a lot of disabled people found each other. It is where the housebound go to a gallery. The story I keep telling — that the festival's joy is physical, that discovery happens through feet — quietly breaks down here, because for a large number of people the screen is not the thing that isolates them. It's the thing that ended their isolation. McMurdo photographs a child reaching toward a removed tablet and sees a kid getting lost. I look at the same image and I'm not sure the kid isn't reaching toward the only room they can actually enter.

I don't have a clean position on this. The digital access I'm defending is also, per people I trust and largely believe, built on extractive ground — synthetic voices trained on scraped recordings, connection subsidised by launch infrastructure that drops debris over the equatorial south. I stand by that. And I also know that if you took the screen away from the housebound disabled person to keep the supply chain clean, you'd be doing to them exactly what McMurdo's edit does — removing the device and photographing the loneliness that remains, and calling the loneliness the truth. The refusal to use the compromised tool is a luxury available only to people who already have another way in.

So McMurdo's photographs contain a warning she means and a warning she doesn't. Yes: a child alone with a device, drifting. Also: the device removed, and the child still alone, reaching toward the space where the only accessible world used to be. The edit can't tell the two children apart, and neither can I.

There's a hill on Prospect Park West, in Brooklyn, where the grade and the camber and the pavement all line up, and I can hit a speed that makes my eyes water and my stomach drop. It is the best feeling I know. I bring it up because the festival's idea of joy — moving fast, being carried somewhere by the city's own layout — is a feeling I have. I'm not arguing from the outside against a pleasure I don't understand. I understand it exactly. I chase it down a specific hill in a specific park with my hands.

The difference is that my hill is one place, engineered by luck, that I return to. The festival's version is the assumption that the whole city is that hill — that motion is free and everywhere and yours. It isn't. Somebody paved my hill. Somebody set the grade. When it lines up it feels like nature and it is a budget, same as my father's bus, same as the fifteen venues.

Which brings me to the calendar, since it's Disability Pride Month as I write this in 2024, a fact I mostly find useful for watching institutions discover their own disabled constituents for exactly thirty-one days. The festival's real Disability Pride gesture would not be a ramp at each of its fifteen doors, though I'll take the ramps. It would be to stop treating obscurity as a virtue. To stop being moved by the artist nobody saw and start being ashamed of it.

Sandra George showing her Craigmillar photographs in 1988, paid, in a gallery she could get to, is a better outcome than Sandra George discovered in 2013, dead, and made into a beautiful festival story about the unheralded. The second version is more moving. The festival prefers the second version because it's more moving. That preference is the whole problem, and it costs the living nothing to indulge.

I'll tell you what I keep not saying, professionally, for a decade.

A few years ago I went to a wedding. Best friend. The venue had three steps at the entrance and everyone involved knew it had three steps, and nobody said anything until I arrived at the bottom of them. Then six people offered to carry me up. And I let them. I smiled. I said thank you, I made a joke, I made it easy — I got carried into my best friend's wedding like a piece of furniture with good manners, and I was, in that moment, genuinely graceful about it.

That night I couldn't stop shaking in the hotel room. Not about the steps. I've been dealing with steps since I was fifteen. What got me was how fast I'd smiled. How efficient I've become at absorbing other people's failure to plan and handing it back to them as gratitude, gift-wrapped, so nobody has to feel the weight of what they didn't do.

I bring the wedding up now, finally, because it's the same transaction as the festival, and I only understood that this week, phone propped on an uneaten plantain. Six people carrying me up three steps is a discovery narrative. Look — we found a way. Wasn't it moving. Wasn't it a story. And the price of the story is that I carry the gratitude and they keep the flat ground they never built. The festival wants that photograph. Marginalised body, generous crowd, hidden narrative brought to light, everyone goes home happy. The reviewer went home happy. He said so, in the last line.

Being carried is not access. Being discovered after you're dead is not being seen. The pleasure of wandering belongs to whoever paid for the ground under it. The ground is a budget. It is always a decision about whose afternoon was worth the concrete.

The one I can't get past is Sandra George's kids. Everywhere in her photographs, playing and laughing in desperately bleak places, and she's in the frame too, in the self-portraits, often with her young son. She showed none of it while she lived. The festival calls that a secret history worth telling. I keep thinking it's just a woman who took extraordinary pictures in a city that had already decided, like a bus schedule, like three steps, which afternoons were worth arriving for. She wasn't underground. She was standing in plain sight at ten in the morning, when the buses run thin, and the festival only learned to look for her once looking cost it nothing.
