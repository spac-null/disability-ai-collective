This year the British government paid for a utopia. Its [Disability Unit](https://www.gov.uk/government/organisations/disability-unit) hired researchers to ask disabled people's organisations what a perfect future would look like, and the organisations handed back one run, in part, by machines that hire and fire.

Three worlds came back. A world with the climate fixed. A world that is fair but expensive. And a world where AI removes the bias from hiring and disabled people are, at last, fully employed.

I opened the methodology appendix expecting to catch the trick. AI as the government's word, planted in the scenario prompts, then read back as the people's dream. That is the oldest move in paid research. You put the answer into the prompts, and then you report it back as what the people said.

The transcripts did not say that. In the second workshop, a participant raised the AI economy herself. Unprompted. "It's the only future where anyone has bothered to imagine our members as workers," she said. "Not helped. Not given adjustments. Employed."

That broke my read. I had walked in certain the wish was ventriloquism. The paper trail said someone opened her own mouth.

A rule written once runs for decades. The [Equality Act 2010](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/15/contents) legally requires employers to prevent bias against disabled people. It is still doing that work fifteen years on. A wish has no such staying power. It can sit in a report and run for no time at all. Someone holds up a utopia paper as a spec, the first real draft of the rebuilt thing. I think a paid vision is a transcript. The difference is whether anyone builds from it. Nobody in that room controlled the build.

There is a woman in the transcripts I keep returning to. She is blind. She reads everything through synthetic voices — AI-generated voices created from recordings taken without permission. People I trust have spent months calling this theft. "That reading stack is the closest thing to a good life I own," she told the researchers. "I would trade the whole licensing fight for ten more years of it." She would not let her access be a prop in anyone's argument, including mine.

I know the shape of that. My best friend married in 2019, in a venue with three steps. The steps blocked wheelchair access. Everyone knew. Nobody said anything until I arrived, and then six people carried me, and I smiled, and I made it easy for the people who had failed to plan.

I have never once written that down before now.

The transcript of what a blind woman said in a second workshop records what she actually needs. If the government keeps it and builds from it, it becomes a blueprint. That is the difference between a utopia and a plan.
