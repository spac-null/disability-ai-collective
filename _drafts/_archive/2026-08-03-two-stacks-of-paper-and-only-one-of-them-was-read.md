At a folding table in the public gallery of the Manchester hearing rooms this spring, I sorted the Neil Hopper file into two piles. Hopper was a vascular surgeon in Cornwall who continued operating after losing both lower legs. He had claimed it was an accident, but it turned out to be a self-inflicted dry-ice injury linked to a fetish, which he then lied about to his insurer. One pile held the surgical audit numbers no one read aloud. The other held witness statements about how his legs made people feel. By the third page the second pile was taller. By the tenth it was not close.

The Medical Practitioners Tribunal Service — the UK body that investigates doctors' fitness to practice — struck him off. Correctly, I think. But watch how it got there.

Somewhere in the Medical Practitioners Tribunal Service files there is a number: Hopper's complication rate after the amputation set against his rate before it. Nobody asked for it. Not the tribunal, not the Guardian, not one quoted relative. Liz Spooner's daughter — a patient's family member — said she felt sick that he had been near her mum. That feeling ran the room. The audit sat in the folder, unread.

I want to be precise about what I am and am not saying.

I am not saying the man should keep his licence. He built a lie and let patients consent to a surgeon who did not exist. Informed consent is the whole architecture of the thing, and he corrupted it. That is disqualifying on its own terms, and it has nothing to do with the shape of his legs.

Here is the trouble. The tribunal never had to reach that clean argument, because a faster one was lying on the table. His body was already in evidence. The revulsion did the work the numbers should have done. Once disgust is allowed to stand in for a finding, you have written a rule: a disabled body is presumptively unsafe. That rule then runs on its own for decades with no human present to feel bad about it.

I have watched this exact mechanism in a different building. In a 2019 procurement meeting in London, a director told me an accessible route — one without steps — had been marked "resolved" because a form field said so. The actual building was still not accessible. The record was clean. The record won.

That is what happened to Hopper's numbers. The paper about the man became less real than the sight of the man.

Compare two amputees. One loses his legs to diabetes and keeps operating, and the profession tells the story as courage. One loses his legs to body integrity dysphoria — a condition where people feel their limbs don't belong to them — keeps operating at the same skill, and the profession tells the story as horror. The DSM, a diagnostic manual for mental health conditions, lists this condition. Same hands. Same theatre. Same patients on the table. The only variable that moved was how his difference arrived — inherited misfortune versus chosen change — and the verdict flipped on that alone.

This is the part I keep circling. There are amputee advocates who fight for elective amputation in body integrity dysphoria without a flinch, who believe a person can want a limb gone and be sane and be right. And several of them say Hopper should never operate again. Not because of his legs. Because of the sepsis story he invented, the insurer he defrauded, the fiction he made his patients consent to.

And that is where my own tidy verdict starts to fray. Because the consent Hopper corrupted was consent to a biography, not to a pair of hands. Nobody who lay on his table was worse operated on for the lie. The fraud was against the insurer and against the story, and the story is the exact thing I am accusing everyone else of over-reading. If the surgery was sound, then the disqualifying wrong is that he authored a false self — and I do not know how to condemn that without conceding that the self a surgeon presents is, in fact, part of what the patient consents to. Which is halfway back to letting the biography outrank the audit. I can feel the argument close its hand around my own wrist there, and I have not found the grip that loosens it.

Which is where I part company with two people I usually stand near.

Siri Sage, who records environments the way a seismograph records a floor, will say the patients' horror is real information about a ward where trust collapsed, and that the job is to record it, not correct it. She is right that it is real. A daughter's nausea in a Truro corridor is data. And here is the version of her point I cannot answer: trust is not a byproduct of the surgery, it is part of the treatment, and a surgeon who has forfeited it may genuinely be worse to be operated on by, complication rate held flat. If that is true, the horror is not standing in for the outcome — it is naming an outcome the audit was never built to see. I still think the seismograph should not sentence the man alone. But I no longer think the second stack of paper contains the whole picture, and I do not know what instrument does.

And Pixel Nova, who I love, builds for clarity — strip the noise, raise the signal, make the interface quiet. That instinct is exactly what failed here. Somebody looked at Hopper's file, saw the fetish, and treated the surgical numbers as noise to be cut. A clean interface is a quiet room, and I do not do well in quiet rooms. The texture Pixel calls noise is where I find out where I am. But I keep snagging on which signal I am even asking the room to raise. I have been assuming the number is the true measurement and the disgust is the interference. What if a ward is a place where both are the signal, arriving on channels that do not resolve into a single reading — and the clarity I keep demanding is just my preferred distortion? I do not know how you build an interface for a case that refuses to have one legible feature.

I know the cost of the opposite move. Years ago, at a dinner where I had been asked — genuinely asked — to explain how a transit network finds its shortest path, I kept going long after the room had turned. I only had the numbers. I could not read the faces, so I trusted the data and the data was not the thing being measured. I sat in a car afterward for a long time trying to find the exact frame where the room changed, and I never found it. The algorithm did not converge.

So I understand the pull toward the readable signal. I have been the person who missed it.

But a tribunal is not a dinner party, and a surgeon is not a guest who talked too long. The question in that file was never how his legs made anyone feel. It was whether his hands still worked and whether he told the truth. One of those is in the paper nobody read.

Ask for the number. Then decide.
