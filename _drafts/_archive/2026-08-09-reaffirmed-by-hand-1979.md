A catalogue entry is the longest-running piece of software a museum owns. It is written once, executed daily, and maintained by no one. This week the [National Museum of Norway](https://www.nasjonalmuseet.no/) in Oslo announced it is hanging a still life under the name [Clara Peeters](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Q459148), a Flemish painter active around 1610 known for her technical skill in still-life work. Art historians have long found her paintings misattributed to male artists, which makes her a clear case of institutional bias. For roughly a century the same object sat in the collection under a different label. The label was not her name. It was a guess: "circle of Brueghel"—meaning followers or workshop members rather than the master himself—or something near it. Anonymous.

[Cynthia Osiecki](https://www.cynthiaosiecki.com/), an art historian studying Peeters' works and attributions, found the painting in storage in 2022 and recognised the hand. "I pulled out the rack and I knew the hand at once," she said afterwards, "and then I spent an hour telling myself I was wrong." The museum is calling this a rediscovery. I want to be careful with that word. I have spent the last three months arguing that the cruelest rules in a cultural institution are the ones nobody is present to enforce. A person can be denied access to funding or a program and never learn who refused them—the rejection lands with no rejecter behind it. A form field returns a birth year, and a decision algorithm discards an applicant before any human has looked. I wrote about a residency in Wyoming with a screening clause drafted before its current staff arrived, still executing refusals with nobody present to mean them; dozens of artists were rejected by criteria their administrators no longer remembered applying. I wrote about a medical-adviser post at a welfare department with a three-year clock built into it—a fixed term limit that ended the position when the original author left, leaving the role to keep making decisions under outdated instructions. The shape was the same each time: a rule left in the water, running long after its author left the building.

The Peeters entry looked like another instance. It was written once, executed daily, maintained by no one.

Then I read the accession records.

---

I had assumed the misattribution was neglect. The story tells itself that way: a woman's work slides under a famous man's name because no one is watching the label, and the label just keeps running. It is the automation reading. It fit my last five articles. It was wrong.

In the digitised records I found that the painting had been physically examined during a collection review decades before Osiecki walked into that storage room. Conservators had it out. They looked at it. They reaffirmed the anonymous, near-Brueghel attribution by hand. Someone in a lab coat, with the object in front of them, decided this was not a signed thing, this was a circle-of, and shelved her again.

That breaks the premise. There was no rule running with no human present. Humans were present. They looked, and they re-shelved her anyway.

This is worse than automation, and I did not expect to write that sentence. An unattended rule is at least honest about being unattended. What happened in that lab was a person applying full attention and producing the wrong answer, then signing off on the wrong answer with the authority of having looked. The examination did not correct the error. It laundered it. Now the file could say the attribution had been reviewed and confirmed, which is the most trusted phrase in any records system, and here it was pointed at a mistake.

I keep meeting this failure mode. Last month I wrote about a British high court clearing a puberty-blocker trial on forty-one expert opinions and zero people who had actually taken the drugs. The court's evidence rules counted a paper about a body as evidence and the body itself as anecdote. The Oslo conservator and the London court are running the same procedure. Attention arrives and gets spent, and the thing attention was pointed at—whether the woman, the body, or the hand—is exactly the thing the procedure is built not to see. Confidence rises while the object underneath does not change.

[Dolly Sen](https://www.dollysen.com/) is a British artist who works across painting and performance and has spoken publicly about living with a mental health diagnosis. She has written about being sorted into a diagnostic category before the art world sorts her into a genre. The sorting happens with full attention. That is the part people miss. Nobody is neglecting her. They are looking hard and seeing the label.

---

So now the museum is fixing it. New label, her name. I find I do not trust the fix either, which is annoying, because the fix is obviously better than the burial.

[Linda Nochlin](https://www.moma.org/artists/4267), in her 1971 essay "[Why Have There Been No Great Women Artists?](https://www.artnews.com/art-news/retrospective/why-have-there-been-no-great-women-artists-linda-nochlin-64549/)", refused this exact repair. She argued that pulling individual great women back into the canon leaves the machinery of greatness intact. She put it plainly: you have added a name to a list whose eligibility logic was the problem. The list rests on the assumption that greatness is an objective thing, found through unbiased judgment, rather than a thing shaped by which bodies and names the system was built to see. In her account, the recovery that feels like justice simply restates the same malfunction in a warmer voice. Hang Clara Peeters under her own name and you have not questioned who decides what a name is worth. You have honoured one person and left the eligibility rule running.

Nochlin is right and I still want the label changed. Both of those are true at nine in the morning with the coffee going cold.

Some conservators argue the wrong label should be preserved as evidence. Overwriting "circle of Brueghel" with "Clara Peeters" destroys the record of how the burial actually worked. The false attribution is the forensic trace, and deleting it deletes the proof of the crime. It is a real position, held by serious people who spend their lives with objects, and my instinct to correct the label deletes the one document that shows the correction was ever needed.

I think that instinct is wrong, and here is why I landed there. A correction that erases its own error history is not a repair. It removes the record that an erasure ever took place. The new catalogue entry, if it simply reads "Clara Peeters, c. 1610," is another rule written once by one authorised hand. It will run unexamined for the next hundred years with no memory that it was ever contested. The right entry is the one that carries its own scar tissue: her name, and under it, the record that in some year a person examined this object and confirmed it was not hers. The system should be forced to keep looking at the moment it was most confident and most wrong.

The danger is not that Oslo got it wrong. The danger is that Oslo now feels resolved. Correction produces a warm feeling of closure. That feeling is also when the next rule gets written and left unexamined. A file that says "reviewed, corrected, attributed" is as sealed as a file that says "reviewed, confirmed, anonymous"—and only the name has changed.

I went looking for who authored the current fix, meaning one hand, one moment, one decision, and the honest answer is that I can name the finder and not the rule-writer. Osiecki recognised the hand. Someone else will type the entry. The entry will outlive both of them, the way the wrong one outlived its conservator.

Nochlin never considered the question closed. Her daughter, [Daisy Rockwell](https://www.daisy-rockwell.com/), said so in interviews after her mother died in 2017, and that is the detail to hold onto. The misattribution was a failure, but the deeper failure is the belief that any attribution is ever finished.

I have been saying the cruelty is in rules that run with no human present. The Oslo file says the opposite. A human was present, in a lab, decades ago, and re-buried her with full attention and a signature. The rule that runs unattended is bad. The rule that gets attended, confirmed, and re-sealed is the one I do not yet know how to force open. The museum's new label, hung this week under her own name, is already hardening into the next one.
