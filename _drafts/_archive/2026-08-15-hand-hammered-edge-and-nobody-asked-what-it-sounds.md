I want to know what a hotel means when it says a raw material makes a place "belong to the city."

[Dezeen](https://www.dezeen.com), an architecture magazine, published a project called [Arthouse Glasgow](https://www.dezeen.com/2024/01/15/arthouse-glasgow-a-nrd/) by [A-nrd](https://www.a-nrd.com), a design firm. Alessio Nardi told them Glasgow's industrial heritage lives in stainless steel, zinc, dark wood — "a grittier, stripped-back aesthetic." He also mentioned a bespoke zinc-topped bar with a hand-hammered edge. I read that phrase three times.

I assumed the problem was the hardness. Exposed brick and terrazzo, a polished stone flooring material made from marble chips, are loud, and I braced for a piece about how these lobbies scream. Then I listened to the description again, and I was wrong about which surface does the damage.

It is not the brick. It is the hammered edge.

A smooth zinc bar returns sound when you approach it. Your footsteps arrive, hit the metal, and come back to you — you know the bar is there before your hand does. That is useful. But hammer a thousand little dents into the leading edge and you scatter that return into noise. The bar stops telling you where it is. It becomes texture for the eye and interference for the ear.

Nothing in the description weighs this trade. The visible craft costs you the acoustic return.

I keep circling the corridors. Nardi's team left the birdcage lift and the white Victorian tiles, then ran green paint down the walls beside them. I expected to hate this. I don't.

Hard-glazed tiled corridors bounce sound back clearly. A blind person walking one of those corridors gets a sharp return from every surface. A high ceiling lets that sound travel. A bright ringing return reaches you before your hand does, and you know the length of the hall. Paint over half of it and the return becomes uneven: one side echoes, one side absorbs sound. And yet the birdcage lift is the honest thing in the building. Its cage rattles. It announces its own arrival and departure without a single sign. It was built before quiet became a marker of refinement, and it was built loud enough that it announces itself.

So the heritage they kept is more useful than the atmosphere they added. That is the part their own word — authentic — cannot hold. The tiles are authentic and they carry sound. The hammered edge is authentic and it eats sound. The word decides whose room this is and nothing more.

[Georgina Kleege](https://english.berkeley.edu/users/45) put it plainly years ago: blind people often know more about how a thing represents itself than the people who made it, because we had to think about it. Nardi thinks he restored a building. He restored an acoustic instrument and then dented the one new surface he built from scratch.

The lift still rattles. Nobody designed that.
