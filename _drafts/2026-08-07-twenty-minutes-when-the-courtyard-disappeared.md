A single note held long enough stops being music and becomes architecture. I went to Walthamstow in September to prove this, or rather to praise it, at the [Backyard Biennial East](https://backyardbiennial.org), a community art festival behind a row of houses in East London. A broadsheet critic had just called the festival meaningless in print. He used the word about art you smell, art that hums through your feet, art made by fifty people who are not artists. I read the review on the subway over my everything bagel and got annoyed enough to buy a ticket. The critic missed the point, obviously. I was going to say how.

Here is the point I thought I would make. In a traditional gallery, everything non-visual becomes just background atmosphere. You stand still, you keep quiet, you look. The critic dismissed the drone, the rosewater smell, and the crowd humming a harmonium note because his training treated only sight as valid understanding.

I have watched this happen to a person's face. A Deaf friend once tried to write down, on a museum notepad, what she saw in a Rothko, and the docent kept answering about what Rothko meant. Her seeing was treated as a question someone else already held the answer to. So the biennial's defence had a shape I recognised. Art in the body, art in the nose, is more open, not less. That was my thesis, ready before I arrived.

Then I stood in the courtyard while the harmonium drone came on.

I navigate a room by its echoes. Not romantically. A doorway returns a different edge of sound than a brick wall does. The gap between two stalls has its own small emptiness I can hear. In that courtyard, for the twenty minutes the drone held, all of it flattened into one wash. The door, the wall, the gap — gone. I stood dead still because I had lost the map. The piece the critic called meaningless was, for those twenty minutes, the thing that erased the space for me.

So much for access-rich.

[Christine Sun Kim](https://christinesunkim.com/) has been saying a version of this for years, in her own register. She refuses the vibration cliché. When hearing people say Deaf people feel music through vibration, she hears a hearing person flattering their own imagination — deciding what her body does so they can feel generous about it. In a 2023 talk she was blunt: the assumption is the problem, not the sound. She was right about a thing I was about to do to blind visitors. I was ready to hand them a drone and call it inclusion, because the drone was not the eye, and anything-not-the-eye had become my hero by default.

There is a comfortable position I want to argue against here, and it is nearly my own. The claim that a good visual channel — a caption, a colour-code, a clean information hierarchy — is what makes a place open to more people. I have made that argument. [Enid Marx](https://www.vam.ac.uk/collections/search?listing_id=2344755)'s Underground seat fabric works as a redundant channel. It tells you where you are without the announcement. I wove a whole essay around it and I stand by the seat. But no caption restores a courtyard whose echoes have been painted over. For the person navigating by sound, the acoustics are not the mood-lighting around the real information. They are the information. Overwrite them and there is nothing left to caption.

I do not doubt that the 2018 harmonium performance outside the National Theatre in Dhaka was extraordinary. A multisensory experience just creates different barriers for different people. The critic was wrong to call the festival meaningless. I was wrong to call it access.

The drone stopped. The courtyard came back — doorway, wall, gap, in that order, the way it always does. A woman near me said out loud, to nobody, "I could smell the roses better once it went quiet."
