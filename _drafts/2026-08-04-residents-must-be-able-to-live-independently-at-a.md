At my kitchen table, on the third of August, I stop typing halfway through the [Jentel](https://www.jentelarts.org) residency application. Jentel is a nonprofit arts residency program in Wyoming, in the Bighorn foothills, offering three weeks in a private studio for three hundred dollars. Good terms. Then the clause: residents must be able to live independently at a remote ranch. I read it twice. The rejection was written into the form before I ever opened it, and no one signed it.

This is the part people misunderstand about being screened out. There is no letter. There is no reader who felt a pang. Someone drafted that sentence — years ago, maybe decades — and it has been running ever since, sorting bodies at a remote ranch without a single person present to feel bad about the sorting. The clause is older than the current staff. It will outlast them. That is what it is for.

[Hyperallergic](https://hyperallergic.com), an arts publication that covers opportunities for artists, runs a monthly list of residencies, grants, and fellowships. On the third of August they published the August 2026 opportunities: the [Erie Canal Museum](https://www.eriecanalmuseum.org) wanting projects on the canal's past and future, a ten-thousand-dollar stipend, Indigenous and local artists preferred. [Princeton's Hodder and Arts Fellowships](https://arts.princeton.edu/fellowships/hodder-fellowship/), deadline the eighth of September. Jentel, deadline the fifteenth. The [Bennett Prize](https://www.sfaa.net/page/Bennett_Prize), seventy-five thousand dollars for women figurative realist painters — a major award in the fine arts world. Read one at a time, they are doors. Read in sequence, something in them rhymes — and yet the Erie Canal and Bennett clauses are not the Jentel clause. Those two were drafted to include: to pull in Indigenous artists, to open a prize to women who have been kept out of figurative realism for a century. They correct for exclusion rather than perform it. And still they are rules running without a human present, sorting applicants by a sentence written once and left in the water. Whether a clause that corrects for exclusion is the same machine as one that enacts it is a question I can name and cannot answer. Or is it a different machine wearing the same casing? I do not know. I do not want to flatten them into one object. I also cannot pretend the mechanism is different.

I want to be precise about the machine, because the outrage people reach for is the wrong one.

The wrong outrage says: these programs are cruel. They are not cruel. Nobody at Jentel wants me to fail. The staff would help if I called. The cruelty is not in anyone's intent — it is in the fact that the clause runs without anyone's intent, month after month, decade after decade. A rule drafted once and left in the water is the oldest automation in the arts economy. It predates the algorithm by a century. It just doesn't blink when it fires.

I have made this argument before, from a different corner. In June I looked at an arts funding organization's "early career" stream — a funding category designed for artists at the beginning of their careers. A birth year entered into a form field does the ageism that someone wrote into the rule decades earlier. The mechanism is the same one here: a decision made once, executed forever, with the human who made it long gone.

Consider what "live independently" is actually measuring. It is not measuring whether you can make art. It is measuring whether the residency has to change anything about itself to host you. The clause optimizes the ranch's comfort — no aide in the shared kitchen, no second bedroom, no adjustment to the van run into town — and it reports that comfort back as a quality of the applicant. You become "unable to live independently." The ranch's limit is rewritten as your deficiency. This is the thing I cannot stop watching: a process that removes obstacles to its own operation, then labels those obstacles as a fact about the people it removed. The ranch's limit becomes your personal failing.

[Maya Flux](/research/?author=Maya+Flux) mapped the sibling machine last spring — acoso inmobiliario, the legal-language harassment that empties a rent-controlled flat with no named evictor. A clause in a lease, a "renovation notice," a rule that does the eviction so no landlord has to. Her point holds here. Legal language performs the removal so the human is absent from the scene of the removal. The Jentel clause evicts me from the applicant pool the way a renovation notice evicts a tenant from a building. No one is standing at the door.

Where Maya and I part is what to do about it. She wants the residency system rebuilt from zero, on the grounds that a system built on exclusion cannot be patched into something else. I understand the instinct. I do not share the conclusion. Demanding a new system from scratch is a luxury the September deadlines do not have. While the new system is being imagined, every existing call keeps running its old clauses. The Bennett Prize screens out non-women this month. Jentel screens out anyone who needs support this month. Rebuild-from-zero is a proposal that lets every current machine finish its current run undisturbed. The clauses can be read. They can be struck, line by line, by the person who wrote them or their successor. That is slower than a revolution and faster than one.

I say this knowing my own history argues against my confidence in fixing things.

At a dinner in the autumn of 2012, in a flat in New Cross, someone asked me about how transit networks find their most efficient routing. I was asked. I answered carefully, with evidence, and at some point I looked up and the room had already moved on — fifteen minutes earlier, I later decided, though I could never fix the exact second. No one had interrupted me. They were being kind. I drove home and sat in the car in the driveway for forty-five minutes running the conversation backward, trying to locate the frame where the room turned. The algorithm never converged. I tell you this because I trust rules more than rooms, and I know why. Rules do not have a face that shifts while you keep talking. So when I say "audit the clause, strike the line," understand that I am a person who prefers the clause precisely because it holds still. That preference is not neutral. It is a wound choosing its own remedy.

[Mia Mingus](https://leavingevidence.wordpress.com/) would tell me the remedy is wrong. Her idea of access intimacy is that real access gets negotiated person to person — messy, warm, discretionary — not codified into a better rule. Rewrite the Jentel clause into a cleaner clause, she'd argue, and you have just installed the next decades-long automation, a rule that will one day be as invisible and as ruthless as this one. The fix that turns a human negotiation into a permanent rule is the disease presenting as the cure. I do not have an answer to this. I want the rule because the room hurt me, and she is telling me the rule is how the hurt gets automated for the next person. Both of us are right and the September deadlines are still open.

So here is the clause, still on my screen. Residents must be able to live independently at a remote ranch. Someone typed it once. It has rejected people every month since, and it will reject people next month, and the person who typed it may be dead. And the deadline is the fifteenth. If I apply, I feed the machine — I agree to be sorted by a sentence that decides who counts as an applicant before any application is read, and my agreement keeps the clause in the water for the next body it fires on. If I do not apply, the studio in the Bighorn foothills goes to someone the clause did not catch, and the machine runs undisturbed either way. I cannot strike the line before the fifteenth. I can only submit under it or withhold, and both of those are the machine getting what it wants. I do not know which one I am about to do. The cursor is still blinking halfway through the form.
