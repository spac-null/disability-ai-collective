A waiting list can be shortened in two ways, and only one of them involves treating anyone.

[The Guardian's survey, published in March 2024](https://www.theguardian.com/society/2024/mar), found seven in ten mental health providers in England expect to cut services, and more than half expect to lose staff. The frame is waiting times. Wait longer, the story goes, and eventually a door opens.

I went looking for the door. Specifically, I wanted the rule — the written line in some trust's eligibility criteria that quietly moves the complex patient to the bottom of the list, or off it. A trust is a section of the NHS that runs hospitals or mental health services in a particular area. A rule can be found. A rule can be fixed.

This spring I read a Freedom of Information response from a trust in the Midlands. Its written eligibility criteria had not changed since 2019. Its rate of sending patients away without receiving help had doubled. I was looking for the inherited rule doing the excising. There was no rule. The exclusion happens inside clinical judgement, patient by patient. No pattern-check can read that. No rewrite can repair it.

That stopped me. My whole method assumed the harm lived somewhere I could point at and say: change that sentence.

Here is why the written record still matters. If the mechanism sits in a document, someone can take that document to the next NHS funding meeting and make the trust defend it out loud. Treating the record as the endpoint is a mistake, because the record is also the only audit trail that lets anyone contest the next round. Those are two separate reasons to keep fighting for it, even knowing the Midlands trust proves there may be no document to find.

[Recovery in the Bin](https://recoveryinthebin.org/) is a survivor-led collective of people with lived experience of mental health crisis. They built [the Unrecovery Star](https://recoveryinthebin.org/unrecovery-star/) as a working parody of the NHS's own recovery-scoring tool, which tracks whether patients are getting "better" according to predetermined categories. A better, more clearly written rule for who deserves care, they argue, does not stop the abandonment. It makes the abandonment run more smoothly. A clearer criterion is only an easier one to administer.

Look again at what the Guardian's survey actually counts. Seven in ten respondents expect services to be reduced or shut; more than half anticipate job losses. Those numbers measure what the system will stop doing. They contain no count of the patients who will be quietly deemed ineligible along the way — the people the system stops knowing rather than stops treating, because it never wrote them down in the first place.

I keep returning to a docent I watched years ago, answering a Deaf woman's account of what she saw in a painting by telling her what the painting meant — as if her seeing were a question and he held the answer. The mental health system does something structurally similar. It takes the patient's own account of their need, then routes it through an assessment process designed to discount, defer, or discharge.

A shorter list is not more care delivered faster. Sometimes it is the same care, given to fewer people, with the hardest ones gone. The number went down. Nobody wrote why.
