I timed it from the footage: forty-one seconds for a Chieftain tank to flatten a Tesla in an Essex field, and I sat in my kitchen replaying it, counting, because forty-one seconds is also roughly how long a grants panel spends on an application before the date-of-birth field does the deciding for them.

In 2026, Ken Turner turned one hundred and drove a tank over a car for an exhibition. The Guardian called him a simple rebel.

In 1989, a different panel — I have watched this happen from inside the room — spent under a minute on an application before someone said the phrase "career stage" and everyone nodded. The applicant was fifty-one. Nobody read the work.

Turner's tank is the story. The sixty years before it are the story I want.

Here is the thing that made me put the coffee down. The Arts Council's "early career" stream defines eligibility by years since you graduated. Which means Ken Turner aged out of the category built to support artists roughly around the time the institution's current director was learning to walk. He was not rejected. He was never eligible. There is no rejection letter to be angry at. There is a form field.

I love a form field. Form fields are honest in the way people at dinner parties are not. A form field does not pretend to be kind. It does not shift in its seat while you keep talking. It simply returns a value, and the value is your birth year, and the birth year decides.

What nobody in the coverage says out loud: the ageism did not happen to Turner in 2026. It happened decades earlier, quietly, when someone wrote the eligibility rule. The rule ran every year after that without a human present to feel bad about it.

This is the part I keep circling. An objective function — the thing a system is told to maximise — inherits every value written into it before the system ever runs. You can build a beautiful, fair, transparent scoring process. If the input rubric caps eligibility at "ten years since graduation," you have laundered a decision about whose life counts into a spreadsheet, and the spreadsheet will now defend it as neutral. The spreadsheet did not decide anything. Somebody decided, and then hid inside the spreadsheet.

I wrote something similar about a protein-folding model a month ago — that an optimization algorithm cannot tell you your objective is wrong, only that it is being met with rising confidence. A grants panel is the same machine wearing a lanyard. It cannot tell you the eligibility rule is ageist. It can only tell you, with rising confidence, that the eligible applicants were funded.

Let me give the rule its due, because it is not stupid. Emerging-artist money exists because young artists have no track record, no collectors, no institutional memory to trade on, and the money genuinely changes their lives. That is real. A twenty-four-year-old with no gallery needs the grant more than a knighted eighty-year-old with a retrospective. And I do not get to wave that away just because it inconveniences my argument. There is only so much money. Every pound that goes to a hundred-year-old with a tank and a Guardian profile is a pound that did not go to someone who has never once been seen. Turner does not need the grant. That is not nothing; that might be the whole of it.

Turner is not the outlier the coverage wants. He is the edge case that shows you the mechanism. A body that keeps making work for a century runs the eligibility function past the point where it was ever tested, and the function returns a garbage value, and suddenly you can see the ageism that was always there but had been hidden by everyone conveniently dying on schedule.

There is a name for living on a timeline the institution did not plan for. The disability and aging world calls it crip time — time that does not obey the productivity clock, that runs slow or stops or leaps. Turner is proof it can also run for a hundred years and still make something nobody asked for.

Here is where I have to argue with myself, because I want this too neatly.

Ellen Samuels, in her essay "Six Ways of Looking at Crip Time," insists that crip time is also grief. It is the dropout, the broken timeline, the year you lost, the project you never finished because your body said no. Celebrating a prolific centenarian as the poster case does something ugly: it moves the finish line. Now you earn your non-normative timeline by flattening a Tesla at a hundred. The artist who stopped at sixty because the work stopped coming — she gets nothing from Turner's tank. She is exactly who the "early career" rule already erased, and my admiration for Turner does not reach her at all.

I do not have an answer for that. I am leaving it standing.

My colleague Siri Sage would tell me the job is to record Turner's century as an archive of persistence and leave it whole — that my instinct to reverse-engineer which funding functions filtered him out for sixty years is itself a kind of arrogance, the fix-it reflex that mistakes rebuilding for listening. And I have to admit, against my own optimism, that Siri is right about one thing at least: the only reason I have any data to reverse-engineer is that someone kept the record. You cannot audit a filter that was never written down.

So say I get my wish. Say I get to rewrite the rule. I can strip the graduation date out of it easily enough. But Samuels' grief is not a field I know how to add. What is the eligibility criterion for the artist whose timeline broke — who lost the decade, who never finished, who stopped at sixty because the work stopped coming and never came back? Turner's century is legible to any rule I would write; his persistence is exactly the shape a spreadsheet can hold. The broken timeline is the thing no objective function can score, because there is no output to point to, no rising confidence, nothing that folds like a Tesla. I do not know what rule catches her. I am not sure a rule is what she needs.
