A pink crystal went up in northern Sweden last week, and the first thing I wanted to know was what it sounds like from the inside.

Bigert & Bergström, a duo from Stockholm, finished a sauna in Skellefteå on a site that spent a century poisoning itself with pulp-mill chemistry. Seventeen years of scraping the soil clean. Then a sculpture you can sit inside and sweat. It leans back four degrees so it looks like it grew out of the ground. Roughly 280 mirrored prisms, titanium boiled in a vacuum until it condensed pink onto steel. The photographs, I'm told, are extraordinary.

I will never know if the photographs are extraordinary. What I can tell you is what a faceted metal chamber does to a voice, and to water on hot stones, and to the small involuntary sounds a room makes when heat moves through it. I can tell you that nobody involved in this project appears to have asked.

Here is the thing about a sauna. It is one of the few buildings humans make where the whole point is sensation and almost none of it is looking. You go in to feel heat on skin. You go in for the hiss when someone ladles water onto the stones. You go in, if it is a good one, for a particular hush. The best saunas I have been in have a low, close acoustic that presses in on you like the heat does. You lower your voice without deciding to. The room asks for it.

So a sauna shaped like a crystal is a strange proposition before anyone says a word about wheelchairs or thresholds. It is a building whose designers spent their attention on how it catches light, in a room where the experience is heat and sound and the weight of air. They have optimised the one channel that stops mattering the moment you close the door.

I don't say this to be clever. I say it because I have spent fifteen years being paid to think about exactly the thing they skipped.

---

Start with the material. Titanium-coated mirrored stainless steel, cut into flat facets. Metal is a mirror for sound the same way it is for light. Hard, dense, unforgiving surfaces bounce sound back almost whole. Soft surfaces — wood, cloth, a human body, water — soak some of it up and hand the rest back changed.

A traditional Finnish sauna is lined in wood for reasons that have nothing to do with looking rustic. Wood absorbs. It takes the high, harsh edges off a sound and softens what comes back. The room stays warm to the ear as well as the skin. You can talk quietly and be heard. The silence between words has a texture you could describe as furred.

Put 280 flat metal facets around a heat chamber and you get something else entirely. Every hard consonant comes back at you off the walls. The hiss of water on stones — normally a single soft event that decays fast — smears across all those angled surfaces and returns as a scatter. Faceted geometry is what concert-hall designers use on purpose, in specific spots, to break up sound and stop it pooling. Do it to a whole small room and you don't get richness. You get a wash where nothing sits still.

I have not been inside the Lithium Crystal Sauna. I want to be honest about that. I am reading its acoustic off its geometry and its skin, the way I read most buildings before I ever get to them. Sometimes I am wrong. When I recorded a renovated tram shelter in Amsterdam in 2019, I expected the new glass to ring like a bell and it barely did — they had lined the roof with something I couldn't see and hadn't been told about. So I hold this loosely. But metal and facets and heat is not a combination that rewards the ear, and I would put money on it.

Now the part that actually costs me something to say.

I thought, when I started thinking about this piece, that the acoustic failure was the interesting failure. That the artists had built a beautiful thing for the eyes and forgotten there was a person inside it with other senses running. Clean argument. The kind I have built a career on. Sighted design defaults to the looking body, and here is another gorgeous example.

Then I did what I try to do before I commit, which is to find the person who disagrees with me. I went looking for anyone who had written seriously about how saunas actually feel to sit in, and I ended up back with something the sauna scholar Mikkel Aaland has said for years about the Finnish original — that the löyly, the burst of steam off the stones, is felt as a wave that moves through the whole room and every body in it at once. Not heard. Felt. A pressure change, a heat front, a thing your skin and your inner ear register together before your mind names it.

And that stopped me. Because if the real event in a sauna is a wave you feel rather than a sound you hear, then my clean acoustic complaint is aiming at the wrong channel. The steam wave doesn't care whether the walls are wood or metal. It arrives on your skin regardless. A Deaf sauna-goer would get the whole of it. The one thing I know how to measure — reverberation, absorption, the return off a surface — might be the least important thing happening in the room.

I sat with that for a couple of days and it genuinely annoyed me, because I wanted my point back.

---

Here is where I landed, and it is not where I started.

The failure in Skellefteå is not that the metal walls ring wrong. That might even be survivable. The failure is that nobody thought about the inside as a place a body has to get into, orient itself in, and stay in without falling over.

A four-degree backward lean. The artists wanted it to look like it emerged from the ground, and it does, apparently, magnificently. But a four-degree slope is not nothing to a body that navigates by balance and by the feel of the floor under the feet. I read a room through my feet more than through anything else. Level tells me where I am. A floor that lists, in a chamber where I am also being cooked and can't see, where the walls are all the same hard cut angle and give me no landmark to sort one from another — that is a room that has removed every rail I would reach for. Not a handrail. The invisible rails. The place where the sound of my own footstep changes because the wall got closer. The spot where the air moves because there's a door.

In a wood-lined sauna I can find those. The wall in front of me sounds different from the wall to my side, because the bench and the door and the corner all return my own small noises differently. I build the room in my head from the way it answers me back. Take a step, hear the answer, place myself. It is not mystical and it is not a superpower. It is the same thing a sighted person does with a glance, done through the ear and the skin instead, and it is faster and more accurate than most people would credit.

Put me in a chamber of 280 identical facets and every wall answers the same. The room refuses to tell me where I am. Every surface is a mirror, and a mirror has no address.

That is the exclusion, and it is worse than a missing ramp, because a missing ramp at least admits what it is. This building presents itself as a temple of sensation and then flattens the actual sensory information a body needs to be safe in it. It is loud with feeling and silent with fact.

I keep circling back to the word the project uses. Remediated. Seventeen years of cleaning poison out of the ground before anyone could stand on it. I find that genuinely moving. A site that spent a hundred years making people sick, made safe enough to sit and sweat and feel your own pulse. There is real care in that. Someone tested that soil, batch after batch, for the better part of two decades.

And then they built a wellness monument on top of it that a blind person can't safely walk into and a person who overheats dangerously can't regulate inside, because there is no mention anywhere of how you'd know when to leave. No cool signal. No graduated threshold. Just heat and mirror and a floor that leans.

They spent seventeen years thinking about what the ground does to a body. They spent, as far as I can tell, no time thinking about what the room does to one.

---

I want to bring in a person here, because I keep talking about metal and slopes and it would be easy to lose the actual body.

My mother was an audiologist in Edinburgh. When I was small she used to test rooms the way other parents test bathwater — walk in, clap once, listen to the answer, decide whether it was a good room or a bad one before she'd looked at a single thing in it. She did this in restaurants, in churches, in the waiting rooms of other doctors. Once, in a new hospital wing in the late nineties, she clapped in the corridor and said, out loud, in front of a consultant: "You'll not hear a word anyone says to you in here." She was right. Every hard surface, nothing to soak the sound, all the voices smearing into each other. The consultant found it funny. The patients who couldn't hear their own diagnosis over the ring of the corridor did not, I imagine, find it funny.

That clap is the whole of what I do. Send a small signal into a room, listen to how it comes back, know the room. The Lithium Crystal Sauna would give my mother's clap back to her as a hard bright scatter with no information in it. She'd have known in one second it was a room that didn't want to tell you anything true.

The point is not that saunas should be dull. I love a room that sings. The swimming pool at my local centre in Amsterdam, empty at quarter to six in the morning before anyone arrives, is the most beautiful acoustic I know — all that tiled hardness over still water, the whole space holding its breath and hearing itself. I stand in the doorway for forty seconds most mornings for no reason except greed. So I am not against the drama of a hard, resonant room. I am against a hard resonant room that has been designed entirely for the one sense that switches off the moment you're inside it, by people who never asked what the others were doing.

---

There is a version of this piece I could have written a year ago that would have been angrier and less true. It would have said: sighted designers build for sighted bodies, full stop, and here is proof. Case closed.

But the löyly wave complicated that, and I'm glad it did. Because the honest position is stranger and more useful. A sauna is one of the few public buildings where the disabled sensory range and the non-disabled one converge — where a Deaf person and a blind person and a person who feels heat as pain all have equally legitimate, equally central claims on what the room is for, because none of them are about looking. It is, potentially, the most level sensory ground in public architecture. Everybody in a sauna is reduced to skin and breath and the wave off the stones.

And Bigert & Bergström took that rare piece of common ground and built a sculpture for the eyes on top of it. They had, without knowing it, one of the few chances architecture offers to design from feeling first. They used it to make something that photographs well.

I don't think they're villains. I think they're artists who talked, in their own words to Dezeen, about wanting to build on the site's history rather than erase it, and who clearly meant it. The lithium reference is thoughtful — a mineral that promises a clean future and poisons the ground it comes out of, planted on ground that was poisoned and cleaned. That is a real idea, held with real care. The contradiction they wanted to sit inside is a good one.

They just held it entirely at the level of what the thing means when you stand back and look at it. The meaning is in the silhouette against the sky. It is not in the room. And a sauna is only ever the room.

---

Disability Pride Month is on this week, which means my feeds are full of people insisting that access is not an afterthought and design should start from the widest range of bodies. I believe all of it. I also notice that the wellness industry, which sells sensation harder than any sector alive, keeps building its temples for the one body that can see the brochure. The steam room, the salt cave, the ice bath, the crystal sauna — spaces sold entirely on how they feel, designed entirely on how they look.

I keep coming back to a Tuesday a long time ago, and the specific thing I learned from it, which was that there are things a body genuinely cannot give another body, and pretending otherwise is a kind of cruelty. I can't tell you what the pink looks like against the snow. That's true and it doesn't wound me anymore. What wounds me is the reverse — a whole building that can't, or won't, tell me the one thing I actually need from it, which is where the walls are and whether the floor is level and when it is time to get out.

The crystal will be there for years. People will drive to Skellefteå to sit inside a pink jewel on cleaned poison and sweat, and most of them will love it, and I am not going to pretend they shouldn't. But when the water hits the stones in there and the hiss comes back off 280 mirrored facets as a scatter with no direction in it, somebody sitting in that heat with their eyes shut — which is everybody, eventually — is going to lose the room for a second. They just won't have the words for what went missing.

I do. It's the floor telling you where you are. Nobody thought to leave that in.
