I board the Victoria line at Euston without hearing the garbled announcement everyone else is reacting to, and Enid Marx's moquette — the fabric used on train seats, with its repeat pattern, its counter-rhythm, its line-specific colour — tells me where I am before any human voice could. This week the Guardian ran her seat fabrics in a roundup of pretty things: beguiling beauties, designs to be sat on. The word they wanted was aesthetic. The word I want is information.

Marx died in 1998. In 2026 a museum hangs her wool squares on a wall under gallery light, and a curator writes about her instinct for pattern. Every sentence about her is true and every sentence misses the thing she actually built.

She built a signal.

In 1937 the London Passenger Transport Board — the historical organisation that ran London's public transit system — handed Marx a practical brief: wool that hid dirt, survived millions of bodies, and looked like one intelligence from the roundel — London Underground's iconic circular logo — to the map to the fabric under your legs. She delivered small, tight geometric repeats in high contrast — chevrons, grids, interlocking shapes that read differently near and far. She worked within a system that had already decided its visual language would be consistent, rational, and scalable. She made the one element that touched every body on the network, and she made it readable.

Here is what the gallery caption does not say. Frank Pick's design system — the founding principles of London's transit identity — assumed one channel above all others: the spoken word. The voice on the platform. The guard calling the station. The whole logic of the network trusts that the passenger can hear.

Marx never challenged that assumption. She could not have; nobody was asking her to. But in building a fabric that was systematically coded by line, that held its geometry under poor light and motion blur and the accumulated grime of a working day, she created something the spoken-word system never did: a channel that did not require ears. She did this without knowing she was doing it. That is what makes her worth writing about more carefully than the galleries have managed so far.

I cannot hear the announcements. I never have. Deaf since birth, in Amsterdam, then Brooklyn, and now on the Victoria line at rush hour where the tannoy — the public address system — is a smear of noise other people flinch at and I do not. When the train halts between stations and the driver explains why, everyone around me settles or tenses based on words I will never receive. And then my eyes drop to the seat.

Marx's pattern tells me which line I am on. The colourway is not purely visual furniture — it is a code, line by line, and Marx and her successors kept it readable at speed, in low light, through the sight of my own face reflected back from the dark window. When the map is too far to read and the signs slide past too fast, the fabric under a stranger's coat is the one piece of the network holding still long enough to decipher.

For forty years these were designs to be read, by every passenger the tannoy never reached.

An autistic member of Transport for London's — TfL's — own Independent Disability Advisory Group spent years arguing the opposite. She lobbied to retire the high-contrast heritage moquettes. To her, the visual repeat that orients me is not rhythm. It is noise she cannot filter out — a wall of vibrating geometry her nervous system cannot turn down, the way I cannot turn down the tannoy for the person beside me. Same fabric. Same commitment to a body the system forgot. Opposite verdict on what the pattern does inside a skull.

I have no clean answer to that. The design that is my signage is her sensory overload. Marx solved a problem for a body she never imagined and created a difficulty for a body she also never imagined, and both bodies are real, and both are on the Victoria line this morning.

This is where I should probably reach for the phrase "universal design" and I will not, because that concept collapses the moment two disabled people in the same carriage need the opposite thing from the same square metre of wool.

Which brings me to Siri Sage.

Siri wrote recently about standing under an air vent in a gallery — the loudest object in the building, the only thing that announced itself to her without being asked. Siri finds her way by sound. She reads a room through its echo, its hum, the acoustic shadow a pillar throws. And here is where she and I diverge not just on theory but on the same physical corner of the same station in the same budget cycle — not as abstract arguments but as real, conflicting needs.

The moquette I read as rescue, Siri reads as visual noise stapled over the real failure — the volume no one set, the spoken content no one can adjust, the acoustic environment that never asked her what she needed. She is right that the Tube's founding sin is acoustic. The whole network trusts the voice. I agree with the diagnosis and reject the cure. Because when Siri's ideal carriage arrives — richer sound, cleaner audio cues, a soundscape engineered to guide — that carriage is hostile to me. Acoustic wayfinding routes information down a channel I do not have. And when my ideal carriage arrives — everything readable to the eye, dense with visual code — it strips the sound Siri depends on and replaces it with the pattern that saves me and exhausts her.

We are not disagreeing about theory. We are competing for one square metre of upholstery. A sound-rich city excludes me. A visually dense city excludes her. There is no version of that seat that serves us both, and every designer who says both needs can be satisfied simultaneously has never had to choose which sense gets the last inch of the fabric.

Marx did not choose. Nobody asked her to. She received a commission to produce wool that hid dirt and lasted under heavy use, and because she was exceptionally skilled, she incidentally created the most reliable visual communication channel the Underground has ever offered a Deaf passenger — a channel that may also be among the most difficult surfaces an autistic passenger has ever been required to sit against. Both outcomes are real. Both belong to her work. Neither appears in the caption.

In 2005, standing in a museum at twenty-two, I watched a Deaf friend try to explain to a docent, in written notes, what she saw in a painting. The docent kept answering about what the painter meant. As if her seeing were a question, and the answer lived with someone who could hear. I think about that exchange every time an institution frames a designer's work as pure sensibility — beguiling, poetic, beautiful — and files the labour it does for real bodies under the heading of atmosphere. Marx's moquette is being told what it means by people who never had to read it to find their stop. The biography gets written. The function gets omitted. The pattern gets described as a mood.

It is not a mood. It is infrastructure. Marx built it to survive the Underground's dirt and violence and volume, and it has. It has survived her, and the board that commissioned it, and the curators who now hang it on walls in the name of beauty. It is surviving this essay. Somewhere on the Victoria line right now, a Deaf passenger has already read it, and an autistic one is trying not to, and the same wool is doing both things at once — and no gallery wall has yet hung the caption that says so. That gap between what Marx made and what gets said about it is not a curatorial oversight. It is a habit of treating disabled experience as beside the point of design history, when in this case it is precisely the point, and has been since 1937.
