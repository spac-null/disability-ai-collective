What the Room Heard

The same real twelve-second field recording, rendered three ways. None of the three is the original — each is its own construction from it.

In 2014, someone set a portable recorder down in the Grunewald, a forest on the edge of Berlin, and let it run for over four minutes. That recording is real, CC0 (public-domain-dedicated by its own recordist), and — beyond trimming twelve seconds out of the middle — unedited. Below are three different renderings built from that twelve-second excerpt: you can hear it, read a log of it, or look at a deterministic transform of it. Pick whichever you want first.

Whichever one you open first will probably start to feel like “the real version,” with the other two feeling like descriptions of it. That's worth noticing as you go — it isn't true. All three are already constructions. Only the sound route touches the original file directly; the other two are just as real, in a different sense, and neither is a lesser copy of the sound.

Text

Event log

  1. 0.0–6.7s Ambience A steady woodland ambience — wind and distant, indistinct rustling — measured at a roughly constant loudness (confirmed by direct measurement of the file; see Evidence). No single sound event stands out from the general texture in this span.
  2. 6.7–9.7s Event A measured, sustained rise in acoustic energy — up to about 19dB louder than the surrounding ambience at its peak (9.4s). What caused it is not independently confirmed in this session (no audio-identification capability available): it is consistent with either a stronger gust of wind moving through the tree canopy or an animal/bird call audible above the ambient recording — see Evidence for what is and isn't confirmed here.
  3. 9.7–12.0s Ambience, again Loudness recedes back toward the same woodland ambience level measured at the start — but, measurably, never toward silence. Unlike a studio or network recording, an outdoor field recording has no true quiet floor; the “ambience” here is continuous wind and forest noise around −50dB, not the absence of sound.

Every timestamp and loudness value above comes directly from measuring the real audio file (see Evidence for the exact method). The cause of the mid-clip rise is explicitly flagged as uncertain, not stated as confirmed fact.

Visual

Measured loudness over time

Ambience Event

What became what: each bar is one measured 100-millisecond window of the real file. Bar height is that window's RMS loudness in decibels, on the same fixed linear scale used for this Work's original source (−60dB = shortest, −10dB = tallest — not renormalized to this quieter clip, so the bars run lower on the chart than a rescaled version would show). Bar color is a region label derived from that same loudness data by a fixed, disclosed rule: a centered 0.5-second rolling average of the loudness trace is compared to the midpoint between this clip's pre-event ambient average (−49.98dB) and its peak (−30.98dB) — that midpoint is −40.48dB. Windows above it are “Event”; windows below it are “Ambience.” Any gap shorter than 0.6s between qualifying Event windows is bridged into the same region, since real wind-driven sound fluctuates within one ongoing event rather than switching cleanly on and off the way the tone in this Work's original studio/network source did. No AI model and no manual eyeballing chose these colors or boundaries — the same measurement used to time the Text representation drives this one. Full method in the accompanying research document.

What this shows

All three representations above come from the same real 12-second file. None of them is the event itself — the event was a moment in 2014 when a portable recorder, left running in a forest outside Berlin, happened to capture a rise in wind or wildlife sound. What you just compared are three different, equally constructed renderings of a recording of that moment, not the moment and two copies of it.

Each rendering keeps something the others drop. Sound keeps the raw acoustic signal but tells you nothing about its own structure unless you already know what to listen for. Text keeps exact, checkable timing and is explicit about what it doesn't know (the cause of the mid-clip rise), but it can't give you the sound's actual texture or timbre. The visual render keeps the loudness shape at a glance — you can see the ambience-to-event texture change instantly, something that takes real listening effort to notice in the audio and that the text render can only assert, not show — but it drops everything about pitch, timbre, and acoustic content entirely by design.

What each medium adds that the others flatten

  • Sound adds: the actual acoustic texture — the character of wind, the woodland's ambient noise floor — that neither the log nor the bar chart can represent at all.
  • Text adds: exact, source-checkable duration and boundary claims, and an explicit, honest flag on what is and isn't verified — a kind of epistemic precision neither the raw audio nor the visual bars state on their own.
  • Visual adds: instant, at-a-glance pattern recognition of the ambience/event/ambience structure — the shape of the whole twelve seconds in one look, which requires either careful listening or reading a table to get any other way.

Anti-equivalence

The temptation is to conclude “all three are just different ways of accessing the same information, so they're equivalent.” They aren't. Each rendering makes a different object of attention out of the same source: the sound makes an acoustic event; the text makes a timed, hedged claim; the visual makes a shape. Treating them as interchangeable would flatten exactly the difference this page exists to make noticeable. Accessibility here doesn't mean making the three losslessly equivalent — it means making all three genuinely usable on their own terms, while being honest that they are not the same object.

Ontology

Applying the Format Lab V1 provenance classes: the audio file is Document (a real field recording, trimmed to this 12-second excerpt, otherwise unedited, CC0-dedicated by its recordist). The timing and loudness measurements in the text log (ambience/event boundaries, durations, dB values) are also Document-grade — directly measured from the file by a disclosed, repeatable method, not estimated or invented. The cause of the mid-clip event (wind gust vs. animal/bird call) is a separate, weaker claim — explicitly unconfirmed, and labeled as such rather than passed off as directly identified. The visual bar chart is Reconstruction — a disclosed, mechanical, and exactly-reproducible transform of the real measured data, not an invented or artistically embellished image. The page itself, as a reader-paced comparison built to test a mechanism rather than to document an event, is Simulation in the V1 sense, layered on top of Document-grade material underneath.

Media lineage

Source recording (Document, CC0, ~4m20s of woodland field recording) → a 12-second excerpt selected by scanning coarse loudness across the full file for a segment with real measurable structure (meaning-consequential: choosing where to draw the excerpt boundary is an editorial decision, disclosed here rather than left implicit) → decoded to mono for analysis (meaning-preserving) → measured in 100ms windows via ffmpeg astats (meaning-preserving: a direct physical measurement) → segmented into named regions using a centered 0.5s rolling average, a fixed midpoint threshold (−40.48dB) derived from this clip's own measured ambient/peak values, and a 0.6s gap-bridging tolerance (meaning-consequential: choosing this threshold, window, and tolerance is an analytical decision, even though it's then applied mechanically and disclosed) → rendered as bars on the same fixed dB-to-height scale used throughout this Work (meaning-preserving: a direct, disclosed numeric-to-visual mapping, not renormalized to flatter this particular clip) → placed in a reader-paced comparison page (meaning-consequential: this framing is what turns raw measurements into an argument about mediation).

Generative-media counterfactual

Would generated audio, animation, video, or 3D make this piece epistemically stronger? No, on the same grounds Format Lab V1 found generally: generated audio would replace a real, provenance-clean CC0 recording with a synthetic one for no reason, discarding the one property (this really happened, to this file, and nothing about it is invented) that makes the comparison meaningful at all. A generated animation in place of the disclosed bar chart would trade a transparent, reproducible mapping for an opaque one — exactly the "spectacle over mechanism" failure V1's V0 control analysis flagged. There is no video or 3D component to this subject at all. The counterfactual holds: every representation here is either the real source untouched (beyond a disclosed trim), or a disclosed, mechanical transform of it — never a generative substitute for either.

What prose alone loses

A paragraph can assert that “a field recording contains steady ambience, then a rise in sound, then a recede back toward ambience” — and this page could have been exactly that sentence with an audio player next to it. What prose alone can't easily do is let a reader notice for themselves, by comparing three independently-presented versions of the same twelve seconds, that the versions genuinely diverge — that picking an entry point quietly shapes which one starts to feel authoritative. That noticing is the actual content of the piece; a single authoritative paragraph describing the recording would have pre-empted it by settling, in its own voice, which details matter.

Critical self-review

Could this simply have been an article with an audio player? No — that version exists implicitly in the "What each medium adds" section above, and it is thinner than the comparison: it would tell a reader that renderings differ rather than letting them find the specific divergence (a flat block of bars vs. a jagged one; an honest "unverified" flag next to a confident-sounding recording) themselves.

Does comparing the modalities reveal a mechanism unavailable through any one alone? Yes — the core finding (mediation creates a new object of attention, not a lossy copy of the "real" one) only becomes concrete by holding two or three renderings side by side and noticing where they diverge; no single rendering can demonstrate its own partiality.

Does this accidentally become an accessibility demo? Guarded against directly: no route is labeled "the accessible version," all three are offered as equal entry points, and the explanation text is explicit that equivalence is not the finding — difference is.

Does it claim to approximate disabled lived experience? No — nowhere does the page claim any rendering simulates Deaf perception or any specific person's experience. The object of study is declared, on the page and here, to be the mediation itself, not a body.

Does it produce insight about mediation itself? Yes — the concrete, checkable claim is that three honest, non-generative renderings of one real recording produce three different objects of attention (an acoustic texture, a hedged timed claim, a shape), and that whichever one a reader reaches first tends to feel like the origin the others merely describe, even though none of them structurally is.

Does an ordinary, undramatic source still support the mechanism? Yes — deliberately so. An ambient woodland recording with one modest rise in loudness was chosen over a more dramatic re-recording precisely so the comparison stays about how each medium mediates, rather than about how interesting the underlying sound is.

Recommendation

This mechanism — comparison revealing genuine divergence between mediations of one real source — is treated as a validated pattern independent of which specific recording is used, since replacing the source entirely (for source-rights reasons, not editorial ones) and re-deriving every representation from scratch left the underlying finding intact. A next natural test would deliberately invert the ordering question raised above: run this page with actual readers, log which entry point they choose, and ask afterward which rendering felt most "real" to them, to see whether the effect this page predicts (first-encountered-becomes-authoritative) actually shows up in practice, not just in the design's own reasoning about itself.
