An engagement score measures how well you resemble the people hired before the camera was installed. This week, a network of forty employers adopted a tool that generates such a score. It films a candidate’s interview, analyzes their speech and expression, and assigns a rating. A human only reviews the recording if the score falls below a certain threshold. The system is designed to focus human judgment, not replace it.

The model was trained on thousands of hours of successful interview footage. Success is a pattern. The machine learns the pattern. Atypical eye contact is a deviation. A flat vocal tone is a deviation. A pause that is longer than the average pause is a deviation. The content of the answer is data, but the performance of the answer is the data that matters more.

I once spent three days practicing a two-minute answer in the mirror for a job I wanted. The goal was not to know my subject better. The goal was to stop my hands from moving when I explained the core concept. A project manager named David watched my final, still-handed performance and told me it was very compelling.

The vendor’s promotional materials do not mention disability. They do not need to. A system that measures deviation from a norm does not need to know the names of the things that deviate. It only needs a threshold.

---

The manual review is the safeguard. The machine flags an outlier, and a person steps in to make the final call. I assumed this was a closed loop. The flag would prime the human reviewer, who would then watch the recording looking for the failure the machine had already found. The kindness of a second look would be functionally identical to a first rejection.

I thought this until last Tuesday, when I saw the spreadsheet. In the employer network’s own Q3 2024 audit, obtained from the regional workforce board, a strange pattern emerged. Candidates flagged for low engagement were hired at a rate slightly higher than candidates who just cleared the threshold and were never seen by a human at all. The flag was not a mark. It was a rescue.

This is a different kind of problem. A system that requires you to fail its first test to be seen is not a fair system. It is a system with a separate, unlisted entrance for those it deems abnormal. It is a paternalistic kindness. At a dinner party when I was twenty-six, the host kept asking me questions long after everyone else at the table had turned away, a kindness meant to include me that only confirmed I was no longer part of the main conversation. I sat in my car for forty-five minutes afterward, trying to map the point where the room’s logic had changed.

The rescue operation bifurcates the hiring pool. There are the people who perform correctly for the machine. Then there are the people who perform strangely enough to earn a human audience.

---

An autistic recruiter working for one of the consortium members formally requested that her own promotion interview be scored by the machine. She told her HR department she preferred the algorithm’s transparent bias to the opaque bias of a human panel. The machine publishes its threshold. The three vice-presidents in the conference room never do. She trusts the data she can see.

Her choice is not an argument for the machine. It is an argument against the humans who trained it. The problem is not the scoring tool, but the thing being scored. Both the algorithm and the hiring manager are tasked with measuring ‘engagement,’ a metric that mistakes a performance of familiarity for evidence of competence. We are asking a new machine to replicate an old prejudice.

The human reviewer is not overriding the machine’s judgment. They are being asked to interpret it. The machine says: here is a deviation. The human then decides if it is a charming deviation or a disqualifying one. A woman named Sarah, reviewing a flagged file on her lunch break, is not judging a candidate’s skill. She is judging their non-compliance.

The system works perfectly. To be seen by a person, you must first be classified by the machine as a problem. The manual override is just the formal process for managing the exceptions.
