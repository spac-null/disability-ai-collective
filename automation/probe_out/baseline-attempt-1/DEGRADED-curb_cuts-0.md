A petition is not a survey. It counts only the people who already knew there was a form to sign, and who had the time to sign it. It is a show of hands from those already in the room.

This week, my city council voted to remove three accessible parking bays downtown. They will be replaced by a protected bike lane, the result of a petition that gathered 1,200 signatures. A transport lead from the council called it a ‘genuinely difficult trade-off’.

I pulled the council’s records from the March 14 planning session. I was looking for the usual trick: a decision made about a group of people without ever asking them. I expected to find silence, a consultation where one side never got the invitation.

The truth was harder. The accessible-bay users had been notified. One of them spoke at the meeting. They did not lose because the process was rigged. They lost because they were outnumbered.

---

Four hundred metres is not a number on a planning document. It is the distance between the new, relocated accessible bay and the transit interchange. For two blocks of that route, there is no dropped kerb.

My father drove a municipal bus for twenty-two years, and he could tell you the grade of a street by the sound of his engine. I measure a city by the burn in my shoulders. The official detour adds another 150 metres to avoid the high kerbs. That is a tax, paid in energy, that the planning department will never have to budget for.

The easy analysis is to call this an information problem. Better signage, a clearer map, a wayfinding app. This assumes the problem is navigation. But a map of a broken system is just a confirmation that the system is broken.

Then I found Deborah Antwi’s name in the meeting minutes. She is a wheelchair user. She co-signed the petition to build the bike lane. She told the council a protected lane keeps her chair off a road where cars had already clipped her twice. She would take the 400-metre detour, she said, for that safety. Access is not one thing.

---

The language of ‘difficult trade-offs’ is a way to make a political choice feel like a law of physics. It presents two legitimate needs as a zero-sum game that the city is regretfully forced to referee. The unasked question is why the design brief had to be zero-sum in the first place.

Why was the only option a bike lane that erased a parking bay? A shared route, designed from the start to accommodate both, was never on the table. One group gets a new piece of infrastructure, built to the latest standard. The other group gets sent back into the old city, the one of cracked pavement and unlevelled crossings.

This is not a failure of information. It is a failure of imagination. At my best friend’s wedding, the venue had three steps. Six people offered to carry me up. It was a performance of help that served only to underline the architectural failure they had all agreed to ignore. I smiled, because it is easier to absorb the cost yourself than to name it out loud.

The council’s statement has the same smile. It expresses regret while ratifying the original exclusion. It makes the people who have to live with the bad design responsible for being gracious about it.

The new bike lane has smooth asphalt and clean white lines. The detour follows the old sidewalk, where the tree roots are winning.
