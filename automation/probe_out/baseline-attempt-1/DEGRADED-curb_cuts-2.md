A petition is not a survey. It counts only the people who already knew there was a form to sign. In the city council records, the form has 1,200 signatures, and so three accessible parking bays on Exchange Street are gone. In their place is a protected bike lane. The nearest accessible bay is now 400 metres from the transit interchange, not 40.

This is the kind of decision I have seen a hundred times. I know the mechanics of it before I read the details. The consultation notice is a PDF, scanned crooked, unreadable by the software that speaks the screen aloud. The public meeting is at six in the evening, up a flight of stairs, in a room where the hearing loop is broken. My friend once spent twenty minutes trying to zoom in on a badly scanned floor plan on her phone, trying to see if a proposed cafe redesign had left her a path to the toilet. The plan was a grey blur; she gave up. This is how exclusion becomes policy. It arrives looking like due process.

So I pulled the minutes from the March 14 planning session, expecting to expose the trick. I found something harder. The accessible-bay users had, in fact, been notified. One of them spoke, her testimony recorded in three flat sentences. The council members thanked her for her contribution. There was no trick. There was a fight, and the wheelchair users lost on turnout. A petition with 1,200 names has a different weight than one person in a room. A democracy of signatures is a democracy of the organized, the networked, the people with time to stand on a corner with a clipboard.

Then I found the transcript of Deborah Antwi. She is a wheelchair user. She was also a co-signer of the bike lane petition. She told the council she would happily take the 400-metre detour. She’d been clipped by wing mirrors twice on that road, she said, and a protected lane keeps her chair out of the flow of traffic. An access claim that cuts directly against another access claim. One person’s safety is another’s exclusion. Her body, her history with that specific street, produced a different calculus of risk.

The problem is not a lack of information. It is not a matter of better signage or a map of dropped kerbs. To frame it that way is to pretend the street is a neutral grid where data points can be rearranged for a better outcome. A street is a social agreement, made physical in asphalt and paint. My father drove the same municipal bus route for twenty-two years. He knew the dips and cracks in the road not from a report, but from the way the steering wheel felt in his hands at 5 a.m.

When you rebuild a street for one kind of body, you don’t just add an asset. You re-write the agreement. The cyclist becomes the intended user. Everyone else becomes a guest, navigating a space made for someone else. We are told this is a trade-off, a complex balancing of needs. The council’s transport lead called it a "genuinely difficult" choice.

That is the language of careful administration. It is also the language of the person who sees you at the bottom of three steps and says, with real sympathy, how sorry they are about the venue. It makes their failure to plan sound like a thoughtful, philosophical dilemma. You learn to smile, a quick, efficient smile that makes it easy for them. You thank them for the offer to help carry you. The grace is public. The cost is private. You pay it later, when you can’t stop shaking.

A route rebuilt from scratch for one constituency is not the same as a shared route optimized so no one is demoted to atmosphere. The bike lane is the first thing. The 400-metre detour is the second. This story is about the second thing failing, not the first succeeding. It is about whose body is expected to absorb the cost of a civic improvement. The extra distance, the missing kerb cut, the longer push in the rain—these are not data points. They are the invoice.

On my way home from the library, I roll past a section of pavement buckled by a plane tree. The concrete is cracked into a dozen small plates, pushed up by a root as thick as my arm. It is completely impassable. It is a failure of public maintenance. The tree is winning.
