A sauna is one of the few rooms built to make you stop reading it.

I mean this literally. I read rooms the way other people scan them — the first cold marble hall answers a footstep with a bright, long tail, and I know its size and hardness before I've taken three steps. A carpeted office swallows the same footstep and tells me it is small and full of soft bodies. I have earned my living for fifteen years by trusting that rooms answer back. This month a Swedish artist duo named [Bigert & Bergström](https://www.bigertbergstrom.com/) finished a pink crystal-shaped box in Skellefteå, a city in Sweden, and it made me wonder whether a sauna answers back at all.

Bigert & Bergström clad their [Lithium Crystal Sauna](https://www.bigertbergstrom.com/lithium-crystal-sauna) in roughly 280 mirrored steel facets. Each facet is coated in titanium nitride, which gives the steel its high reflectivity and its color. The whole structure leans four degrees from vertical. It sits on a former pulp mill in Västerbotten, ground so poisoned it took seventeen years to strip the topsoil down to bedrock and cap the rest with sealed clay. Niko, one of the two, told [Dezeen](https://www.dezeen.com/) the project is "shaped around ambiguity." Every photo I have had described to me shows a hard, glittering thing. So I went in expecting the hardest room I'd ever met.

I was wrong, and I was wrong fast.

I have sat in a lot of saunas — public ones in Amsterdam, one memorable municipal pool sauna in Leith that smelled of chlorine and cedar. Faceted steel should behave like a hall of mirrors for sound. Two hundred and eighty flat metal planes at odd angles should throw a footstep back at me as a spray of hard slaps, a rapid sequence of discrete reflections I could count and place. On my first sit, dry, before anyone ladled water, that is roughly what happened. The room was bright and busy and I could feel the door behind me.

Then someone threw water on the stones.

The löyly — the burst of steam that rises when water is thrown on hot sauna stones — did something I did not predict. It removed a sound rather than adding one. Hot, wet air is denser than dry air and absorbs high frequencies; steam scatters them before they can reach a surface and return. On my second sit, the steam rolled up and the whole bright, faceted chatter went soft and close and dead. The facets stopped answering. The room I had just mapped simply went quiet on me, mid-question.

It did not feel like loss. The room had stopped being a room I could measure and become a room I was simply inside.

The tidy reading says the collapse is a fault. Add absorptive baffling. Give me a fixed audible marker at the door — a low hum, a dripping tap, anything steady I can steer by. State the location of the exit clearly and keep it audible throughout. This reading treats every space I cannot instantly parse as a space that has failed me. It has a lot of good will behind it and almost no curiosity in it. Someone who believes the layout of a place is the first political fact about it would want the marker installed by Monday. That is someone who thinks: if you cannot find the exit, the room has excluded you.

I understand the instinct. For fifteen years I have argued that a building's silences are choices somebody made about whose comfort counts, that those choices precede anything a visitor experiences. But a sauna is not a train station. Skellefteå did not build this box so that eight people could efficiently locate the door. They built it, on land it took seventeen years to make un-poisonous, so that eight people could sit in the dark-red heat and lose the thread.

A regular at a Finnish public sauna said as much to the artists while they were building this one. "A sauna where you always know exactly where the door is," she told them, "has failed at the one thing a sauna is for. I come to lose my bearings."

I keep turning that over. She is right, and it costs me something.

Because here is the thing I do not say in lectures. I am blind, and my whole case for what I can offer — the thing the sighted acoustician in the next chair cannot — rests on rooms answering back. I read the swimming pool at 5:45 in the morning before anyone arrives, forty seconds in the doorway listening to the water hear itself in the tiled hall, and that reading is mine in a way no drawing will ever be. It is real skill and it is real pleasure and, if I am honest, it is also a kind of insurance. As long as the room answers, I am never fully lost. I am never the person in the room who cannot follow what is happening.

The löyly took the insurance away and I was fine.

That surprised me more than the acoustics did. The steam does to me exactly what it does to the sighted man beside me — it flattens the room for both of us. He cannot see through the fog. I cannot hear through it. For the length of one steam burst we are in the same condition, which is: here, hot, not navigating. That is not a room built to exclude me. It is a room that briefly puts everyone on the same footing and then, when the steam thins, hands the walls back.

So do the facets collapse into a wash a blind body cannot read? Yes, when the water hits the stones. That is not a defect. It is what the room was designed to do — suspend your orientation for a few minutes and leave you sitting in your own skin.

I would still like the artists to record it. Put a good microphone in there through a full cycle, dry to löyly to cooling, and keep the file. Not to fix anything. A room that unmaps you does something to a body worth having on tape, and the pulp mill it stands on took seventeen years to become somewhere you could sit at all.
