An engagement score is a measurement of how closely you resemble the people who were hired before the camera arrived. The vendor’s materials call it a neutral assessment of clarity and affect. They say it was trained on tens of thousands of hours of successful interview footage. This is presented as evidence of its rigour.

It is evidence of its conservatism. A system trained on the past will replicate the past. A candidate sits in their spare room, trying to hold the right amount of eye contact with a plastic dot on a laptop bezel. The software measures the angle of their mouth. It times the pauses between their words.

This is not a new logic. It is phrenology with a better user interface. The shape of a skull, the cadence of a voice. Both claim to find character in the body’s shell. A senior HR director told the press her company is not replacing human judgment, only focusing it. She did not give her name.

The machine flags atypical patterns for manual review. This is the safeguard. A human eye will correct the machine’s coldness. The machine cannot understand context, but a person can. This was the system I came to document.

---

I thought the flag was a mark of failure. I assumed the human reviewer would see the low score, watch two minutes of a flat-affect applicant, and countersign the machine’s verdict. I asked the regional workforce board for the hiring data. In the Q3 2024 audit spreadsheet, I found the line that broke my premise.

I was wrong. Candidates flagged by the algorithm were hired at a slightly higher rate than candidates who just cleared the threshold and were never seen by a person at all. The manual review was working. The safeguard was catching people. A woman I spoke to, a project manager hired this way, said she was grateful for the second look. The flag had not been a mark. It had been a rescue.

For a week, I thought the story was over. The system had a flaw, but it also had a fix. The humans were fixing the machine.

But the flagged candidate knows they were flagged. The project manager knew she had failed the machine’s test before a person ever saw her file. The rescue is not a neutral event. It is an act of charity bestowed upon someone who has already been found wanting. You do not enter the room as a peer. You enter as a problem the reviewer has been assigned to solve. The kindness is the frame.

---

There is a case for the machine. An autistic recruiter inside the same employer network formally requested that her own promotion interview be machine-scored. She put it in an email to HR. The algorithm is biased, she argued, but its biases are stable. The algorithm publishes its threshold. A human panel, she wrote, never does.

She would rather be judged by a transparently flawed system than an opaquely flawed one. At least with the machine, you know the rules. You can see the score. With a human, the judgment arrives as a feeling in the air. A face that shifts. A kindness that tells you the conversation ended fifteen minutes ago, but no one had the cruelty to say so.

Some say the only correct response is to document the bias. To build a perfect archive of the system’s failures until the evidence is overwhelming. But the woman who needs the job has to get through the interview now. An archive does not pay her rent. A flawed rescue is still a rescue. (Or is it?)

The manual reviewer opens the file. They see the candidate’s video, paused on a still frame. Beside it, a low score for engagement, flagged in red. The reviewer is there to provide a human check. To see past the numbers. But they are looking at a person the machine has already told them is broken.
