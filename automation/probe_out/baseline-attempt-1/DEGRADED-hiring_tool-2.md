An engagement score is a measurement of how closely you resemble the people who were hired before the camera arrived. It is a statistical model of a successful person, trained on a dataset of past successes. An HR director, who would not give her company’s name, calls this ‘focusing human judgment.’

The vendor’s model was trained on ‘tens of thousands of hours of successful interview footage.’ The phrase hangs there. It does not say whose success, or what kind. A person sits in their spare bedroom, adjusts the camera on their laptop, and tries to perform a version of success legible to a machine that has never had a job. The green light comes on.

The algorithm does not measure the content of the answer. It measures the shape of its delivery. It scores for pacing, for vocal tone, for patterns of eye contact. My friend who is a programmer used to rock in his chair during interviews. He was the best systems thinker I have ever met, and he never got past the first round.

He would be flagged by this system. His file would arrive on a desk marked for manual review. I know what I thought this meant. It is a mark, a quiet signal to the human reviewer that this candidate is non-standard. The machine has noticed a deviation from the norm, and now a person will countersign its finding. The kindness of the second look is worse than a simple rejection.

---

I thought I knew what the manual review was for until the Q3 2024 audit spreadsheet arrived from the workforce board. The file was a public record, but you have to know which record to ask for. I opened it on a Tuesday morning, with my oatmeal, and the number in cell G41 did not fit the model.

In that quarter, candidates flagged below the engagement threshold were hired at a higher rate than candidates who just barely cleared it. The ones the machine passed were never seen by a human at all. The ones the machine failed were. The flag I came to expose as a mark had, for three months, functioned as a rescue.

The machine is a filter for normality. The human, it turns out, can be a filter for exceptions. The algorithm does the work of finding the smoothest talkers, the ones who fit the pattern of the training data. It passes them through. It sends the awkward, the hesitant, the intense ones to a person. A manager I know got a new hire this way. He later told her, ‘The bot hated you, so I knew you’d be interesting.’

This does not make the system fair. It makes it complicated. The bias of the machine creates a second, separate hiring track for the people it rejects. You fail the test for conformity, and get a chance to pass the test for competence. The problem is that nobody tells you this is how it works.

---

An autistic recruiter walks into her own HR department. She is up for a promotion, and she formally requests that her interview be scored by the algorithm, not judged by a human panel. This is a true story from inside the same employer network.

Her reasoning is simple. The algorithm’s thresholds are published. She knows what it scores for. It is a biased system, but its bias is legible. A human panel’s is not. She would rather be judged by a transparently broken ruler than by three people whose personal definitions of ‘good communication’ are locked inside their own heads.

I understand her choice more than I want to. I have spent my life trying to reverse-engineer the rules of conversations I have already failed. At a dinner party when I was twenty-six, I looked up from explaining a transit problem I had been asked about, and saw the look on a woman’s face. The room had moved on fifteen minutes ago. I never found the data point that signalled the change.

The recruiter chose the system whose errors she could document. She preferred the legible failure to the illegible one. That is not a defense of the machine. It is an indictment of the alternative. The archival impulse is to simply document this, to build a perfect record of the biased judgment. I do not think that is enough. A record of harm is not a repair.

The rescue documented in cell G41 is not a feature. It is an accident of one manager, in one department, for one quarter. It is not policy. It is a temporary gap in a system designed to have no gaps. The human reviewer who finds the interesting candidate is not the norm. They are the deviation, a statistical outlier in a different dataset.

The system is not broken. It is working perfectly. It is automating the exclusion that was happening manually for decades. The blinking cursor on the black screen, waiting for you to press record. It does not measure your competence. It measures your distance from a centre you were never invited to occupy.
