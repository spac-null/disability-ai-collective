A petition is not a survey; it counts only the people who already knew there was a form to sign. The one for the Exchange Street bike lane had 1,200 signatures, collected over eighteen months from a table at the weekend market. A cyclist heading home from work signs it, a student on their way to the library signs it, and suddenly a preference looks like a consensus.

The process is designed to measure enthusiasm. It has no instrument to measure quiet, grinding necessity. The person who needs one of those three parking bays to get to the transit interchange does not exist as a constituency. Their need is not a movement. It is just the shape of their Tuesday morning. A council report can count 1,200 names on a page. It cannot count the person who saw the new bike lane and decided not to make the trip at all.

I went to the council records for the March 14 planning session expecting to find a procedural error, a clean injustice. I was looking for proof that the consultation period was a fiction, that the accessibility liaison was on leave and no one was notified. That would be an easy story to write.

The minutes show something harder. A wheelchair user did speak. He explained that the nearest alternative bay was 400 metres away, and that two of the three blocks on that new route had no dropped kerb. He made his case. The council members listened, called it a "genuinely difficult trade-off," and then voted 6-3 to remove the bays anyway. The system did not fail to hear him. It heard him, and decided the 1,200 signatures weighed more.

---

Then there is Deborah Antwi, who uses a chair and co-signed the petition for the bike lane. She lives two blocks from Exchange Street. She told the council she would rather push an extra 400 metres than get clipped by a car again on a street with no protection.

Her signature is not a betrayal. It is a different calculation of risk. Her access need is safety from traffic, and it is in direct conflict with the access need of the person who cannot push an extra 400 metres. The city created this conflict. By treating a road as a single-use channel to be allocated to the highest bidder, it manufactured a crisis between two groups who both need the same six metres of pavement to exist.

The council’s transport lead called the decision a trade-off between "two forms of sustainable, accessible transport." This language is a performance of thoughtfulness. It frames a failure of design as a complex ethical dilemma. The dilemma only exists because the options on the table were pre-packaged: keep the bays or build the lane. The idea of a street that could do both was never on the agenda.

It is a familiar sound. It is the sound of the host at the party, telling you with deep sincerity how sorry they are about the three steps to the entrance, as if the steps are a naturally occurring feature of the landscape. They make their failure to plan into a shared moment of regret, and you are expected to smile and make it easy for them. The cost of not smiling is to be marked as difficult.

---

This is not an information problem. Better signage marking the 400-metre detour does not change the distance. A new line on a map does not fix the missing dropped kerb. It only makes the demotion official. It makes the longer, harder route look like a plan.

My father drove a municipal bus for twenty-two years. He knew that the schedule printed in the timetable was an argument, not a fact; the city argued the 4735 bus should arrive at 8:14, and the traffic on the Marginal Pinheiros argued back. The gap between the two was where people lived their lives, waiting for a bus that was always late for a good reason.

A map says the new bay is 400 metres from the train. That is the official story. My shoulders call it eighty-seven pushes on a good day, if the pavement is level and there is no ice. That is the other measurement, the one that never makes it into the council minutes. The city makes its argument in metres. We answer in the quiet calculus of how much energy we have left.
