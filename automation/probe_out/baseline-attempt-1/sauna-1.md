A sauna is one of the few rooms built to make you stop reading it.

Most rooms want to be understood. A church at noon tells you its size before you take a step — the reflections come back long and thick off the stone, and you know without touching anything that the ceiling is far away. A tiled pool at 5:45 in the morning tells you it is empty by how the water hears itself. I go stand in that doorway. Not to swim. To listen to a room that has nobody in it yet, which is a specific and greedy pleasure.

This week Swedish artists [Bigert & Bergström](https://www.bigertbergstrom.com/) finished a pink sauna near Skellefteå, a town in northern Sweden. It is shaped like a crystal, clad in roughly 280 mirrored steel facets, leaning four degrees back from vertical. It sits on a former pulp mill that took seventeen years to clean, down to bedrock in places. The whole thing is designed to catch light. I do not care about the light. I wanted to know what 280 hard steel faces do to sound.

I went in — a smaller mirrored steam room in Amsterdam, not the Swedish one, close enough in shape — expecting the facets to break the sound into hard, fast slap-echo I could parse. Angled panels usually do that. They throw reflections back at slightly wrong times. A wrong time is information. I can walk a room on wrong times. I sat down on the second bench and mapped the door by ear in about four seconds, pleased with myself.

Then someone threw water on the stones.

The löyly — a Finnish sauna term for the burst of steam off the hot rocks — came up and swallowed every reflection into a soft, dead close. The room went quiet in a way that had nothing to do with loudness. The geometry I had just mapped stopped answering. I threw my usual questions at the walls and got nothing back. Vapour is thick with water, and water eats the high frequencies I use to read edges. The steel was still there, still angled, still four degrees off true. But it had gone mute under the wet.

I sat there genuinely surprised, which does not happen often in rooms.

There is a tidy way to describe what happened next: the room failed me. A blind body could not navigate it. Fix it. Add absorptive baffling somewhere it won't cook, put a fixed audible reference at the door — a small fan, a dripping tap, anything steady — so a person can always find the way out by ear. This is a real position, and a sensible one, and I understand why someone would hold it. A room you cannot leave by ear is not a safe room.

But I keep landing somewhere else. Recording what the collapse does to a body might matter more than repairing it. A room built to unmap you is not obviously broken.

Niko, one of the two artists, told [Dezeen](https://www.dezeen.com/) — an architecture and design publication — that the project is "shaped around ambiguity." I read that and rolled my eyes at first — it is the kind of line that gets said about a lot of pink buildings. Then I sat in the wet dead quiet and thought: no, they mean it, and they built it into the physics without maybe knowing that is what they did. The steam does the ambiguity. The steel is just theatre for the eyes.

Here is the part I cannot make fit. A regular at a Finnish public sauna told the artists during the build that she comes precisely to lose her bearings. A sauna where you always know exactly where the door is, she said, has failed at the one thing a sauna is for. I want to argue with her and I cannot. She is describing, from inside sighted experience, the exact thing I feel as an ambush — and calling it the point.

So I have to sit with the possibility that the collapse is not a defect I detect and other people miss. It is the design working, and I am simply the person who notices the mechanism because I run everything through it.

I want to be exact about what is lost, because this is where sighted people get lazy about blindness. They assume losing the read of a room is my special problem, a thing that happens only to me. It is not. In that steam, the sighted person beside me lost the room too. The mirrors gave them back a warped, split, unreliable version of themselves — a body chopped into 280 facets, none of them lining up. They could not read the room by eye any better than I could read it by ear. We were both un-mapped. For once, at the same time, by the same room.

That is rarer than it sounds. I spend most of my life in spaces where I am the only one who cannot get the free continuous read everyone else takes for granted. Here the room took it from everyone. There is a grim comedy in a building achieving universal access by removing everyone's grip at once.

And there is the thing underneath, which I will name and then leave. Years ago someone I loved was crying in a room and told me, gently, that she needed someone who could see her face. She was right. There are reads I cannot give. I have spent a long time building a case for the reads only I can give, and the case is good, and it does not touch that afternoon. The sauna does something I did not expect it to do. It puts the sighted person in the room where a face is unreadable, where the mirror gives back nothing you can use, where you are down to the wet air on your skin and the voice next to you and no idea where the door is. It does not fix anything. It just, for a few minutes, stops sorting people into the ones who can read the room and the ones who cannot.

The steam still hangs in the corners after everyone leaves. Nobody in there is reading it either.
