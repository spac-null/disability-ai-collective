An engagement score measures how closely you resemble the people who were hired before the camera arrived. Specifically, it rates how similar your interview behavior is to candidates who were already hired. That is the whole mechanism. This week forty regional employers announced they had bought one — a tool that records your first interview, scores it for "engagement," "clarity of speech," and "positive affect," and hands the composite to a manager before any human watches the tape.

The vendor says the model learned from tens of thousands of hours of "successful interview footage." Successful means the person got hired. So the machine is a portrait of everyone who already passed. It rewards the candidate who most resembles them and flags the one who does not.

I know what a flag feels like from the inside. When I answer a question I have been asked, I answer it fully, with evidence, and I do not always notice the moment the room stops wanting the evidence. A camera scoring "engagement" is scoring that gap between what I said and how a panel wanted me to say it. It does not read the transit-network answer. It reads the pause before I look up.

I went into this certain the flag was a mark. Not a second chance — a stain that primes the next hand to touch it. A manager who opens a file already labelled "below threshold" (meaning the candidate scored lower than the system's cutoff for moving forward) is not neutral. She is reviewing a decision, not making one.

Then I got the numbers. In the employer network's third-quarter audit for 2024, obtained through a records request to the workforce board — the public agency that tracks regional hiring and job training — candidates flagged below the engagement threshold were hired at a higher rate than candidates who cleared it narrowly and were never seen by a human at all. The flag pulled you in front of a person. Clearing the bar by a whisker meant the machine's yes was the only yes you got, and a machine's yes to a low-but-passing score is a shrug. The mark, in at least one quarter, was a rescue.

I sat with that spreadsheet longer than I want to admit. The pattern I had come to expose was doing the opposite of what I expected.

Here is what I think is happening, and it is not comforting either. The flag works as a rescue only for the people a manager already wants to rescue. A human who opens a flagged file brings her own portrait of a good hire. If the candidate reads as promising in every other line — the right school, the warm reference, a name that sounds like her own — the flag becomes a story about nerves, and she overrides it. If the candidate reads as marginal already, the flag confirms what she suspected. The machine did not decide. It handed the outcome to the manager, and the manager supplied the name.

So the question the flag answers is not "is this person engaging." It is "does the reviewer already like this person enough to argue with a computer on their behalf." For most autistic candidates, the honest answer is no, and the flag countersigns the no. For the well-connected candidate having an off day, the flag is a courtesy call.

There is a woman inside this same employer network who ruined my clean version of this. She is an autistic recruiter — autistic people often have different ways of showing emotion or maintaining eye contact in interviews, traits the system may score as "low engagement" even though they do not affect job performance. Last year she asked HR, on the record, to have her own promotion interview machine-scored instead of judged by a panel. She wanted the algorithm. Her reasoning was clear and correct on two points. First, the tool publishes its threshold — you know the number it applied to you. Second, a human panel reading her still face and even voice never publishes anything. A panel decides she is "cold" and files no evidence.

The machine at least tells her the number it hit her with. She would rather be marked by something that shows its working than dismissed by something that does not.

She is not wrong. A panel of humans reading affect is also a machine trained on successful interview footage — it has just never had to say so.

Which brings me to the response I keep hearing from people whose instincts I trust: if the instrument is biased, force it to keep a complete, unedited record of every score it renders, and let the record be the fight. Document everything. Make the pipeline show its work. I understand the appeal. I have spent my life arguing that sensory input is data and the failure to log it is the error.

But documentation assumes there is a moment downstream where the record gets read by someone with the power and the will to reverse the machine. The last three scoring systems I looked at — this one, and two hiring tools in other industries studied by [Ifeoma Ajunwa](https://www.law.ucla.edu/faculty/ifeoma-ajunwa), a researcher at UCLA whose work on "automated hiring" shows how algorithmic bias affects employment — all showed the same thing. When managers reviewed the machine's flagged candidates, they approved the machine's decisions more often than they overrode them. A perfect record of a decision nobody overturns is an archive of harm with better metadata. I refuse to accept that as the ceiling, but I have stopped pretending the record alone repairs anything.

None of the forty employers' public materials mention disability, or an appeals process, or what happens to the audio when the machine keeps the tape. One HR director, speaking only if her company stayed unnamed, said: "We're not replacing human judgment, we're focusing it." Focusing is exactly what the flag does. It aims a human's existing judgment at the candidate.

I re-sort my desk drawer every Tuesday because the pens migrate overnight and the order matters to me and to no one else. It is the kind of thing a camera would score as low affect and high rigidity, and it is also the reason I read the spreadsheet three times instead of once. The audit said the flag saved people. But the saving had a guest list, and I was not on it. Neither was the recruiter who asked for the machine because at least it counts.
