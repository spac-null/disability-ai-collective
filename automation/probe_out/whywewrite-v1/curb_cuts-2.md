A petition is not a survey. It counts only the people who already knew there was a form to sign.

On Tuesday the city council voted six to three to pull three accessible parking bays off Exchange Street and lay a protected bike lane where they used to be. The bays sat forty metres from the transit interchange. The nearest one now sits four hundred. Two of the three blocks between the new bay and the trains have no dropped kerb, so the marked wheelchair route swings an extra hundred and fifty metres around them. The cyclists' petition ran eighteen months and gathered twelve hundred names. The council's accessibility liaison was on leave the whole review and the post is still empty.

I read that last fact and thought I had the story. A form was passed around a room, and only one kind of body was in the room to sign it. That is the oldest trick in planning, and I have written about it for ten years. So I went looking for the room.

I pulled the consultation record from the March 14 planning session, expecting silence. It was not silent. The accessible-bay users had been notified. One of them had come and spoken. The bike lane group brought thirty people to a Tuesday evening meeting; the disabled residents brought one. The wheelchair users did not lose because they were locked out. They lost on turnout. That is a worse problem than a rigged form, because you cannot fix it by fixing the form.

Here is what thirty people looks like. A cycling group can put out a call on Monday and fill a church hall by Thursday, because their members can get to the church hall. My father drove the 4735 for twenty-two years; he knew that a nine a.m. meeting is a survey of who can be somewhere at nine a.m. Most of the people who need those four bays cannot casually turn up to a weeknight session downtown to defend the four bays. The bays are the precondition for showing up to the fight about the bays.

[Deborah Antwi](https://www.deborah-antwi.com), a wheelchair user, had signed the cyclists' petition. She came to the council meeting to speak. She told the council she backed the bike lane and would take the four-hundred-metre detour. A protected lane keeps her chair off a road where she has already been clipped twice. She told the meeting, "I would rather roll further on a road that isn't trying to kill me than roll close on one that already has, twice." So here was a disabled resident using an access claim to knock down an access claim, and she was not confused, and she was not a plant. She had done the arithmetic on her own body and come out the other side.

There is a way of reading this whole story as a problem of missing infrastructure. Fix the dropped kerbs. Put up clear signage. The council's own report points to the missing kerbs, the detour, and a future phase with no date. Get those things right and the harm shrinks.

That is real, and it is the second problem. A route rebuilt from scratch for cyclists does not work the same way as a route designed so that neither group gets pushed to the margins. Those are different engineering outcomes. Whether you can physically finish a trip is one question. Whether the planners counted your trip when they drew the route is another. Install every kerb ramp on the detour and the disabled resident's journey is still four hundred metres longer than it was before the lane went in. The council's transport lead called it "a genuinely difficult trade-off between two forms of sustainable, accessible transport." One group sent thirty people to make that trade-off. The other sent two.

I know the shape of the smaller thing that happens next, and I have never written about it. A few years ago I went to a friend's wedding in a venue with three steps. Everyone had known about the steps for months. Nobody said anything until I arrived, and then six people offered to carry me, and I let them, and I smiled, and I thanked them, and I made it easy. That night I could not stop shaking, and it was not about the steps. It was about how fast I smiled. Deborah taking the detour and saying it was fine is not the same as my wedding. But I recognise the reflex to make the failure of planning look like a personal choice you made gracefully. The council report will file her line under consensus. It was not consensus. It was one person's arithmetic, and it does not license anyone else's.

So was this rigged? No. That is what I went in believing and it did not survive the consultation record. The bays did not vanish because a bureaucrat asked a question only cyclists could answer. They vanished because a petition rewards the people who can already gather, and gathering is exactly the thing the missing bays make hard. The form worked as designed. The design counts hands in a room, and getting to the room was the whole contest.

The cycling group I do not fault. They organised, they turned up, they won. That is politics working. The empty liaison chair is not a scandal of malice; it is a scandal of nobody minding the shop. A petition that draws twelve hundred names over eighteen months looks like overwhelming demand. It is demand from the people who could reach the pen while the pen was being passed around.

The new bay is coming "as part of a future phase." There is no date. On Exchange Street this morning the three old bay markings are still painted on the road, faded but there, forty metres from the trains, waiting for someone to paint over them.
