An engagement score measures how much you look like the people who got hired before the camera showed up. This week a group of forty regional employers turned on a tool that records your first interview, scores it for three qualities — engagement, clarity of speech, and positive affect — then hands a human a single number before that human watches a second of you. The vendor says the model learned from tens of thousands of hours of "successful interview footage." Successful means the people who were already hired. It means the shape of a face and a voice that a room full of hiring managers has already decided it likes.

I read the vendor's materials on a Tuesday, oatmeal going cold, and I felt the old thing arrive in my chest before I had a word for it. The eye contact that scores well is the eye contact I do not do. The pacing that scores well is not my pacing. I do not modulate my voice up at the ends of sentences to signal that I am pleased to be here. I am often pleased to be here. The signal is missing.

So I set out to prove what I was sure I already knew. The flag was a brand. Below-threshold files get sent for human review, and the human who picks up a reviewed file does not overturn a machine. They countersign it. A doctor once asked an albino man I interviewed when he "became" albino. That is what authority looks like when it has less information than the person in front of it and the power anyway. I expected the reviewers to be that doctor.

Then I got the numbers.

I filed a records request with the [workforce board](https://www.doleta.gov/workforce/) for the network's Q3 2024 hiring audit. It came back as a spreadsheet. I sorted it by outcome. The engagement score ranges from low to high, with higher scores meaning the machine rated the candidate's interview more favorably. Candidates below the engagement threshold — those who scored low and were sent to a human for review — were hired at a higher rate than candidates who cleared the threshold narrowly and were never sent to a human at all. In that quarter the flag was not a disqualifier. It was a second look. The people the machine disliked got a second pair of eyes, and the second pair of eyes liked several of them enough to hire them. The people the machine mildly approved of sailed through unseen and unhired.

I sat with that for most of a morning. My tidy case was wrong in exactly the way I most enjoy being wrong, which is the way that means the world is more complicated than my model of it.

I want to be careful about what the spreadsheet actually says. It is one quarter and one network, and I have watched three systems in the last two years where the reviewing hand simply approved the machine's error and moved on to the next task. The flag produces no second look if the human treats it as confirmation. There is also a quieter problem in the design that the good quarter does not fix. The candidates who cleared the threshold and were never seen are invisible. Nobody counts them. Nobody appeals for them. The network published no appeals process at all. A person the model under-scored just above the threshold line gets no review. They get an automated thank-you and silence.

A recruiter inside this same employer network, autistic, formally asked HR to have her own promotion interview scored by the machine instead of judged by a panel. On the record. "The algorithm publishes its threshold," she told me. "You can see the number. A panel decides in the corridor that you seemed a bit off, and there's no line in any spreadsheet you can file a request for. I'd rather be marked by something that shows its work." She is defending the camera from inside the exact group it flags. I do not think she is naive. I think she has simply run the two options and picked the one with visible data.

That is the part that undoes the clean archival answer, the one that says the fix for a biased instrument is a complete unedited record of every call it makes. Document everything, and the bias becomes contestable. I believe in that. I have filed the requests. But a record does not change the hand that touches it next. The Q3 spreadsheet is exactly the kind of record the archival position wants, and it exists only because I asked for it, months late, about a quarter already closed. Nobody inside the network was reading it as a rescue. The document survived contact with the pipeline. The people did not read the document. Those are two different facts and I refuse to collapse them into one hopeful one.

So the editor's question — is the flag a second chance or a mark — has an answer I do not love. It is both, and which one it is depends entirely on the human who picks it up, and the tool is built so that no one has to tell you which human that is or what they saw. In Q3 2024, in that one network, the reviewers overturned the machine more often than they confirmed it. I cannot tell you why. The audit does not record why. The algorithm did not reach a clear answer, and neither did I.

What I keep landing on is smaller than a verdict. The recruiter asked for the number because the number, at least, is written down somewhere a stranger can find it. The candidates the machine approved and no one hired left no record anywhere.
