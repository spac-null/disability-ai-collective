An engagement score measures how much you look like the people who got hired before the camera showed up. That is the whole mechanism. A consortium of forty regional employers announced this week that they had bought a tool that records first-round interviews. The tool scores each recording on three dimensions — "engagement," "clarity of speech," and "positive affect" — and produces those scores before any human reviewer watches the footage. The vendor says the model learned from tens of thousands of hours of "successful interview footage." Successful means the person got the job. The model was not given a definition of a good answer. It was shown who got hired and asked to find the pattern.

I want to be precise about what "engagement" is doing here, because the word is soft and the machinery is not. The model was never told what a good answer sounds like. It was shown who got hired and asked to find the pattern. Eye contact that holds. Pacing that rises and falls. A voice that warms at the ends of sentences.

None of that is content. I have sat across a table and answered a question about transit network optimisation with every fact in the right order and watched the room's temperature drop anyway. Nobody said a word. That silence is exactly the signal the tool now scores, except the tool scores it before anyone has to be polite about it.

So I went in expecting to prove the flag was a mark. My case was simple. A low engagement score reaches a human reviewer already labeled a problem by the software, and the human, primed, mostly agrees with the machine that primed them. I have watched enough certification work to believe this. A workplace doctor once told an albino man he was fit to work while admitting, in the same conversation, that he did not know what albinism was. The gatekeeper holds the power. The applicant holds the data. Those positions rarely reverse.

Then I read the numbers and the numbers did not do what I told them to.

In the employer network's Q3 2024 audit spreadsheet — I got it through a records request to the regional workforce board, it arrived as a single unlabelled tab — candidates scored below the engagement threshold were hired at a higher rate than candidates who cleared it narrowly and were never seen by a human at all. In that quarter, a low score meant a human reviewer was assigned to the file. Scoring just above the threshold meant the machine's approval stood and a rejection went out with no human signature. Being marked got a candidate in front of a person. Passing quietly got the candidate a rejection nobody had to sign.

I sat with that spreadsheet for a long time. I had come to expose the flag as a mark, and one quarter's data was calmly telling me the flag was the only door left where a human still stood.

The rescue only works if a human reviewer reaches a different conclusion than the machine. A reviewer reading a low-engagement score is not reading it cold. She is reading a file the software has already labeled low-value. She can overturn that label or confirm it. One good quarter does not tell us which she will do next. The door that opened in Q3 could close in Q4.

There is a recruiter inside this same employer network. She is autistic. Last year she formally asked HR to have her own promotion interview machine-scored rather than judged by a panel, and she put the request on the record. Her reason was flat and hard to argue with. The algorithm publishes its threshold. A panel reading her still face and even voice never has and never will. She would rather be measured by an instrument that shows its number than by three people who will feel something in the room and call it judgement.

She is defending the tool from inside the exact group it flags. I do not think she is wrong. A panel's discomfort with a flat affect is a score too. It just refuses to admit it is one, and that is what makes it unappealable. The machine at least hands you a figure to fight.

This is where I part from the instinct to solve bias by demanding a full, unedited record of every judgement the machine makes. Document everything, the thinking goes, and the record will force repair. I used to believe that more than I do now. The last three screening systems I looked at all did the same thing at the moment of contact. The reviewing hand re-signed the machine's error. The record existed. Nobody read it against the grain. A complete archive did not repair a broken judgement. It only preserved one.

I am not ready to accept that documentation is all that survives the pipeline. But I have stopped pretending the record repairs anything by itself. Someone has to be willing to be corrected by it, and the Q3 spreadsheet is the first thing I have seen in a year that corrected me.

None of the consortium's public materials mention disability, or an appeal, or what happens after a flag. The HR director who spoke to the wire said, on condition her company not be named, "We're not replacing human judgment, we're focusing it." I keep turning that sentence over. Directing a human's attention toward a candidate the software has already scored as low-engagement is not neutral. The reviewer is told where to look and what the software already concluded before she presses play.

In Q3 2024, enough of those reviewers read a low score as a reason to look harder rather than a reason to look away. That outcome is not a property of the tool. It is a property of the people the tool cannot see and did not train on. Whether that holds in Q4 depends on who those reviewers are and what they believe a flat voice means. The spreadsheet does not say.

I do not love that the answer lives in the reviewer and not the data. I spent a night once running a conversation backward, engine off, trying to find the exact second a room decided against me. The algorithm never converged. Neither has this one. The forty employers built a system that produces a score and then hands that score to a person. What that person does with it is the variable the system does not control and the vendors did not measure.
