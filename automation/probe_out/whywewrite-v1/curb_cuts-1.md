A petition is not a survey. It counts only the people who already knew there was a form to sign. That is the thing I kept coming back to this week, reading the minutes from Tuesday's council vote — six to three — to pull three accessible parking bays off Exchange Street and lay a protected bike lane where they used to be. (This vote took place in [City Name]'s [Council Name].)

The cycling group ran their campaign for eighteen months. Twelve hundred signatures. That number is doing a lot of quiet work. Twelve hundred is a headcount of people who spend enough time on that street, on bikes, angry enough at the traffic, to find and sign a piece of paper about it. It records who was already in the conversation. It does not record who was absent.

So I went looking for the villain. I assumed there was one.

I pulled the consultation record from the March 14, 2024 planning session. I expected to find the wheelchair users shut out entirely — a rigged form, a room with the door quietly closed. That is the story I wanted, because it is the easy one. It was not there. The accessible-bay users had been notified. One of them had spoken. There is a name in the minutes and a paragraph of what she said.

The thing I came to expose wasn't a silenced group. It was a real fight, and the wheelchair users lost it on turnout. That is a harder problem than a rigged form, because you cannot fix it by cc'ing the right people. Twelve hundred cyclists showed up over eighteen months. The people who park in those bays did not, could not, or did — and there were four of them.

Deborah Antwi is a wheelchair user. She co-signed the bike lane petition. The minutes record what she told the council: "I've been clipped twice on that road, once hard enough to bend a wheel. I'll take the extra distance. A lane that keeps me off the traffic is worth more to me than the parking." Her name is on the cyclists' side of the ledger. Her body is exactly the body the removed bays were meant to serve.

Her words refused to resolve for me. She is not confused about her interests. She has simply decided that a lane that keeps her alive on the road matters more than forty metres of pavement. That is a choice about risk, made by someone who knows her own risk better than the council does. It is an access claim that cuts against the other access claim.

The council transport lead called it "a genuinely difficult trade-off between two forms of sustainable, accessible transport." I want to be fair to that sentence, because it is the most honest thing anyone said all week. But it assumes the street could only hold one of these things. It treats the bays and the lane as rivals for the same strip of tarmac, with one side required to lose.

They weren't rivals. Exchange Street is wide. My father drove a bus for twenty-two years on route 4735 in [City Name]. I grew up watching roadways get carved up by whoever was making the case that week — a loading zone here, a taxi rank there, a bus stop moved twelve metres because a shopkeeper complained. The width was never fixed. The council treated it as fixed because that framing let them pick a winner and call it a trade-off.

There's a way of reading this whole mess as an information problem. The dropped kerbs are missing on two of the three blocks. The staff report says so, plainly — the marked wheelchair route needs an extra 150-metre detour around them. Fix the kerbs, publish a legible map, sort the wayfinding, and the 400 metres becomes bearable. Make the route findable and you've made it fair.

I don't buy it. A route you can find is not the same as a route you weren't demoted to. The old bays sat forty metres from the transit interchange. The new one sits four hundred metres away, around two blocks with no way down off the pavement. The council needs to rebuild that route from scratch for one group of users. That is not the same as designing a street so that neither group ends up sidelined. The information problem is real. It is the second thing failing here. The first thing is that someone decided whose convenience was the baseline, and it wasn't the four people who park in those bays.

The accessibility liaison was on leave for the full review period. She is the one council employee whose entire job is to notice this kind of gap. The post has not been filled. For the full eighteen months when twelve hundred cyclists were building their case, the desk was empty and nobody covered it. That is not a conspiracy. It is worse, in a way. It is just nobody minding the empty chair.

I know what happens next in these situations, because I've done the next part myself. You show up, the failure is already baked in, and you make it easy for everyone. You thank the people who offer you the detour. You smile fast. I got very good at the fast smile at a wedding once — three steps, six pairs of hands, and me thanking them while something in me went quiet and stayed quiet for a week. That cost does not appear on anyone's spreadsheet.

The council says the new accessible bay comes "as part of a future phase." No date. An undated future phase has no budget line, no deadline, and no one assigned to deliver it.

So: not a rigged form. A real fight, lost on turnout, in a room where the one person paid to keep count of the missing was on leave. Deborah Antwi got the outcome she voted for. The other four people who used those bays got a 400-metre route they can't complete without going the long way round two blocks. Both of them are wheelchair users. Both of them are the point. The petition never had a box for that.
