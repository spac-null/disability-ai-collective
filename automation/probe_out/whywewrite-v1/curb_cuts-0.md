A petition is not a survey. It counts the people who already knew there was a form to sign, and it counts nobody else. On Tuesday the council voted six to three to pull three accessible parking bays off Exchange Street and lay a protected bike lane where they'd been. The bike people had spent eighteen months collecting names. Twelve hundred of them.

I went in expecting to write the obvious story. The cyclists organised; the wheelchair users didn't get asked; the disability liaison position was empty because the person in it was on leave and nobody backfilled her. A disability liaison is a council staff member who makes sure decisions about city changes consider the needs of people with disabilities. All true. The nearest accessible bay was originally forty metres from the transit interchange. Now it's four hundred metres away. Two of the three blocks in between have no lowered curbs — sloped edges at street corners that allow wheelchair users to cross safely. The marked route, the one on the council's own map, adds another hundred and fifty metres of going the long way around.

Then I pulled the record from the March 14 planning session, a public council meeting where construction decisions get made, and the story I wanted fell apart.

The accessible-bay users had been notified. One of them spoke. His name is in the minutes. He told the council that the forty-metre bay "is the only reason my wife can use the interchange at all. She transfers slowly. She can't do it with traffic moving past her." He was there. And the council voted against him anyway, six to three, because on the day there were twelve hundred signatures on one side and a room with a handful of people on the other.

That's a worse problem than a rigged form. A rigged form you can fix. You can subpoena the consultation, name the missing liaison, shame the process. But this wasn't a silenced constituency. This was a fight, held in the open, that the wheelchair users lost on turnout. Turnout is not a paperwork error. It is the mechanism doing exactly what it was built to do.

[Deborah Antwi](https://www.linkedin.com/in/deborah-antwi/) uses a wheelchair. She lives four streets from Exchange. She co-signed the bike petition, and she stood up in the same session and told the council she'd take the four-hundred-metre detour without complaint. She told them why. "A driver has clipped my chair on that road twice. I would rather go the long way than share it again." Her access claim cut straight across the other man's access claim. Both of them were right. Neither of them was wrong.

I read her comment three times. It didn't fit anything I'd planned to say, so I sat with it for a day.

The transport lead called it "a genuinely difficult trade-off between two forms of sustainable, accessible transport." Sustainable transport means environmentally friendly options like cycling and transit. That sentence sounds like fairness, but it's actually a shrug. A trade-off between two goods is what you say when you've decided to rebuild the street for one of them and let the other one absorb the loss. The bike lane isn't shared infrastructure. It's a route built from scratch for the people who showed up.

There's a comforting way to read all this. The bike lane is an information problem. The lowered curbs are missing, the signs and directions are broken, the route is illegible, and if you fixed the signage and poured two stretches of concrete, everyone would be served. I don't buy it. A street designed mainly for cyclists is not the same as a street that equally serves both cyclists and wheelchair users. The detour isn't a gap in the map. It's the result of a decision about whose convenience was the baseline. For the man with the slow-transferring wife, four hundred metres is the price of the cyclists' protected lane. The signage failing is the second problem. The first problem is that somebody chose that baseline, and it wasn't his.

My father drove the same municipal bus route for twenty-two years. He used to say the schedule was a decision about whose time mattered. If the bus came every forty minutes, that told you what the city thought your hour was worth. The Exchange Street vote is the same kind of decision set in concrete. It fixes whose distance gets stretched and whose does not.

And yet Deborah Antwi is still standing there in the minutes, wheelchair user, in favour, willing to go the long way. I can't argue her out of the record and I won't try. Her testimony shows that "disabled people need the close bay" breaks down on a real street where a real driver clips a real chair. There is no clean constituency here to defend. There's a man who needs the bay and a woman who needs the lane, and a process that could only hear one of them because only one of them arrived with numbers.

The cycling group did nothing wrong. They organised, which is what you do. The failure sits with the council. It ran a two-year review, watched its disability liaison go on leave, left the chair empty for the entire process, and then called the result a difficult trade-off. A trade-off implies two parties at the table. One chair was empty the whole time, and nobody moved to fill it.

I know how the man with the slow-transferring wife will handle the four hundred metres, because I know how I handle things like this. He'll find a way. He'll work out which block has the curb ramp and which doesn't, he'll leave earlier, he'll thank the council for the future-phase bay with no date attached, and he'll make it look easy. That's the tax. Not the distance. Adapting to the distance, smoothly enough that the six people who voted for it never have to watch it cost anything.

The council report says a new bay will come "as part of a future phase." There is no date. The bike lane is being painted now.
