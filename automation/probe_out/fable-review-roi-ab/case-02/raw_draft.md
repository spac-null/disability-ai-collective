An engagement score measures how closely you resemble the people who were hired before the camera arrived. That is the whole of it. This week a group of forty regional employers announced they had adopted a tool that records your first-round interview and scores it for "engagement," "clarity of speech," and "positive affect," then hands a single number to someone who decides whether a person ever watches the recording at all. The vendor says the model was trained on "tens of thousands of hours of successful interview footage." Successful. The word is doing enormous work and no one is watching it.

I want to tell you the obvious thing first, because it is true. Independent researchers looking at tools like this in other markets have found they dock you for eye contact that comes at the wrong intervals, for pacing that runs fast or slow, for a voice that stays level when the model expects it to lift. None of that is about what you said. It is about how much you move like the footage. An autistic candidate can give the better answer and score lower for giving it in an autistic body. The consortium's public materials do not mention disability, or an appeals path, or what happens after the flag.

So I set out to write the flag as a brand. My case was going to be simple. A low engagement score is not a second look. It is a mark, and a mark primes the hand that touches you next — the reviewer opens your file already told you are a problem, and confirmation is cheaper than disagreement. I had three other systems in my notes where the reviewing hand just re-signed the machine. I was confident.

Then I read the spreadsheet.

In July 2024 I sent a records request to the regional workforce board for the employer network's quarterly hiring audit. What came back was a Q3 2024 sheet, unglamorous, the kind of thing someone builds because a compliance box needs a number in it. I sorted it by outcome. Candidates flagged below the engagement threshold — the ones the machine wanted pulled for manual review — were hired at a higher rate than candidates who cleared the threshold narrowly and were never seen by a human at all. The flag I came to expose as a brand had, for one quarter at least, worked as a rescue. My hands went still. The pattern I had walked in holding was wrong.

I sat with that for a while. The flat conclusion is boring and I do not like it: sometimes a human being, told a machine flagged someone, opens the file, watches the whole interview, and thinks *this person is fine.* The machine's suspicion bought that candidate the one thing the confident-scorer never got — a human's full attention. The people who sailed past the threshold were triaged out by their own good scores. Nobody watched them. Nobody had to.

I keep turning this over because it undoes the shape of the thing. A flag is a request for attention. Whether it functions as a wound or a rescue depends entirely on who receives it and what they do with their eyes for the next four minutes. That is not a property of the algorithm. It is a property of the reviewer, which is to say it is not reproducible, which is to say the tool cannot promise it.

Here is what I did not expect. An autistic recruiter working inside the same employer network asked, in writing, to have her own promotion interview machine-scored instead of judged by a panel. On the record, to HR. Her argument was that the algorithm publishes its threshold and a human panel reading her flat face never does. She would rather be measured by a number she can see than by a room full of people deciding, silently, that something about her is off — and never telling her what. She defends the tool from inside the exact group it flags. I cannot make her disappear and I will not try.

She is describing my dinner party. Age twenty-six, I had been asked — asked — about transit optimization, and I answered carefully, with evidence, until I looked up and saw the face. The one that means the room shifted fifteen minutes ago and everyone was too kind to say. I drove home and sat in the car with the engine off for forty-five minutes running the conversation backward to find the exact frame where it turned. I never found it. The panel had a threshold too. It just never published it. At least the machine tells you the number.

There is a position I respect that says the right answer to a biased instrument is a complete, unedited record of every judgment it makes — keep everything, let the archive testify, refuse to optimize the horror away because optimizing is its own kind of erasure. I have felt the pull of that position for years and I want to argue against it here, on its merits, because the last three pipelines I looked at showed the reviewing hand re-signing the machine's error, and a perfect record of that changes nothing for the person who did not get the call. Documentation may be all that survives contact with the pipeline. I refuse to accept that it is the ceiling. The Q3 sheet is documentation, and it did not save anyone — I only found it after the quarter closed. What saved those candidates was a person who watched.

So the honest answer to the question I came in holding is: it depends, and the dependence is the scandal. Forty employers bought a tool that turns a hiring decision into a coin whose faces are named "reviewer who watches" and "reviewer who countersigns," and they did not publish which face is up. They cannot, because they do not know. The engagement score does not measure engagement. It measures resemblance, and then it names a stranger who will decide whether resemblance is destiny.

I still do not know how the recruiter's promotion went. I sent her request through the records office in a follow-up and the field came back blank — no outcome logged. The one file I most wanted to watch, nobody had.
