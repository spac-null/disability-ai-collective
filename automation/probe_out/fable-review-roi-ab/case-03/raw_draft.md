An engagement score measures how closely you look like the people who got hired before the camera arrived. That is the whole function. A consortium of forty regional employers announced this week that a recorded first-round interview now runs through a model trained on what the vendor calls "tens of thousands of hours of successful interview footage." The word doing all the work is *successful*. Someone already chose those people. The camera is not judging you against a standard. It is judging you against a hiring committee's past taste, rendered as a number.

I went in certain I knew what the flag meant. A candidate scores below the engagement threshold. The model kicks the recording out to a human reviewer. My first read was that the flag primes the hand that touches it next — that a person told a machine already flagged you as low will find the low in the footage, because that is what a flag is for.

Then I read the audit spreadsheet.

The workforce board released the employer network's third-quarter 2024 figures after a records request. I opened it expecting the flagged candidates to sink. They did not. Candidates flagged below the engagement threshold — the ones a human then watched — were hired at a higher rate than candidates who cleared the threshold narrowly and were never seen by a person at all. The flag I meant to expose as a mark had, in at least one quarter, worked as a rescue. The candidates the machine passed on quietly were the ones who lost, because nobody ever looked.

I sat with that for a while. My whole case had assumed the second look was contaminated. The data said the second look was the only look worth anything, and the threshold was busy denying it to the people who scraped over the line.

So the flag is not the problem. The threshold is. A composite score of, say, sixty-two clears; a sixty-one gets a human; a fifty-nine gets a human and, it turns out, a better shot than the sixty-two. The person who is quietly discarded is the near-miss who never triggered review. The engagement metric does its worst damage precisely where it looks most confident.

Here is the part I keep turning over. An autistic recruiter inside that same employer network asked HR, on the record, to have her own promotion interview scored by the machine instead of read by a panel. Her reasoning was flat and practical. The algorithm publishes its threshold. A panel reading her still face and even voice publishes nothing, and has never once told her the number it was scoring her against. She wanted the instrument that flags her community, because at least the instrument shows its work.

She is not wrong about panels. A human reviewer watching a recorded interview is running the same model the algorithm ran, only unquantified and unlogged. Simon Baron-Cohen built a whole research program on the idea that you can read a person's inner state from their eyes and face. I know that literature in detail and I think its central instrument measures how well a face performs legibility, not what a mind contains. Forty employers have now automated exactly that instrument and pointed it at first-round candidates. The panel was always the algorithm. The camera only made the scoring visible enough to audit.

Which is why I cannot fully hold the position that the right response to a rigged instrument is a complete, unedited record of every judgment it renders. The archival instinct says: document it all, keep the flag, keep the score, keep the trail, and repair comes from the record. I want that to be true. But the last several hiring pipelines I have looked at showed the reviewing hand re-signing the machine's call more often than overturning it, and a perfect record of a hand that mostly countersigns is a perfect record of a rubber stamp. Documentation might be all that survives contact with the pipeline. I refuse to accept that it is enough, and I notice I cannot yet say what would be.

There is a physical thing I know about being scored on engagement. In 2012, in a glass meeting room on the second floor of the Crossrail consultation offices near Tottenham Court Road, I laid out eight structural errors in how the network fed the central section. I was asked to. I was precise. I looked up partway through and the room had already left — not the bodies, the attention. Nobody interrupted. They were being kind. Kindness has no clear data, which is why it is worse than the alternative. A machine would have given me a number. I would have known where the line was. That is the recruiter's whole point, and I resent how good it is.

Independent researchers who studied these tools in other markets found the models penalize atypical eye contact, pacing, and vocal tone regardless of what a candidate actually says. None of the consortium's public materials mention disability, an appeals process, or the threshold itself. That last omission is the one that matters. Publish the number and the recruiter's argument stands: a visible line is a line you can fight. Hide it, and the machine inherits every quiet cruelty of the panel plus a claim to objectivity the panel never dared make out loud.

The HR director who led the rollout put it plainly, speaking on condition her company not be named. "We're not replacing human judgment," she said. "We're focusing it." She meant it as reassurance. Read against the spreadsheet it is a confession. The judgment was already there, already biased toward the people hired before the camera, and the tool has now focused it — sharpened it, aimed it, and hidden the aim behind a composite score.

I do not know whether the flag rescues or condemns. In one quarter it rescued. The near-miss who cleared by a point, unseen, is the one I keep thinking about. The camera never flagged her. Nobody ever watched. She simply resembled the past closely enough to be discarded without a second look.
