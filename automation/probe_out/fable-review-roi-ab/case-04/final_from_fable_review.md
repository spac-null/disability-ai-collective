A petition is not a survey. It counts the people who already knew there was a form to sign. On Tuesday the city council voted 6-3 to pull three accessible parking bays off Exchange Street and put a protected bike lane where they were. The removed bays sat forty metres from the transit interchange. The nearest one left is now roughly four hundred.

I read the vote the way most people in my field would — as a familiar theft. A cycling group had run a petition for eighteen months, gathered twelve hundred signatures, and won. No disability group was in the room. The council's own accessibility liaison had been on leave for the entire review, and the post still sits empty. I thought I knew this shape. Somebody left the disabled people off the list, ran the process without them, and called the result democracy.

So I went looking for the missing constituency, because that is the story I expected to write. I pulled the consultation record from the March 14 planning session. The accessible-bay users were not missing. They had been notified. One of them had stood up and spoken.

That stopped me. The thing I came to expose was not a silenced group. It was a fight the wheelchair users had, and lost, on turnout. Twelve hundred people signed a form over a year and a half. The people who parked on Exchange Street to make a train were a much smaller number, and they were fighting a slower, uglier fight — showing up to a Tuesday evening session, one voice against a mailbag.

That is a harder problem than a rigged form. A rigged form you can fix. You add a name to a list, you backfill the liaison post, you write a better consultation policy. But a petition rewards the group that already treats itself as a public — people who know each other, share a mailing list, can hand a clipboard to a friend outside a café on a Saturday. The cyclists had that. They had eighteen months and a thousand-plus signatures. The bay users had one person and a microphone.

One of the people who signed the cycling petition was a wheelchair user. Deborah Antwi told the council: "I've been clipped twice on this stretch in my chair. I'll take the extra four hundred metres if it means the cars are slowed and I'm off the carriageway. A protected lane isn't a loss of access to me. It's access." She was willing to pay for it in distance.

I have no clean rebuttal to Deborah Antwi. I don't think one exists. Her claim and the other claim are both real, and they both come from the same place — a body that has learned exactly what a road will do to it.

Which is why the framing everyone reached for this week bothers me more than the vote. The story got told as an information problem. The staff report itself flagged that two of the three blocks between the new bay and the interchange have no dropped kerb, which forces an extra hundred and fifty metres of detour around them. Cue the fixes: drop the kerbs, mark the route, add signage, do the wayfinding properly. Give the disabled people a legible path and the trade is fair.

A route built for one group and a route shared by two are not the same object, even when they are the same length. The cyclists got a protected lane — a thing designed from scratch around their body, their speed, their fear of being clipped. The bay users got a detour, retrofitted, tacked on, promised for a "future phase" with no date. One group got a thing built around them; the other got what was left over. The failure here is not that the wheelchair route lacks a dropped kerb. It is that nobody rebuilt anything for the wheelchair user. They rebuilt for the cyclist and handed the wheelchair user the leftovers.

My father drove a bus for twenty-two years, route 4735 out of Santo André. He used to say the schedule was a promise the city made to people who couldn't afford to be late. When the timetable slipped, the people who suffered were never the ones who could hail a cab. They were the cleaner starting a shift at six, the kid getting to school. Four hundred metres costs one person a mild inconvenience and costs another person the train they were going to catch.

I keep coming back to how the question got asked. The council's transport lead called it "a genuinely difficult trade-off between two forms of sustainable, accessible transport." Fine. But a trade-off implies two parties at a table. What actually happened was that one party spent eighteen months building a case and the other party got a letter and a Tuesday. When you ask "should there be a protected bike lane here," the only people primed to answer are the ones who came to want a protected bike lane. Everyone else is answering a question they didn't help write.

I know something about making it easy for people who didn't plan. I am very good at it. I have thanked people for solutions I didn't ask for and smiled while I did it, and the smile came fast — faster than the thing being solved deserved. There is a version of this vote where the bay users are asked to be gracious about their four hundred metres, to understand the difficult trade-off, to take the detour with good humour because Deborah Antwi does. I won't be running that errand today.

The council says a new accessible bay will come "as part of a future phase." The bike lane has a start date. The bay does not. On Exchange Street right now there is fresh paint going down where three bays used to be, and two blocks of kerb with nowhere to get up.
