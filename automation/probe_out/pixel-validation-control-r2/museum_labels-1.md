In September 2025, [Calder Gardens](https://www.calder.org/) opened in Philadelphia without a single wall label, and called the absence a philosophy. Calder Gardens is an art museum, and removing all printed labels is notable because it forces visitors to rely entirely on other sources of information. Open to interpretation. I read the announcement at my window in Brooklyn, everything bagel in one hand, and I thought: interpretation by whom, and delivered how.

Here is the thing nobody in the wall-text debate has to notice, because the debate is being run by people who hear. When you delete the label, the information does not evaporate. It moves. The question is which door it walks through on the way out. If it walks through the mouth of a docent, or the earpiece of an audio guide, it has not gone anywhere I can follow.

[The Belvedere in Vienna](https://www.belvedere.at/) is an art museum that proved something small and exact in 2021. Eye-tracking researchers watched visitors and found a fixed rhythm at every labelled work. Art, then label, then art again. The label is not read alongside the image. It is a separate object, consulted, then set down. I have done this my whole life, except for me there is no other channel humming underneath. The label is not a supplement to the room's talk. It is the room's talk, printed.

So when Calder Gardens takes the label off the wall, I want to know what fills the silence it thinks it created. For the hearing visitor: nothing, and that is the point, they get pure looking. For me: the docent starts talking. The tour murmurs past. The audio guide becomes the real exhibition and the wall becomes wallpaper. The default channel has quietly become speech, and text has been demoted to atmosphere.

I was ready to write exactly that essay. Then I went to Cleveland.

In October 2025 I stood in the [Cleveland Museum of Art](https://www.clevelandart.org/) expecting their [ArtLens app](https://www.clevelandart.org/open-access/artlens-app) to be text's exile. The ArtLens app is a mobile application that displays artwork information on a visitor's phone instead of on museum walls. Context banished from the wall into a phone, out of the room, out of the way. I opened it in front of a painting and it handed me more written context than any wall in that building has ever offered. Resizable. Captioned. In my pocket, at my eye level, which is the only eye level that has ever mattered to me. The Belvedere found that labels above or below eye level barely get read. My phone solves that by being wherever my hand is.

The museum called the printed label "the beginning of a relationship, not the end of an explanation." I had come to sneer at that sentence. Standing there, thumbing through more prose than the wall ever gave me, I could not. For the first time in that building's history, text was the primary channel and not the polite afterthought. My whole theory — that going digital means going demoted — broke against a screen.

Then I remembered [Christine Sun Kim](https://christinesunkim.com/). Christine Sun Kim is a Deaf artist whose work centers on captions, sound, and who controls how meaning is created and shared in public spaces. She replaced the public captions across Manchester with her own. Not neutral, not informational. "The sound of anticipation." "The sound of obsessing." A Deaf artist, whose entire practice is a fight over who controls the caption, looked at the plain institutional label and rejected it. Within Deaf culture, there is a long history of resisting the idea that Deaf experience needs explanation or interpretation by hearing people. Kim was choosing interpretation on her own terms. She was not asking for a better description of the Rothko. She was refusing the premise that the sound needed describing at all, and putting her own reading on the wall instead.

I keep her book near the label maker. On Tuesday mornings I relabel things that already have labels, because the font is wrong. Kim relabelled a whole city because the labels were wrong in a deeper way — they pretended to be the neutral truth of a room, and there is no neutral truth of a room.

Which sends me back to the question under all of this. There is a position, held seriously by people I respect, that the wall label was never the architecture in the first place. The real political layer of a museum is its air. The acoustics matter. The docent's low murmur matters. The way a guard's body tells you where you may stand matters. The address the space makes to a person walking in matters. On this view the label is paper laid over that, and peeling it off changes nothing. If that is right, the whole wall-text panic is a fight over decoration.

When I was twenty-two I watched a Deaf friend try to tell a hearing docent, on a pad of paper, what she saw in a Rothko. The docent kept replying with what Rothko meant. Her seeing was treated as a question, and he held the answer. The label on that wall was the docent's authority, printed and mounted, saying: here is the correct relationship to have with this rectangle. That is not decoration. That is the printed record of who is allowed to be right in the room. Kim understood this exactly. She did not ask for a fairer label. Instead, she went after the mounting brackets — the physical hardware that held the labels to the wall. She was rejecting the entire system that allowed museums to mount their authority as permanent fixtures.

So the label was both. It was the apology — the institution's nervous little paragraph telling you how to feel. And it was the architecture — the printed proof of who owns the meaning. Delete it and you have not automatically freed anyone. You have only reopened the question of who gets to answer.

Calder Gardens answers by handing the room to whoever can hear the docent and the guide. Cleveland answers by putting the text in my hand at my height, which is the most level that room has ever been for me. The deletion is the same in both places. The result is not. What changed was not the absence of the label but what the museum decided to trust in its place.

[Dawn Reid Brean](https://www.frick.org/about/staff), [the Frick](https://www.frick.org/)'s chief curator, made a comment I have not been able to put down. The Frick is a major art museum in New York City, and Brean's perspective on exhibition design carries significant weight in the field. She said: "What is not being said in exhibition text is often just as revealing as what is being shared." She meant it about curatorial silence. I read it about channels. What is not being said in text is being said in speech, in a register I cannot enter, and the museum decides — before the concrete is poured, before the first visitor walks in — which of those silences is the philosophy and which is just the exit I am left standing outside.

Open to interpretation. I am still waiting to hear whose.
