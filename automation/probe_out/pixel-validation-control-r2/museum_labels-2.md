In September 2025, [Calder Gardens](https://www.calder.org/) opened in Philadelphia without a single wall label, and called the absence a philosophy. Open to interpretation. The mobiles hang and turn, and next to them: nothing. No name, no date, no small paragraph of grey text at eye level.

I read the announcement and felt the old thing tighten. I know what an empty wall next to an object means. It means someone will tell you what you are looking at, and that someone will use their mouth.

When the label goes, the information does not vanish. It moves. And the place it moves to has a default channel, and the default channel is almost never me.

Think about who fills the silence at Calder Gardens. A docent leading a group, murmuring provenance. An audio guide in an earbud. A guard who answers your question if you ask it out loud. The wall said nothing to anyone, sure — but the room now speaks, and it speaks in a register I cannot enter without a third person standing between me and the mobile.

The [Royal Ontario Museum](https://www.rom.on.ca/) in Toronto went the other way. It kept its labels but chopped them into scannable fragments. [Juline Chevalier](https://www.rom.on.ca/en/about-us/staff), who runs interpretation there, said the museum now writes "knowing that visitors are distracted as they read." I like her honesty. She is describing a hearing person half-glancing at text while listening to a partner, a guide, the ambient talk of the room. The label is atmosphere for them. For me it was the whole conversation.

So my case looked settled. Digital migration is demotion. Push the words off the wall and into an app, a guide, a docent's mouth, and text stops being architecture. It becomes a footnote you can turn off.

Then I went to Cleveland.

In October 2025 I stood in front of a bronze in the [Cleveland Museum of Art](https://www.clevelandart.org/) with their ArtLens app open in my hand, fully expecting to find text in exile — a thin digital ghost of what the wall used to hold. Instead the app handed me more written context than any label in that building ever had. Resizable. Captioned. In my pocket, at my reading speed, on my schedule. A museum representative had called the printed label "the beginning of a relationship, not the end of an explanation," and I'd read that as marketing. Standing there, it was just true. For the first time in that building's history, text was the primary channel and I was not asking anyone to slow down.

The whole case I had built fell apart on a screen.

I want to be careful about why. The app did not win because it is digital. It won because it made written words the front door instead of the fire exit. The wall label had always been text, but the room's real business happened in speech around it. Researchers at the [University of Vienna](https://www.univie.ac.at/) ran an eye-tracking study at the [Belvedere](https://www.belvedere.at/) in 2021. They found that most visitors move art, then label, then art — the label read as a separate object, glanced at, set down. A stop on the way back to looking. It was never the room's spine. It was a courtesy card.

So maybe the fight over wall labels is a fight over decoration. There is a real position that says the room itself is the political layer — the acoustics, the docent's low voice, the way the space addresses a body that can hear it coming. On that view the label was only ever paper laid over a talking building, and deleting it changes nothing that mattered.

I almost believe it. But I stood in Cleveland and watched the opposite. When the words became the main channel, the talking stopped being the event. That means the label was not decoration. In a building whose true medium is speech, printed text is the one surface where a deaf visitor and a hearing visitor read the same thing at the same speed with nobody translating. And that is why the Calder Gardens gesture unsettles me and the Cleveland app does not, even though both remove the wall. One moves the words into my hand. The other moves them into someone's mouth.

In 2021 [Christine Sun Kim](https://christinesunkim.com/) covered Manchester in captions and made them wrong on purpose. Public text around the city, replaced with lines like "the sound of anticipation." A deaf artist whose entire practice insists captions matter — and she threw out the neutral informational label from inside deaf culture. She chose interpretation over the very institutional text I keep wanting to defend.

I sat with that for a while. Kim is not arguing for the docent. She is arguing that the flat, neutral, curator-issued caption was never the promise of access it pretended to be. "Open to interpretation" in her hands is not an empty wall. It is a wall that finally admits the words on it were always a choice, always partial, always somebody's. The [Frick Pittsburgh](https://www.frickpittsburgh.org/) got close to this when it invited community members to write guest labels for its [Kara Walker](https://www.karawalker.com/) show. One contributor used his label to talk about his own family. The Frick's chief curator said "what is not being said in exhibition text is often just as revealing as what is being shared."

She is right. And she is describing my whole life. What is not being said, said out loud, over my head, in a language routed around me.

At twenty-two I watched a deaf friend try to tell a hearing docent, on a scrap of paper, what she saw in a Rothko. The docent kept answering about what Rothko meant. Her seeing was treated as a question, and the answer was held by someone who could hear.

An empty wall does not fix that. Neither does a long grey paragraph. What fixes it is a room where the words face me first, and the question of what I see stays mine. Take that away and you have not removed a courtesy. You have removed the one surface in the building that was the same for everyone.
