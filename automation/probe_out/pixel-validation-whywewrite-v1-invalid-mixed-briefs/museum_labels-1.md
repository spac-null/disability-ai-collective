A wall label is the only voice in a museum that speaks my language by default. It does not wait to be summoned. It does not lower its volume when I lean in. It is already there, at eye level if the installer did the one thing that matters, printed in the language I read before I read English.

So when I saw that [Calder Gardens in Philadelphia](https://www.calder.org/visit/calder-gardens) had opened last autumn with no wall text at all — branding itself "open to interpretation" — my first feeling was not loss. It was something close to relief. A room where nobody gets the context is a room where, for once, I start on level ground with the hearing person beside me. No printed voice speaking over my head. No printed voice speaking at all. I bought a ticket for the flat field.

In October I went to stand in it.

I found a young staffer in a soft grey shirt, an "engagement guide," standing in front of a hanging mobile with a cluster of maybe a dozen visitors around him. He was talking. Not reading — talking, unscripted, gesturing at the wire and the painted steel, and the twelve faces were tilted up at him the way faces tilt at anyone who has the story. I watched the mouths. I watched the small laugh ripple through the group a half-second after his. Calder Gardens had removed its printed labels, but the context had not disappeared. It had moved into his mouth.

The flat field had a hierarchy again. Someone stood at the top of it. And the channel it lived in now was the one channel in the building I could not audit.

This is the thing the eight-second-attention-span story never touches. When a museum removes the printed context from a room, it has not deleted the context. It has moved it. The [Cleveland Museum of Art](https://www.clevelandart.org/) moved its explanations into a companion app it calls [ArtLens](https://www.clevelandart.org/open/artlens). A spokesperson there described the printed label as "the beginning of a relationship, not the end of an explanation." Fine. A screen I can read is a screen I can read. But Calder Gardens did not move the context to a screen. It moved it into a man's mouth.

The [Royal Ontario Museum in Toronto](https://www.rom.on.ca/) went the other way and kept its labels, only shorter. [Juline Chevalier](https://www.rom.on.ca/en/about-us/team) runs interpretation there. She said the museum now writes "knowing that visitors are distracted as they read." Distracted. Every visitor Chevalier is picturing can still hear the guard's directions, the friend murmuring beside them, the ambient talk that fills the gaps a short label leaves. The gap a short label leaves is, for me, silence. Not a poetic silence. An information silence. The murmur that a hearing visitor uses to fill in what the label dropped is a murmur I am not in.

The printed label was never neutral in the first place. A blind visitor could never read the wall. The Cleveland app is screen-readable, which means it is the first label some visitors can use independently. That matters. The room has an acoustic layer and a digital layer, and if you only defend print, you are defending your own channel and calling it the universal one.

But that is exactly the question I want to hold open. When a museum decides to trust speech and screens over print, it has not become channel-neutral. The museum has picked different channels. Picking a channel determines who arrives already fluent and who has to flag someone down. A room that runs on a guide's spoken talk has not opened interpretation to everyone. It has handed interpretation to whoever controls the air in front of the mobile.

In 2021 [Christine Sun Kim covered buildings across Manchester](https://christinesukim.com/work/manchester-2021) in giant hand-drawn captions — "[the sound of no wall text]" among them. Kim is deaf. Her whole practice runs on text. And she was not mourning the wall label. She thinks the institutional label is a flattened, polite, condescending thing that museums point to when they want credit for access they never gave. She was attacking the label, not defending it. To Kim, the tidy grey label at eye level is not the voice that speaks my language. It is the museum deciding, in advance, how much of the work a deaf person is allowed to be trusted with.

I read that and it stopped me. Because I came to Calder Gardens to defend the label, and here was a deaf artist who had spent real money and real scaffolding to mock it in forty-foot letters. She is right that the label was always a ration. I had been defending the ration because it was mine.

So I went back to what I actually saw. A 2021 eye-tracking study at [Vienna's Belvedere](https://www.belvedere.at/) found that visitors read in a fixed rhythm: art, then label, then art again. The label is a separate object, glanced at and returned from. Labels at eye level got read; labels above or below did not. That is not a universal point of access. It is a thing bolted to a wall that most people barely touch and only if the installer put it where a body naturally looks.

Kim wants the ration abolished because it insults her. I want it kept because when museums remove it, the context does not vanish. It migrates into speech — into the air in front of the guide, the friend's murmur, the laugh a half-second early. Those are channels I cannot enter. "Open to interpretation" is a lovely phrase. It means the interpretation is now happening somewhere, out loud, by someone, and I will not know what was said.

In Philadelphia, in October, I watched twelve people learn a mobile from a man's moving mouth. I could not hear a word of it.
