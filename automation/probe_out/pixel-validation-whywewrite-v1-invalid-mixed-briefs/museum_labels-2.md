A wall label is the only voice in a museum that speaks my language by default. It sits at eye level. It does not turn away when I approach. It does not ask a friend to relay it. I read it in my first language, which is written text, and it gives me the same words it gives the person standing next to me. This is rare. Most of a museum's information lives in the air, and the air is not my medium.

So when I read recently that [Calder Gardens in Philadelphia](https://www.calder.org/) had opened with no wall text at all — branding itself "open to interpretation," works left entirely bare — I wanted to see what that room felt like. A gallery where nobody gets the backstory is, on paper, the first gallery where I stand on level ground with the hearing visitor. No caption for them to have and me to lack. Flat field. I booked the trip.

In October I stood in that flat field and watched it grow a hierarchy in about ninety seconds.

A staff member — the museum calls this role an "engagement guide," a person hired to talk to visitors about artworks — gathered maybe a dozen people near a mobile and started talking. Unscripted. The visitors leaned in. Heads tilted. Someone laughed. I watched the group's shoulders soften the way people's shoulders soften when they are being told something. The context had gone oral. The words I came to celebrate the absence of were now being spoken into a room I cannot audit, to everyone but me.

When you remove the printed label, you do not remove the information. Someone decides to move it instead. At Calder Gardens, that decision moved it into speech. [The Frick Pittsburgh](https://www.frickpittsburgh.org/) made a different decision. It gave its [Kara Walker](https://www.karawalker.com/) labels to community members to write as "guest labels." Walker's work deals with race and history. [Shaun Myers](https://www.shaunmyers.com/), a professor at the [University of Pittsburgh](https://www.pitt.edu/) with family ties to the region, wrote a label that drew on his own family history. That is still text. That is still mine. Calder Gardens did the opposite. It did not spread the label around. It eliminated it and left only the spoken channel — the one that has always excluded me.

[The Cleveland Museum of Art](https://www.clevelandart.org/) kept the information available by moving it into a digital app called [ArtLens](https://www.clevelandart.org/artlens), which is screen-readable and compatible with assistive technology. One museum staff member called the printed label "the beginning of a relationship, not the end of an explanation." I have heard the case made that removing the wall label costs nothing, because the printed label was never readable to everyone anyway. A screen-reader can do things a placard never could. I understand that. I do not accept it as a reason to drop the print. The case treats the room's digital and spoken layers as the real ground and treats the printed word as an old skin the building can shed without harm. But which channel a room moves toward determines who it leaves out. A room that replaces print with an app has moved information toward the screen. A room that replaces print with a live talk has moved it toward the voice. Those are not the same room, and they do not fail the same people. My grievance is with the second room. Cleveland built the first.

A 2021 eye-tracking study run by the [University of Vienna](https://www.univie.ac.at/) at the [Belvedere](https://www.belvedere.at/) found that most visitors follow a fixed rhythm at a labelled work — art, then label, then art again. The label is read as its own object, a separate thing you turn to and turn away from. That is exactly how I use it. I do not absorb the wall text and the painting at once. I look, I read, I look. The label is a station I stop at. Strip the station and I do not get purer looking. I get a painting and then a group of strangers who know something I don't, standing three feet away, saying it out loud.

[Christine Sun Kim](https://christinesunkim.com/), a deaf artist whose work centers on text and language, would tell that version of me to sit down. Her entire practice runs on text. In 2021 she covered [Manchester's buildings with giant hand-drawn captions](https://christinesunkim.com/work/the-sound-of-no-wall-text/) — "[the sound of no wall text]" among them. She was not mourning the institutional label. She thinks the institutional label is a flattened, condescending scrap of access mistaken for the real thing. She is not weeping over the placard. She is holding the crowbar.

The wall label is not my ally because it is good. It is my ally because it was readable, and readable was the lowest bar the building happened to clear for me by accident. Kim's objection stands: a museum that thinks a two-sentence card discharges its debt to me was never really speaking my language. It was printing words at me and calling that access.

At twenty-two, in a museum whose name I will not give, I watched a deaf friend try to tell a hearing docent what she saw in a Rothko. She wrote on a pad and passed it over. The docent kept answering with what Rothko meant, as if her seeing were an unfinished question and he held the finished answer. I have carried that for years and named it wrong. I thought the wound was that the room had no words for her. The wound was that the room had plenty of words, all of them his, all of them spoken, all of them arriving as an answer to a seeing that needed no answer.

Calder Gardens says it is open to interpretation. In October I watched the person running the talk finish and step away. The dozen visitors drifted off carrying something. I stood at a bare white wall that told me nothing, which is what it promised, and I could not hear what the room already knew.
