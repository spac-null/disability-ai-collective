In September 2025, [Calder Gardens](https://www.calder.org/), a new Philadelphia museum space dedicated to works by sculptor Alexander Calder, opened without a single wall label, and called the absence a philosophy. Open to interpretation. The kinetic sculptures—Calder's famous mobiles—hang there naming nothing about themselves, and a visitor walks in with whatever they brought.

I read the coverage in [The Art Newspaper](https://www.theartnewspaper.com/) on a Tuesday morning at my window in Brooklyn, watching the G train, a New York City subway line, surface, everything bagel going soft. The framing everywhere was the same. Attention spans have shrunk to eight and a quarter seconds. Nobody reads. The wall text is a dying art form. Museums are cutting it, shortening it, or handing it to an app.

Here is what nobody in that coverage said, because most of them hear. When you delete the printed word from a room, you have not deleted the information. You have chosen where it will live instead. The two most common places it goes are a docent's mouth and an audio guide. For me, both of those are closed doors.

I went in already sure I knew the shape of this. Text is my channel. Delete the label and you demote the one thing in the building I can reach without a translator standing between me and a Calder. I thought the digital turn was exile with better branding.

Then in October 2025 I went to the [Cleveland Museum of Art](https://www.clevelandart.org/) to be angry at their app.

[ArtLens](https://www.clevelandart.org/artlens) is what Cleveland put on the wall's job. I expected the app to be a consolation prize, the thing they hand you after they've taken the real information away. Instead it handed me more written context than any wall in that building ever had. Resizable. Captioned. In my pocket, at my eye level, on my clock. I stood in front of a painting reading three paragraphs I could make as large as I wanted, for as long as I wanted, and I realised the wall had never once given me that. So the story I walked in with fell apart in front of a screen. Cleveland had not demoted text. Cleveland had, maybe for the first time in that building's life, treated text as the main way in.

That should have settled it. It did not.

Because a 2021 eye-tracking study at Vienna's [Belvedere Museum](https://www.belvedere.at/), one of Europe's premier art institutions, found that most visitors move through a labelled work in a fixed rhythm. Art, then label, then art again. The label gets read as a separate object. It is a thing you turn to and turn away from. Labels mounted at eye level got read far more than ones above or below. The word was never fused to the seeing. It was always a stop you made, or didn't.

Which means the wall label was already doing less than I wanted to believe.

There is a position that says the room itself is the real political layer, not the paper on it. The acoustics, the low murmur of a docent, the way a tour clumps and drifts. On this view the label is decoration, and deleting it changes nothing, and I am fighting over the caption on the apology.

I want that to be wrong, and it mostly is, but not for the reason I first reached for. Deleting the label does not change nothing. It changes which sense the room defaults to. Calder Gardens calls itself open to interpretation, and in a room with no text the spoken word fills that space. A guide's voice. An audio wand. The friend beside you saying the thing out loud. Open to interpretation, in practice, means open to whoever is talking.

Dawn Reid Brean, chief curator of the [Frick Pittsburgh](https://www.frickpittsburgh.org/), an art museum, said something about their guest labels that I keep turning over. "What is not being said in exhibition text is often just as revealing as what is being shared." She meant the silences a curator leaves. I read it in my own language. What is not being said in text does not vanish. It relocates to speech, and then it is being said, loudly, in a channel I cannot enter.

The Frick handed a [Kara Walker](https://www.karawalker.com/) show's labels—Walker is a visual artist whose work addresses race, history, and identity—to community writers. A University of Pittsburgh professor, [Shaun Myers](https://www.shaunmyers.com/), used his to write about his own family history. That is text becoming personal, becoming plural, becoming strange. I love it. It is also, physically, still on the wall. Still mine to read at my own speed. The Frick multiplied the voices in the text and kept the text. Calder Gardens removed the text and kept the room. Those are two very different decisions, even if they arrive in similar language.

In 2021 [Christine Sun Kim](https://christinesunkim.com/), a Deaf artist who works primarily in video and performance and whose practice insists that captions carry weight, went into Manchester's public space and replaced neutral captions with her own. "The sound of anticipation." Subjective. Personal. The opposite of the factual label I keep wanting to defend. Here is a Deaf artist, from inside Deaf culture, rejecting the neutral official text and choosing personal meaning over bare facts.

She is not on the docent's side and she is not on the wall placard's side. She is saying the caption should be a hand tracing a bird's arc, not a species name. She is doing to the label what an American Sign Language poem does to a sentence—transforming it into something vivid rather than merely descriptive. Making it a made thing, not a fact sheet. I do not think she would sign onto my defence of the neutral wall text, and I am not going to pretend her work fits my case, because it doesn't.

What I know now that I did not know at my window in September is this. The question was never whether text survives the digital age. Text is fine. It migrated into Cleveland's pocket and got bigger. The question is whether a room's default channel is one a body can reach without a translator. Museums are quietly answering that question every time they choose between a screen and a docent.

At twenty-two I watched a Deaf friend try to write down, for a hearing docent, what she saw in a Rothko—a work by [Mark Rothko](https://www.moma.org/artists/4099), a painter known for his large color fields. The docent kept answering about what Rothko meant. Her seeing was treated as a question someone else held the answer to. Calder Gardens took the label off the wall and called the empty space freedom. I keep thinking about who, in that free room, gets to do the talking.
