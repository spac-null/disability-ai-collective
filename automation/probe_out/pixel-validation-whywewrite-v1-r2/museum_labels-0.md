In September 2025, [Calder Gardens](https://www.calder.org/) opened in Philadelphia without a single wall label, and called the absence a philosophy. Open to interpretation, they said. The mobiles turn. Nobody tells you what they are.

I read that and felt something I did not expect. Not loss. Suspicion.

Here is the thing a hearing designer misses. A wall label is not a caption. It is the one channel in a museum that arrives in the same medium for everyone — silent, held still, waiting as long as you want. The docent's murmur passes. The audio guide assumes you can use it. The label sits there like a printed page in a room built for talk.

[The Frick Pittsburgh](https://www.frickpittsburgh.org/) once invited a University of Pittsburgh professor, [Shaun Myers](https://www.pitt.edu/), to write a guest label for a [Kara Walker](https://www.karawalker.com/) show. He used it to write about his own family. A label can do that — carry a named author, a specific perspective, a choice about what to include and what to leave out. Dawn Reid Brean, the Frick's chief curator, said that what is left out of a label is often as telling as what is put in. Fine. But there is a difference between a label that chooses its silences and a wall that has none.

When a museum removes its wall text, the information does not disappear. It moves.

I assumed I knew where. I assumed it migrated to speech — to the docent, the tour, the audio guide — and that a deaf visitor would end up in a room whose default channel had quietly become the one she cannot use. The text would be demoted to atmosphere. Removing it would simply reassign the informational channel from print to voice, while leaving the print slot empty.

Then, in October 2025, I went to the [Cleveland Museum of Art](https://www.clevelandart.org/) expecting the [ArtLens app](https://www.clevelandart.org/open/artlens) to be text's exile.

It was not. I stood in front of a Monet and opened the app, and it handed me more written context than any wall in that building ever had. Resizable. Searchable. In my pocket, at my pace, at eye level because my eye decides where the level is. A museum representative had called the printed label "the beginning of a relationship, not the end of an explanation." I had read that as marketing. Standing there, thumb enlarging the text, I could not honestly say it was.

That broke something I had walked in holding. Digital migration was supposed to mean demotion. Here it meant the opposite. For the first time in that building's life, text was the primary channel and speech was the extra.

So the fight is not migration versus loss. It is which room you land in, and there is more than one.

A 2021 eye-tracking study from the [University of Vienna](https://www.univie.ac.at/), run at the [Belvedere](https://www.belvedere.at/) in Vienna, watched how people actually read a labelled painting. The rhythm was fixed: art, then label, then art again. The label is not absorbed alongside the image. It is read as a separate object, a second thing, a stop the eye makes on the way back to the work. Labels hung at eye level were read far more than ones placed above or below.

I find this useful, and not for the reason the museums quoting it find it useful. They read "eight-second attention span" and conclude people cannot read. The Belvedere data says something else. It says people read in a loop. Look, check, look again. That loop is exactly how I take in a signed poem — the hand makes the shape, I read it, the shape moves, I read the movement, the meaning lands in the return. The label was never competing with the art. It was part of the visitor's own rhythm.

Consider the argument that the room itself is the real politics and the label is only paper laid over it — that the acoustics and the docent's low voice and the way a space addresses a body are the primary layer, and text is decoration. If that were true, deleting the wall text would change nothing. The quarrel would be about wallpaper.

But Calder Gardens did not change nothing. It changed who has to ask.

In a room with no text, the person who wants to know turns to the docent. That is a spoken transaction. The room did not become neutral when the labels came down. It became a room where the default way to get information is to open your mouth and hear an answer, and where doing anything else marks you as someone with a request.

In 2021 [Christine Sun Kim](https://christinesunkim.com/) took over the public captions in Manchester and replaced them. Not with better information. With subjective ones. "The sound of anticipation." A deaf artist — whose entire practice is a fight to make captions matter — throwing out the neutral informational caption in favour of interpretation. From inside deaf culture. She was choosing the guess over the fact.

I sat with that for a while and did not like where it left me. Because I had been building a defence of the plain informational label as the deaf visitor's one fair channel, and here was the person I would most want on my side saying the plain label is not the prize. She was doing to captions exactly what Calder Gardens claims to be doing to walls — opening them to interpretation. The difference is she did it as an author, on the record, with a body behind the words. Calder Gardens did it by taking the author away and calling the empty wall a philosophy.

I was twenty-two the first time I understood that a hearing person could look at a deaf person looking at a painting and assume the seeing was a question they held the answer to. A docent kept explaining what a Rothko meant to a friend who was trying to tell him what she saw. He never once asked what she saw.

A wall with no label does not fix that. It just moves the answer back into his mouth. Calder Gardens says open to interpretation. Whose interpretation reaches the wall, and whose stays in the pocket, and whose has to be asked for out loud — that is the actual design, and no one branded it. The mobiles still turn. Nobody tells you what they are. Some visitors will ask a person standing nearby. Some will open an app. And some will stand there and decide for themselves, which was always allowed, and never needed a philosophy.
