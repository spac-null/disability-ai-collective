In September 2025, [Calder Gardens](https://www.calder.org/visit/calder-gardens) opened in Philadelphia without a single wall label, and called the absence a philosophy. "Open to interpretation," the branding says. I read that phrase and felt a small alarm go off in a frequency I can almost feel through glass.

Here is what nobody in the attention-span pieces mentions. A wall label is the only channel in a museum that does not require me to hear. Take it away and you have not created freedom. You have quietly promoted the docent, the audio guide, the murmuring tour — every mouth in the building — to the room's default. A research study at the [Belvedere museum in Vienna](https://www.belvedere.at/) (2021) used eye-tracking technology to measure where visitors looked and for how long. The study found that visitors read a label as a separate object, in a fixed rhythm: art, label, art. The hearing visitor loses one of three beats. I lose the beat that was mine.

So I went in braced to be angry. I expected the whole digital turn to be a polite eviction.

In October I went to the [Cleveland Museum of Art](https://www.clevelandart.org/) expecting the same eviction — the same philosophy that removed wall labels at Calder Gardens, dressed up as an app instead. A Cleveland representative described the printed label as "the beginning of a relationship, not the end of an explanation." [ArtLens](https://www.clevelandart.org/artlens), the companion app, was supposed to be where text goes to be exiled. It was supposed to prove my point.

It broke my point instead. The app handed me more written context than any wall in that building ever had. Resizable. Captioned. In my pocket, at my own pace, no docent's back turned to me. For the first time in the museum's history the screen treated text as the main channel, not the atmosphere. I stood in front of a [Mark Rothko](https://www.rothkochapel.org/) painting reading three paragraphs I could actually control, and I had to admit the migration was not the demotion I had come to prove.

I want to be honest about that Rothko. When I was twenty-two I watched a Deaf friend try to tell a hearing docent what she saw in a Rothko. She wrote it out. The docent kept answering with what Rothko meant. Her seeing was not a question. She had the sharpest eye in that room, and he treated her looking as a thing in need of translation. That is the wound I carry into every unlabelled gallery. Calder Gardens, with its no-text philosophy, does not remove that docent. It removes the one thing that let her skip him.

Smart people hold a position I have to take seriously. It says the room itself is the real politics — the acoustics, the address of the space, the way bodies are steered. According to this view, the label is only paper over the architecture. Delete it and nothing structural changes; the fight over wall text becomes a fight over decoration. If that were true I would put down my label maker and go look at neon. But it is not true, and here is the test.

A blind visitor and I stand in front of the same Calder mobile. The room offers her an audio channel and me nothing. The room offers me a printed channel and her nothing. Remove the label and you have not made a neutral room. You have made a room with one working door, and it is not mine. The label was never decoration. It was the second door. Removing it is not an architectural reform. It is the architecture.

Then [Christine Sun Kim](https://christinesunkim.com/), a Deaf artist, replaced public captions at [Manchester's Craftworks gallery](https://craftworksmcr.org/) with her own in 2021. Not "door closing." Instead: "the sound of anticipation." Kim's entire artistic practice insists that captions matter. She threw out the neutral informational label — from inside Deaf culture — and chose interpretation over the very institutional text I have spent this essay defending. She did not want the museum's flat sentence. She wanted an emotionally rich one, written in her own hand.

Kim is not arguing for silence on the wall. She is arguing about who writes the sentence and what it is allowed to say. [The Frick Pittsburgh](https://www.frickpittsburgh.org/) did a version of this last year. It handed its labels for [Kara Walker](https://www.karawalker.com/)'s work to community writers, including a University of Pittsburgh professor, Shaun Myers, who used his label to tell his own family history. The Frick's chief curator, Dawn Reid Brean, said: "What is not being said in exhibition text is often just as revealing as what is being shared." She is right about that. And a guest label is still text on a wall. She kept the door. She just changed who was standing in it.

So the difference is not text versus no-text. The difference is whether the museum keeps a channel I can enter under my own power, and whether the writing in it is alive or embalmed. Cleveland moved the writing to a screen and, almost by accident, made it richer. Calder Gardens deleted it and called the deletion a gift. Manchester kept the wall and let a Deaf artist fill it with feeling. Toronto's [Royal Ontario Museum](https://www.rom.on.ca/) shortened its labels because, as its interpretation lead Juline Chevalier put it, they now write "knowing that visitors are distracted as they read." Fine. A short readable sentence is still a sentence. Eight seconds is enough to read a door and walk through it.

What I got wrong was thinking the enemy was the screen. The enemy is the philosophy that reads absence as generosity. "Open to interpretation" is a lovely phrase if you can hear the person interpreting.

The Belvedere study found one more thing, small and stubborn. Labels mounted at eye level were read far more often than labels placed above or below. Put the words where the looking already is, and people read them. Take the words away, and you have not opened anything.
