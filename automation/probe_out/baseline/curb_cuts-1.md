A petition counts only the people who already knew there was a form to sign. It is not a survey. It does not sample the street. On Tuesday the council in my city voted 6-3 to pull three accessible parking bays off Exchange Street and lay a protected bike lane where they were. The cycling group behind it collected 1,200 signatures over eighteen months. That number sounds like consensus. It records only the people who knew where the door was.

The bays sat forty metres from the transit interchange. The replacement bay sits roughly four hundred metres away. Two of the three blocks between them have no dropped kerb—a lowered section of curb that allows wheelchair access—which means the marked wheelchair route detours another hundred and fifty metres around them. So the honest distance is not four hundred metres. It is four hundred plus a hundred and fifty, because the design stopped at the signature count and left the kerbs unfinished.

I went in wanting to catch them. My first read was the obvious one: the disability constituency was frozen out, the accessibility liaison was on leave for the whole review and nobody backfilled the post, the fix was rigged by who got asked. Then I pulled the record from the March 14 planning session. The accessible-bay users had in fact been notified. One of them spoke. According to the minutes, a woman objected to losing the nearby parking bays. She said the distance to the replacement bay would make the transit interchange unreachable for disabled people.

That broke my clean version. This was not a silenced room. It was a fight on turnout, and the wheelchair users lost it. Twelve hundred signatures against a handful of people who found out in time to show up. That is a harder problem than a rigged form, because there is no villain to point at. One group had a mailing list and eighteen months. The other had a Tuesday and a room.

Deborah Antwi, a wheelchair user and a co-signer of the petition, told the council she supported the bike lane. "I've been clipped twice on that road in my chair," she said. "A protected lane keeps me off it. I'll take the four hundred metres." That is an access claim cutting straight across the other access claim. Some of the people the bays were for wanted the bike lane. The line does not run neatly between cyclists and wheelchair users. It runs through them.

So the story most people will tell — bikes versus disability, sustainability versus need — is wrong before it starts. But the story the council is telling is wrong too, in a quieter way.

The transport lead called it "a genuinely difficult trade-off between two forms of sustainable, accessible transport." I believe he means it. The trouble is what "accessible" is doing in that sentence. A bike lane and a set of parking bays are being weighed as if they were the same kind of thing on the same scale, and they are not.

Everyone is treating the four hundred metres as an information problem. Fix the dropped kerbs. Make the route legible. Put up better signs so the new bay is easy to find. That is the language of the staff report and it is the language people reach for when they want to seem reasonable. It is real work and it should happen. But it answers the wrong question.

The bike lane was designed as a continuous protected corridor. The wheelchair route was assembled from whatever space the bike lane did not occupy: two blocks with no dropped kerbs, a detour, a gap. When a design has one continuous corridor and one route stitched from leftover space, better signs do not change which part of the plan a wheelchair user is on. They are still on the part that was planned second.

I am not saying the bike lane should not exist. Deborah Antwi has been hit twice; her chair on that road is a body on that road. I am saying the council never asked whether the interchange could carry both. Nobody put a plan on the table that kept the forty-metre bays and found the bike lane somewhere else on the same street. That question needed someone in the room whose whole job was to ask it—an accessibility officer. That person was on leave for the entire review, and her post is still empty.

My father drove municipal bus 4735 out of Santo André for twenty-two years. He knew every stop by the state of its kerb — which ones let a woman with a pram roll on and which ones made her lift. The route sheet never recorded that. It recorded time and distance. The thing that decided whether a person could actually use the stop was the exact thing the paperwork could not see. A signature count works the same way. It records who signed. It cannot record who never learned there was a sheet. The details that matter most to disabled people are the details official processes consistently miss.

There is a version of me that would arrive at this wedding of a story, see the three steps everyone already knew about, and smile, and make it easy, and take the four hundred metres, and thank them. I have done that more times than I will write down. It is a skill. It costs something every time and the cost never shows up in anyone's minutes.

The council added that a new accessible bay would come "as part of a future phase." No date. I have read enough of these to know what that phrase does. It sets no obligation. It ends the meeting.

Twelve hundred people knew where the form was. One woman found out in time to speak against it and lost. Deborah Antwi found out in time and signed for it. And the accessibility officer whose entire job was to notice who never found out at all was somewhere else, and her post is still not filled, and the vote happened anyway. That is the fact I would leave on the table. Not the four hundred metres. The empty chair that was supposed to measure them.
