A petition is not a survey. It counts the people who already knew there was a form to sign.

On Tuesday, the council voted six to three to pull three accessible parking bays off Exchange Street. In their place goes a protected bike lane. The bays sat about forty metres from the transit interchange. The nearest surviving one is now roughly four hundred metres away. The transport lead called it "a genuinely difficult trade-off between two forms of sustainable, accessible transport." A future phase would add a new bay, she said, with no date attached.

I read that line and my first instinct was to write the story everyone expects. The cyclists organised. Twelve hundred signatures over eighteen months. The disability advocacy group was never consulted. The council's own accessibility liaison was on leave for the whole review and nobody filled the chair. That reading would make the process a straightforward failure of representation — a constituency that never got to speak because it never knew the room existed.

So I pulled the consultation record from the March 14, 2024 planning session, expecting silence, expecting the empty chair. It wasn't empty.

The accessible-bay users had been notified. One of them showed up and spoke. There was a real fight, and the wheelchair users lost it — on turnout, on volume, on eighteen months of a cycling group knocking on doors versus a handful of people who found out three weeks before the vote. That is a harder problem than a rigged form. You can fix a rigged form by fixing the form. This was not that. This was a loss inside a process the wheelchair users were allowed to enter.

Deborah Antwi uses a wheelchair. She co-signed the bike lane petition. She told the council she supported the lane and would take the longer route. "I'll take the detour," the record has her saying. "I've been clipped twice on that road. I'd rather wheel four hundred metres than get hit a third time." An access claim, cutting straight against an access claim.

I sat with that for a while. Antwi is not confused about her own interests. She weighed a longer route against a road that had hit her twice and chose the longer route. You do not get to tell her she chose wrong. She was in the room. She did the arithmetic on her own body and came out where she came out.

But look at what the arithmetic was made of. She was choosing between a road that had already clipped her and a detour. Not between the bays and the lane. Between the bays and the road as it currently is. Nobody asked her whether the lane could go somewhere that didn't cost the bays. That question was never on the table, because the petition set the table.

Why was this framed as a trade-off between two goods? The council's own staff report tells you. Two of the three blocks between the new bay and the interchange have no dropped kerb. A dropped kerb is a lowered section where the sidewalk meets the street; without one, a wheelchair user cannot leave the footpath at that point. The marked wheelchair route detours around both blocks, adding a hundred and fifty metres. So the four-hundred-metre figure is already an undercount. The real distance is longer, and part of it runs along kerbs you cannot get off.

That detail is where the current fight is being lost, and it is the wrong fight. Everyone argues about information. They discuss dropped kerbs, clear routes, wayfinding, and future phases. Fix the kerbs, add the signage, and the detour becomes tolerable. That is the reasonable-sounding version. It treats the whole thing as a problem of making the new route findable and smooth.

I do not buy it. A route rebuilt for one group differs from a shared route. A shared route ensures neither group is demoted to the background. You can pour a perfect dropped kerb on every one of those three blocks and you will still have moved one constituency four hundred metres so another could have forty. The kerb problem is real. It is also the second thing failing here, and everyone is treating it as the first.

My father drove a municipal bus for twenty-two years, [route 4735](https://www.emtu.sp.gov.br/), serving Santo André and the ABC neighbourhoods in Brazil. He used to say the schedule was a promise the city made and then spent all day breaking. The route on paper and the route at 5pm on a Wednesday were different objects. I grew up knowing that gap the way other kids knew cartoons. A signed inspection certificate works the same way. It says the bay exists, the kerb meets code, the route is marked. What it cannot tell you is whether anyone made it forty metres or four hundred.

The council did the paperwork correctly. Notified the right people. Held the session. Logged the objection. Voted. Every box is ticked and the outcome is still that a wheelchair user now travels ten times the distance a driver did in March. The council measured compliance. It did not measure use.

I keep coming back to the twelve hundred signatures. The cycling group spent eighteen months building that number. That is not a trick. That is organising, and it is the thing my side is worst at, because organising costs energy, and a lot of us spend the energy just getting to the room. You arrive at the meeting having already fought the kerbs to get there. Then you are asked to fight again, on turnout, against people who arrived fresh.

There is a version of me that would have smiled at the vote. Thanked the council for the future phase. Made it easy. I have gotten very fast at that, faster than I like. The smile is efficient. It asks nothing of the person who planned badly, and it leaves me with the whole ride home.

The petition worked exactly as designed. It asked a question, and it counted the people who already knew to answer. Antwi answered it and meant her answer. The forty metres are gone anyway. The future phase has no date. The accessibility liaison chair is still empty, and now nobody is even on leave from it — the post just isn't filled.

Four hundred metres. Two blocks with no way off the kerb. A replacement bay with no opening date.
