An engagement score measures how closely you resemble the people who were hired before the camera arrived.

This week, forty regional employers announced they had adopted a tool that records your first-round interview and scores it for engagement, clarity of speech, and positive affect. It produces one number. A human uses that number to decide whether to watch you at all. The vendor says the model was trained on tens of thousands of hours of interview footage from candidates who were hired. That word, *successful*, carries the entire claim. Nobody is asking what it means.

I know what I look like on camera. Flat affect, they call it, when the doctor is being polite. That's the medical term for showing little emotional expression on your face. I looked directly into a lens once, in [a 2019 practice recording for a consultancy pitch](https://www.autisticselfadvocacy.org/), and watched myself back. My eyes went to the middle distance every time I reached for a number. My hands went still. I have spent my whole life being told this stillness means I have checked out, when it means the opposite. It means the pattern has resolved and I am inside it. A model trained on people who perform attentiveness will read the moment I concentrate hardest as the moment I disappear.

So my starting position was easy and I held it firmly. The flag is a mark. It travels with you. A human reviewer who opens a file already stamped "below threshold on engagement" does not open it neutral. They open it looking for the reason the machine was right. This is not exotic psychology. It is the most boring finding in the study of human judgment: tell someone a thing is probably defective and they will find the defect.

Then I read the spreadsheet.

A regional workforce board—the government agency that oversees hiring and employment programs—released the employer network's Q3 2024 hiring audit after a records request. This audit mattered because it tracked whether the engagement-scoring tool actually produced better hires. I opened it expecting to confirm what I already believed. Instead the numbers contradicted me. Candidates flagged below the engagement threshold were the ones a human was forced to actually watch. These candidates were hired at a higher rate than candidates who cleared the threshold by a hair and were never seen by a person at all. The flag I came to expose as a mark had, for one quarter, worked as a rescue. The people the machine waved through were the ones who quietly lost. My eyes went to the middle distance and my hands went still and I sat with a coffee going cold for a long time.

Here is what I think that spreadsheet shows, and I hold it loosely, because one quarter is one quarter. The threshold does two things at once. To the person above it, it grants a certificate—passed, cleared, no human required. That certificate is precisely the thing that ends their candidacy in a stack of five hundred identical certificates. To the person below it, it forces a human to spend ninety seconds actually watching a human. Being flagged bought the autistic candidate the one thing the smooth candidate was denied: a person's attention.

That is not a defense of the tool. It is a description of how starved the ordinary process already was. A human panel that never reads your flat affect as a flag is not reading it as neutral either. It is reading it as coldness, or arrogance, or "not a culture fit"—meaning you don't match the company's values or work style—and unlike the machine it never publishes the threshold you failed to clear.

A recruiter inside the network told me this directly. She is autistic. In her own promotion round she formally asked HR, on the record, to have her interview scored by the machine instead of judged by a panel. "The algorithm tells me the number," she said. "The panel just tells my manager I seemed off." She wanted the instrument that flags her because the instrument, unlike a room of well-meaning colleagues, cannot claim afterward that it never held anything against her. She defends the thing from inside the exact group it penalizes, and she is not confused. She has simply been in enough rooms to prefer a cruelty with clear data over a kindness with none.

I recognize that preference in my body. The worst rooms I have been in were not the hostile ones. They were the rooms where people smiled and made decisions about me based on impressions they never named and could never defend—decisions that felt personal because they were delivered as concern.

For years I held a position that went like this: the right response to a biased instrument is a complete, unedited record of every judgment it renders—log everything, publish everything, let the audit trail be the accountability. Document the harm precisely and the precision becomes a lever. I no longer think it holds. The last three scoring systems I looked at all did the same thing when a flag met a human. The reviewer countersigned the machine. The record documented, in immaculate detail, a human hand countersigning an error it had been primed to countersign. The trail was perfect. Nothing in it repaired anything. A signed inspection is not evidence the thing works. It is only evidence that the form was filled in.

So the editor's question—is the flag a second chance or a mark?—has the wrong shape. It assumes the flag has a fixed meaning that the human either honors or betrays. It doesn't. The flag means whatever the process starving on either side of it makes it mean. In a pipeline where nobody is watched, being watched is a rescue. In a pipeline where the watcher has already been told what to see, being watched is the mark. The number does not change. The system around it decides what the number does.

None of the consortium's public materials mention disability, accommodation, or how you appeal a composite score. That absence tells you what the tool is for. They have built a machine that decides who gets a human, and they have not once asked what the human does with what the machine hands them.

I keep going back to that Q3 spreadsheet. The people who cleared the bar and were never seen. Somewhere a candidate scored high on positive affect, smiled at the right cadence, hit every threshold, and was filed by a machine into a pile no person ever opened. The tool worked perfectly. The candidate resembled the ones hired before the camera arrived. That was the whole of what it was asked to do.
