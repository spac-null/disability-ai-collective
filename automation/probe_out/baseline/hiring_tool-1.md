An engagement score measures how closely you resemble the people who were hired before the camera arrived. That is the whole function. This week, forty regional employers agreed to let a machine watch a recorded interview and rate it for engagement, clarity of speech, and whether a candidate appears happy or enthusiastic. The machine produces a single number. A recruiter then uses that number to sort candidates, rejecting most of the lower-ranked ones without any human review. The vendor says the model learned from tens of thousands of hours of successful interview footage. Successful means the person got the job. So the target the machine optimizes toward is the last cohort of hires, made by human panels, in rooms with no camera.

I read the vendor's own language three times looking for the sleight of hand. There isn't one. It's stated plainly. The model reproduces the people who already got through. If a panel of humans spent a decade preferring steady eye contact and a warm even tempo, the machine now enforces that preference at scale, faster, and calls it engagement.

I know this preference from the inside. In 2013 at a dinner in Stockholm I was asked about transit network optimization, answered carefully, and looked up to find the face — the one that means the room shifted fifteen minutes ago and nobody stopped me because they were being kind. That moment taught me that kindness has no clean data, and that discomfort in social spaces can be invisible to the person causing discomfort. A machine at least publishes its threshold.

So I went in to prove the flag is a mark. Not a second chance. A stain the next hand rubs deeper. When a recruiter opens a file already tagged below threshold, the machine has told them what to see before they see it. My case was that manual review doesn't overturn the score. It launders it.

Then I got the spreadsheet.

Through a records request to the regional workforce board I obtained the network's Q3 2024 hiring audit. I expected the flagged candidates to be a graveyard. Instead, the flagged-below-threshold group was hired at a higher rate than the group that narrowly cleared the line. The candidates who scored just well enough to skip human review were quietly the worse-off ones. Being seen by a human, even a primed one, beat being waved past by a number.

I sat with that for a week. It broke the clean version of what I meant to write.

Here is what I think the spreadsheet actually shows, and it is not that the tool is fine. The narrow-clearance group is invisible. Nobody watches them, nobody advocates, they are a single combined score made from multiple measurements, and then they are a rejection email. The flagged group at least triggers a human who now has to make a decision they can be asked about later. The flag does not produce fairness. It produces a witness. That is a different thing, and a thinner one.

A witness only helps if the witness disagrees with the machine sometimes. And most quarters, they don't. Q3 2024 is one audit. I do not know if it holds. The last three scoring systems I looked at closely all showed reviewers approving the machine's flag without changing it, just adding their signature for weight. A signed inspection is not evidence that anything works. It is evidence that someone signed.

There's a position I keep running into, and I want to take it seriously because it's the honest one: the fix for a biased instrument is a complete, unedited record of everything it decides. Log every score, every flag, every override, publish it, let the pattern indict itself. I used to hold this fully. I am no longer sure repair is even a coherent goal when the reviewer re-signs the error anyway. A perfect archive of a laundering process documents the laundering perfectly and stops nothing. The record survives contact with the pipeline. The candidate does not. I am not ready to accept that documentation is the whole win, but I can no longer pretend it's the win it claims to be.

A recruiter inside the same employer network asked HR, in writing, to have her own promotion interview scored by the machine instead of judged by a panel. She is autistic and has spent years in interviews being evaluated on how she presents socially — her tone of voice, her eye contact, her facial expression — in ways that felt invisible and unjust. Her request reads, in part: "I have spent my entire working life failing an interview reading I am never shown and cannot appeal. I would rather be measured against a stated threshold than against a panel's impression of my affect." The algorithm publishes its threshold. A human panel reading her flat affect and level voice never tells her what it's holding against her. She'd rather lose to a stated line than to a face doing arithmetic it won't show its work on. She is defending the tool from inside the exact group it flags. I cannot make her wrong. She has better data on her own interviews than I do.

What she's pointing at is the thing I nearly missed while trying to prove my clean point. The machine is not the new enemy. It is a mirror bolted to the front of the old one. The panels were already scoring engagement. They just did it in the dark and called it a feeling. The camera recorded what the panels were already measuring. It did not introduce the bias. It made the bias visible on paper.

That is why the missing line in the whole rollout is the one nobody printed. Forty employers announced engagement, clarity, appearing happy or enthusiastic, a single combined score made from multiple measurements, a triage. None of them announced what happens when the number is wrong. No appeal, no stated threshold given to the candidate, no route back for someone the model marked because they pace a sentence oddly or hold your eyes for the wrong duration. The recruiter wants the published line. The candidate outside doesn't get told there's a line at all.

I check three transit feeds every Tuesday because a published schedule can be held to account and a mood cannot. This is the same principle: transparency makes systems answerable. The people in that Q3 spreadsheet are not a mood. They are a column, sorted, thresholded, and mostly never seen. One quarter, being seen saved some of them. I don't know if the next quarter did. Neither, I'd wager, do the forty employers who signed.
