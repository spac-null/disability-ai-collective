An engagement score is a rating based on how similar your interview behavior is to people already hired. That is the whole mechanism. A consortium of forty regional employers announced this week that a candidate's first-round video interview now gets scored for "engagement," "clarity of speech," and "positive affect" before a human watches a second of it. The vendor says the model learned from tens of thousands of hours of "successful interview footage." Successful meaning: the people already inside got in.

I know this face from the other side. Not the applicant's — the room's. A dinner party, October 2012, my first autumn in London. Someone at the table asked me a direct question about how London's subway system map distorts geography to show the transit lines more clearly. I answered it with evidence. I looked up at some point and the room had moved on fifteen minutes earlier and stayed polite about it. Nobody scored me. But a camera would have. Flat tone, wrong pacing, eye contact aimed at the middle distance where I do my best thinking.

So when I read that this tool flags low-engagement candidates for manual review, I expected the review to be theatre. My working belief was simple. The flag is a mark, and the human hand that touches a marked file touches it differently. You do not read a flagged interview to be surprised. You read it to confirm the flag was right. I have watched this happen with subway system fault reports for years — an amber alert primes the engineer who opens the ticket, and the ticket closes as expected roughly every time.

Then I looked at the numbers, and the numbers did not agree with me.

In the employer network's Q3 2024 audit spreadsheet — obtained through a records request to the regional workforce board — candidates flagged below the engagement threshold were hired at a higher rate than candidates who narrowly cleared it. The candidates who narrowly cleared the threshold were never seen by a human at all. The machine approved some candidates automatically. Those candidates got hired less often than flagged candidates who received human review. The flag, in that one quarter's data, was not a mark. It was a rescue. It pulled the strange file in front of a person, and the person, given the strange file, hired.

I sat with that for two days and did not like what it did to my case. The whole shape of what I meant to write assumed the reviewer agrees with the machine's decision. Here the reviewer was overturning it. The escalation queue was working exactly the way the HR director claimed. "We're not replacing human judgment," she said, speaking on condition her company go unnamed. "We're focusing it." I have quoted that line back to myself with a sneer three times. The spreadsheet does not contradict her.

But getting flagged is not free. To land in the review queue, you first have to be legible as a problem. Approved candidates who were rejected never got human review and don't appear in the data. The flag rescues the people it flags. It does nothing for the people it silently sorts to the bottom. Those people generate no queue and no record. My mistake was reading the rescue rate as evidence the system works. It is evidence that being seen at all is the scarce resource, and the machine hands it out on the basis of resemblance.

Then a recruiter inside the same network complicated it further. She is autistic. In 2024 she formally asked HR, on the record, that her own promotion interview be scored by the machine instead of judged by a panel. Her reasoning: the algorithm publishes its threshold. She can see the number she has to clear. A human panel sees that she shows little emotion in facial expressions and then rejects her using vague language like "fit" rather than stating a clear criterion. She wants the machine because the machine, unlike the room, shows its working.

I cannot dismiss her, because she is describing the dinner party. The room had a threshold that night too. I just never got to see it. I spent forty-five minutes in a parked car afterward running the conversation backward, trying to find the exact frame where the room turned. I never found it. A published number would have given me something to find. She is not wrong that a visible metric, however harsh, is easier to challenge than a kind invisible one. An invisible standard produces no data to contest.

So where does that leave the mark. There is a comfortable position that says: the fix for a biased instrument is a complete, unedited record of everything it decides. Log every score. Keep every flag. Let the archive be honest even when the machine is not. I have leaned on that position myself. I am now fairly sure it is not enough, because in the last three hiring pipelines I have pulled apart, the reviewing hand mostly agrees with the machine's judgment rather than reverses it. The Q3 rescue was one quarter. A complete record of a hand that only agrees with the machine documents the same decision twice. That is not repair.

The deeper problem is what "engagement" is made of. It is not a fact about the candidate. It is a measurement of similarity. It measures the distance between your face and the archive of faces that already won. An autistic candidate does not score low on engagement because they are disengaged. They score low because engagement was defined, before the camera arrived, as looking like the last cohort. That is not measurement. It is the last cohort's preferences, encoded and re-run.

The vendor has not published the demographic makeup of those tens of thousands of hours of successful footage. Neither has the consortium. The Q3 spreadsheet exists because one records request pried it loose. There is no Q4 spreadsheet, no appeals process named in any public document, and no number a flagged candidate can ask to see. The recruiter wanted a visible threshold. Forty employers bought a system and kept the threshold in a drawer.
