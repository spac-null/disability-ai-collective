A sauna is one of the few rooms built to make you stop reading it.

Most rooms I enter, I read whether I want to or not. A cathedral at noon tells me its height before I take three steps — the ceiling throws my footfall back long and cold. A tiled changing room hands me its width instantly. I have spent fifteen years being paid to hear rooms this way. So when Swedish artists [Bigert & Bergström](https://www.bigertbergstrom.com) finished their pink crystal sauna in Skellefteå this summer — 280 mirrored steel facets, the whole thing tilted four degrees off vertical — I assumed I knew what my ears were about to get.

Hard steel means hard reflection. I expected slap-echo: sound bouncing fast between facets, giving me a busy, over-bright picture of a small room I could pace out in under a minute. Titanium-coated stainless steel reflects almost everything that hits it. I went in ready to map it.

That is not what happened.

On my second sit, someone ladled water onto the stones. The steam burst — löyly, the Finns call it, the breath that comes off hot rock — and every reflection I had been tracking went soft and dead. The facets stopped answering. The room I had half-drawn in my head dissolved into a close, wet hush that told me nothing about where the walls were. Hot air absorbs high frequencies. Those are the frequencies I steer by. Add steam and the whole space goes matte to the ear.

I sat there having been wrong for about ninety seconds before I understood I had been wrong about something better than I'd guessed.

Call it a fault. Fix it. Hang something at the door — a small fan, a dripping tap, anything that stays put so a body inside can keep its bearings. Make the room mappable again. That is the instinct, and I think it is mostly wrong here.

Niko, one half of the duo, told [Dezeen](https://www.dezeen.com) that "the project is shaped around ambiguity." I read that at first as artist-statement filler. Then I sat in the thing and it turned out to be a spatial fact. The four-degree lean scatters light into no coherent image. The eye gets the same treatment my ears got. Nobody who walks into that room gets a clean picture of where they are. That is not a failure of the design. That is the design doing its one job.

A regular swimmer at a public sauna in Finland told the artists the same thing during the build. She comes, she said, precisely to lose track of herself. A sauna where you always know exactly where the door is has failed at the only thing a sauna is for.

I want to resist that and I can't. She is describing, from the inside of a hearing body, the state I am usually fighting my way out of. When a room stops giving me its edges, I use sound reflections to map the space, count steps, build the missing picture by hand. It is skilled work and I am good at it and I would rather not do it in a sauna at 90 degrees. She pays for the disorientation I spend my professional life dissolving. We are sitting in the same fog wanting opposite things from it.

The question is asking the wrong room to behave. The geometry does collapse. And the collapse is what the room is for. This is one of the vanishingly few designed spaces that wants me to stop working.

Consider what the site was. The Scharin pulp mill processed wood pulp for decades. Industrial runoff contaminated the surrounding ground so thoroughly that the site became one of the most poisoned patches of land in Västerbotten, a region in northern Sweden. Workers spent seventeen years scraping contaminated topsoil down to bedrock. They capped the rest under a sealed layer of clay. They built the sauna on top of that seal. A room designed to disorient you, sitting on ground that had to be buried and capped because it was still leaching into the water table.

What I know from the body is narrower and more certain. Heat and steam strip out high frequencies. Those are the frequencies I use to find walls. One ladle of water and a blind person loses that. But so does everyone else. The sighted person loses the facet-scattered light at the same moment I lose the reflections. The room removes the same thing from all of us at once. Architecture usually serves the eye first and lets the ear take whatever bounces back. For ninety seconds in that sauna, that order was gone.

There is a version of accessibility that would rush to restore the reference point. It means well. It also assumes that being able to map a room is always the good, and being unable to map it is always the harm. Fifteen years of hearing rooms has not convinced me of that. That ninety seconds of dead, wet quiet was worth more to me than a memo recommending a fan by the door.

I sat in the crystal for maybe twenty minutes. When I stepped out, the changing room's hard little slap came back — the tile, the width, the door exactly where I'd left it. My ears took the room back automatically, the way they always do. The Finnish woman was still inside, staying longer, keeping her bearings gone on purpose. I don't envy her the heat. I envy her the ease of getting there.
