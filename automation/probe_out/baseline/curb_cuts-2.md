A petition counts only the people who already knew there was a form to sign. It is not a survey. It does not go looking for the person who didn't show up. On Exchange Street this week, a cycling group's petition — 1,200 signatures gathered over eighteen months — did exactly what a petition does. It counted its own.

The council voted 6-3 on Tuesday to pull three accessible parking bays and put in a protected bike lane. The bays sat 40 metres from the transit interchange, a central hub where multiple types of transportation — buses, trains, and other services — connect. The nearest one now sits roughly 400 metres away. Two of the three blocks between the new bay and the interchange have no dropped kerb — a lowered section of sidewalk curb that allows wheelchair users to cross smoothly — so the marked wheelchair route detours another 150 metres around them.

I went in expecting to find a rigged process. That is the story you reach for. Cyclists organised for eighteen months; nobody asked the disability groups; the council's accessibility liaison was on leave the whole review period and the job still isn't filled. An accessibility liaison is responsible for making sure decisions don't accidentally harm people with disabilities. Their absence meant nobody was in the room whose job it was to spot problems like this. It reads like a form written for one room, handed only to the people in it.

Then I pulled the consultation record from the March 14 planning session. And the wheelchair-bay users had, in fact, been notified. One of them spoke. He came, he made his case about the 40 metres and the missing kerbs, and the vote went 6-3 against him anyway. The thing I came to expose — a silenced constituency — wasn't quite there. What was there was a real fight the wheelchair users lost on turnout. That is a harder problem than a rigged form, because you cannot fix it by fixing the form.

The cycling group put twelve hundred names on paper. The bay users put one man in a room. Both are democracy. The difference is that one of them was a crowd.

[Deborah Antwi](https://www.wheelchairwars.com/), a wheelchair user who parks downtown and signed the cycling petition, told the council she wanted the bike lane. She said she would take the 400-metre detour. She had already been clipped twice on that road — a driver's mirror, a car door — and a protected lane keeps her chair off it. Her access claim runs straight into the other access claim. Two wheelchair users, same street, opposite votes. There is no side here that is only right.

So the honest picture is not villains and victims. It is a genuine fight over the same forty metres of kerb, decided by who showed up, refereed by a council with no one in the accessibility chair.

Everyone is treating this as an information problem. The staff report flags the missing dropped kerbs. The transport lead promises a new bay "as part of a future phase," no date. The assumption underneath all of it is that if you fix the wayfinding — legible route, painted markings, a kerb cut on those two blocks — you have fixed the thing. Make the detour findable and the detour becomes fair.

It doesn't. A route built for cyclists, with a bay bolted on 400 metres away as an afterthought, is not the same as a street redesigned so both groups have equal access. When a street treats one group as an afterthought, neither group gets treated fairly — both become obstacles rather than people the street was designed to serve. Deborah's own point proves it: she wants the bike lane because the road was trying to kill her, not because 400 metres is fine. The council interpreted Deborah's support as full endorsement and ignored her concerns.

I know how that comma works. I'm a wheelchair user, and in September 2023 I went to my closest friend's wedding. The venue had three steps at the entrance. Everyone had known for months. Nobody mentioned it until I was at the bottom of them, and then six people lined up to carry me, and I smiled and thanked each of them and made it easy, because I have had twenty years of practice making it easy for people who did not plan. That night in the hotel I couldn't stop shaking. Not about the steps. About how fast I'd smiled. Deborah smiled at the council. "I'll take the detour." And they wrote it down as a solution.

The council's transport lead called it "a genuinely difficult trade-off between two forms of sustainable, accessible transport." I actually believe her that it was difficult. What I don't believe is that it had to be a trade-off at all.

Look at the width of Exchange Street. There is a version of that street — protected bike lane, a loading kerb, a bay held at 40 metres, a continuous dropped kerb along the whole run — that gives the cyclists the safe lane and gives the wheelchair users the short distance. It costs more. It needs someone whose whole job is to sit in the room and refuse the binary. That person was on leave. The job is still empty.

That is the real vote here. Not bikes against chairs. The council chose the cheap frame — one room, one petition, one thing wins — over the expensive frame, where you keep both and pay for the kerb. A protected bike lane is roughly the cost of the paint and the bollards. A continuous dropped kerb along three blocks is a few days of a crew and concrete. This was never a money wall. The council chose to let the room with the most people in it draw the map.

A petition is a good instrument for the people already holding a pen. It is a terrible instrument for finding the person who didn't know the form existed, who was clipped twice on the road and can't tell if the fix helps her or moves her bay 400 metres away, who would have shown up if anyone had thought to knock. The accessibility liaison exists to knock. There isn't one.

The bike lane goes in this autumn. The new bay comes "in a future phase." On the two blocks with no dropped kerb, a wheelchair user leaving the interchange will roll to the edge, find the 100mm lip of the kerb, and turn 150 metres out of the way to get around it. She will do it without complaint. She has had practice. So have I. That is the part no audit catches — how good we've all gotten at taking the long way and smiling.
