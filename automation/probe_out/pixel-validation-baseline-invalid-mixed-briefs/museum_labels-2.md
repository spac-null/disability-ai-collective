A wall label is a rule written once that runs for every visitor who stops in front of it. The visitor who needs it most is the one who cannot ask the room to repeat itself. This month [Calder Gardens in Philadelphia](https://www.calder.org/visit/calder-gardens) opened its doors with no labels at all, branding the emptiness "open to interpretation," and a lot of smart people are calling that generous.

I want to look at what the emptiness actually replaces.

Here is the fact I keep coming back to. A 2021 eye-tracking study at [Vienna's Belvedere](https://www.belvedere.at/) found that most visitors follow the same rhythm at a labelled work: art, then label, then art again. The label is not absorbed into the picture. It is a separate object you go to and come back from. Same study: labels at eye level got read far more than labels above or below. So the label is a thing you can find, on your own schedule, and return to.

Return to. That is the whole point.

I read like that. Not because I am slow — because I get the room three seconds late and I am always doing two jobs at once. I watch the painting. I watch whether the group beside me is about to move. A printed sentence sits still while I do both. I can look away and look back and it has not changed. It does not lower its voice when I turn my head.

The [Royal Ontario Museum in Toronto](https://www.rom.on.ca/) is keeping its labels but making them shorter. Their interpretation lead, Juline Chevalier, said they now write "knowing that visitors are distracted as they read." I first filed that under surrender. Then I went to look at the [Frick Pittsburgh](https://www.frickpittsburgh.org/), which had handed label-writing to community members for a Kara Walker show, and I expected mood copy where fact used to be — feelings poured into the hole where a date should go.

One guest label did the opposite. [Shaun Myers](https://www.pitt.edu/) is a University of Pittsburgh professor. He wrote about his own family, but he front-loaded a plain who-what-when that the curatorial voice tends to bury three clauses deep. It read cleaner than the label it replaced. The curator had written for other curators. The outsider wrote for a person standing there with feet that hurt.

That stopped me. The problem was never that curators write. It is that they write in a register you have to already be inside to decode, and then a museum notices nobody finishes the text and concludes the fix is to delete the reading.

So the fix on offer is to delete the reading. The average adult attention span, we are told, is now about 8.25 seconds. Calder Gardens looks at that number and removes the words. The [Cleveland Museum of Art](https://www.clevelandart.org/) looks at the same number and moves the words into a phone app called [ArtLens](https://www.clevelandart.org/artlens); a spokesperson called the printed label "the beginning of a relationship, not the end of an explanation." Both moves treat the wall as clutter.

Consider what the wall actually was. It was a channel that ran at your speed, in your eyeline, that you could re-read without asking anyone. Remove it and something has to carry the meaning instead. On a bare wall, that something is the room. It is the docent's tour you can join if you can hear it. It is the guy next to you nodding like he knows. It is other visitors signalling that this object matters or bores them — signals you now have to read from their posture and expression in real time.

[Alexander S. C. Rower](https://www.calder.org/) is the president of the Calder Foundation. He wants the bare wall precisely so nothing "mediates" the encounter. He means it as a gift. Take the print away and it is just you and the Calder, unmanaged, free.

Except an autistic visitor who needs the wall to say plainly what they are looking at now has less structure, not none. Rower is handing them the emptiness as though emptiness worked the same way for everyone. The bare wall is not a neutral experience. And the people in the room are not all receiving it the same way.

Once the print comes down the wall does not go neutral. It resets the default channel to whatever the room broadcasts loudest. A space that trusts the visitor to "just look" is trusting them to read the same social cues everyone else is reading. That is still one sense doing the work. It is still one kind of body being assumed. "Open to interpretation" quietly means open to whoever can pick up the cue the room is putting out — and that cue is not something you can re-read.

Dawn Reid Brean is the Frick's chief curator. She said something I have not stopped chewing on: "What is not being said in exhibition text is often just as revealing as what is being shared." She meant it about power — who gets left out of the story on the wall. But there is a flatter version. What is not being said is also not being said to the person who cannot receive it another way. For some of us the gap she finds revealing is just a gap.

I keep thinking about a Deaf friend, years ago, standing in front of a Rothko, writing notes to a docent to say what she saw. He kept answering about what Rothko meant. Her seeing was treated as a question someone else already held the answer to. A wall label, whatever else is wrong with it, does not do that. It sits at eye level and says the same thing to her that it says to him, and it lets her be the one deciding what it is worth.

Chevalier's museum in Toronto kept the words and made them shorter. That is not surrender. It is the one institution in this story that still assumes the visitor might want to read something twice.
