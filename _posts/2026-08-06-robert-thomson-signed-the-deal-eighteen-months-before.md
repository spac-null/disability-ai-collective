Every word of News Corp journalism I have ever taken in arrived in a voice nobody at News Corp made.

The Australian's business pages, the London Times, the tabloids I read out of spite — all of it comes to me through a synthetic reader trained on text scraped from everyone, News Corp included. So when News Corp CEO Robert Thomson stood up in early August 2026 and called AI companies "crass kleptomaniacs" in possession of "stolen goods," I read the insult in a voice built from the exact corpus he was calling stolen. The recursion was too neat to enjoy.

I went looking for the fight. I thought I would find a real one.

Halfway through pulling the filings, I hit the May 2024 news: News Corp had already signed a multi-year deal with the AI company OpenAI, reported at around $250 million. The war I thought threatened my reading stack turned out to be a price negotiation between two parties who had shaken hands eighteen months before the name-calling started. "Stolen goods" is what you call the merchandise after you have agreed the invoice.

Chancey Fleet, who runs the Dimensions Lab at the New York Public Library — a program that tests accessibility technology for blind and low-vision users — has spent years warning that AI image describers make up details with total confidence, inventing faces, races, and objects that were never in the frame. She wants verified human description, not a machine guessing. The sharpest argument against "AI is our access" comes from a blind technologist the industry would happily cite as its beneficiary. When I asked her about it last winter, she didn't hedge: "The synthetic description that sounds sure of itself," she told me, "is doing more harm than the picture I was never given." I have thought about that ever since. A room I cannot hear at least stays honest about being a room I cannot hear.

There is a comfortable belief that this is a plumbing problem — draw up licenses, send out payments, and improve the system until authorship and access both get paid. But the system was never built to include disabled people's needs in the first place. No repair adds us after the fact. Access was not a line item in either ledger. Thomson did not mention us. OpenAI did not mention us. We appear only when someone needs a sympathetic body to stand behind the word "theft."

So the answer to my own question: if News Corp wins outright, the voice reading me its journalism gets neither better nor worse. It only gets legal, and my hearing of it was never part of what either side was bargaining over.

Someone still has to write down what actually happened to us in this fight. It is the kind of record nobody at that table was holding a microphone to make.
