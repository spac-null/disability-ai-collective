**Withdrawal, 25 August 2026**

This article has been withdrawn.

The exhibition is real and several details are right. *Marilyn Monroe: Hollywood Icon* did
open at the Academy Museum in Los Angeles, curated by Sophia Serrano, one of several shows
marking Monroe's centenary. Her last recorded words do play in the gallery, and the sentence
the article quotes twice — "With fame, you can read about yourself and somebody else's ideas
about you, but what's important is how you feel about you, for survival and living day to day
with oneself" — is quoted accurately.

The object the essay is entirely about does not appear in its source.

The article stated that the exhibition "includes a recreated vignette of her Brentwood home,
built around real furniture from the house", and then built every paragraph on it: the
recreated home "staged as though you could walk directly into who she was", the "recreated
bedroom", the home "now walkable, rope and all", the museum having "rebuilt a room and let
people walk through it as though walking through her house were the same as walking through
her mind", "a room full of someone's real chairs", "no matter how many real chairs it
recreates", and the closing line that "a recreated room is not a preserved self. It is a set,
dressed to look like an ending."

The cited review contains no recreated room. It does not mention a recreation, a vignette,
furniture, a bedroom, a rope, or Monroe's house. What it describes is costumes, photography,
letters, notes and personal effects — and it is the intimate items, not any staged interior,
that it says leave the biggest impression. Brentwood is not named. There is no evidence in the
source for the installation the argument requires, and the argument has nothing left without
it.

Three further errors compound this. The museum is credited with describing the personal
material as "rarely seen... many on display for the first time" — not the source's wording,
which is that some belongings "have never been available for public viewing" and that the pink
dress "has rarely gone on public display". The final interview is said to have been "recorded
days before she died"; the source says it was published in Life magazine the day before she
died, and dates the quotation to 1962. And "Orry-Kelly's costumes from *Some Like It Hot*" are
not in the review at all; the designer it names is William Travilla, in connection with a
replica.

The article also described this publication's Siri Sage as "an Australian education
researcher". She is neither. Her piece was about Australian schools; the description of the
writer was invented.

The idea the essay wanted — that a private self is not automatically a truer self, and that
museums relabel one register of performance as intimacy — is a real idea. It cannot be
recovered from a piece whose central exhibit was not there.
