When an investment fund bought their block in 2024, the tenants of Calle Tribulete 7, in the Lavapiés district of Madrid, did everything they were supposed to do. They held meetings. They went to the tenants' union. They found a lawyer. They talked to journalists and started an Instagram account.

Then they did something else. They opened their flats and the building's shops to the public and invited musicians to play inside them. A month later they inverted it and carried the furniture out into the street, where they cooked, knitted, played chess in their dressing gowns and sat in their armchairs while a local band played a brass version of *Freed from Desire*. Leah Pattem, who has spent two years documenting the neighbourhood, called it the most beautiful act of resistance she had seen, and she is not wrong about that.

What was pressing them toward the door across the same months was rent increases and construction works aggressive enough that some of the apartments flooded.

Run those two facts next to each other and something clicks. The concert is the story. The water is the eviction.

Landlords in Spain learned a long time ago that you don't need to break the law to empty a building. You make it unlivable and let the tenants remove themselves. Aggressive construction and water that arrives through a ceiling are not accidents of scheduling; they are the mechanism. Spanish tenant unions have had a name for this for decades: *acoso inmobiliario*, property harassment. The Senate passed a motion on it, unanimously, on 19 June 2001, urging the government to legislate. Catalonia defined it in statute in 2007, in the Right to Housing Act, as conduct meant to disturb someone in the peaceful use of their home in order to force a decision they do not want to make. This is not a fresh discovery. Everyone has known for a generation.

Here is what the beautiful-resistance frame misses. Endurance is not distributed evenly. The tenant who can absorb six months of jackhammer and turn it into a living-room concert has a nervous system that lets her. The tenant with a chronic pain condition cannot sleep through the vibration coming through the wall. She does not get to make the art. She gets to leave.

Nani lives on the second floor of Tribulete 7. She runs El Elemento, a DJ collective for people with disabilities, and one of her performers, DJ Jessy, played the residents' first musical protest in the building's now-closed shoe shop, and went on to the main stage of the neighbourhood fiestas. That is the part of this resistance that actually got made: a disabled-led collective, still playing, still in the building, still in the photographs.

The construction site is the eviction notice for the body that cannot tolerate a construction site.

And so "radical creativity" quietly sorts the tenants, even when a disabled-led collective is at the centre of the resistance rather than absent from it. The ones who are still there when the cameras arrive are, by definition, the ones the harassment failed to remove. The frame photographs the survivors and calls their survival a victory. Whoever's body couldn't hold out long enough for the flooding to stop or the case to be built is not in that photograph. They were the point of the flooding.

Here is where my own argument turns on me. The answer, on this page and every other one, is not endurance but planning — the maintenance budget, the enforced law, the fund that gets fined until it stops. I still believe it. But the tenants of Tribulete 7 did everything that argument recommends. They organised, they unionised, and with their lawyer, Alejandra Jacinto Uranga, they filed what could become Spain's first successful lawsuit against an investment fund for alleged real-estate harassment. The building's owners reject the allegation and are fighting the case, which is exactly the point: that is the plan working, on the timeline plans work on, which is slower than a body under a flooded ceiling can always afford. The concerts and the lawsuit both kept people in their homes. Whoever left before either one finished is not in the record of what worked.

I don't know what to call a victory that has that shape. What would a resistance look like that counted whoever couldn't wait out the flooding — not the ones DJ Jessy is still playing for, but the ones already gone before the case is decided — as the measure of whether we won? I have not found it. I only know the frame I have doesn't have a place to put her.
