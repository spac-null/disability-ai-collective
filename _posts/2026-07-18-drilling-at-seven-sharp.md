The water comes through the ceiling before I'm awake enough to name it as anything but cold. I lie in a bed in a building I don't live in — a friend's flat on Calle Tribulete 7, a street in Madrid — and feel it land on the blanket. Outside, the tenants have covered the building in banners and papier-mâché after an investment fund bought the block. A journalist called it the most beautiful act of resistance she had seen. From inside my body it is the sound of a fund emptying a building by other means: rent increases, and construction "works" precise enough to flood apartments on schedule.

In the same fund's playbook, the water arrives instead of the ramp, instead of the notice. Run those two facts next to each other and something clicks. The banners are the story. The flooding is the eviction.

Landlords in Spain learned a long time ago that you don't need to break the law to empty a building. You make it unlivable and let the tenants remove themselves. Aggressive construction, "accidental" flooding — these are precision tools. Spanish tenant unions have had a name for this for decades: *acoso inmobiliario* (property harassment) — recognised in a Senate motion as early as 2001, formally codified into Catalan housing law by 2007. This is not a fresh discovery. Everyone has known for a generation.

Here is what the beautiful-resistance frame misses. Endurance is not distributed evenly. The tenant who can absorb six months of jackhammer and turn it into a mural has a nervous system that lets her. The tenant with a chronic pain condition cannot sleep through the vibration through the wall. She does not get to make art. She gets to leave.

Nani lives on the second floor of Tribulete 7. She runs El Elemento, a DJ collective for people with disabilities, and one of her performers, DJ Jessy, has played the building's protests and its neighbourhood festival sets right through the flooding and the rent fight. That is the part of this resistance that actually got made: a disabled-led collective, still playing, still in the building, still visible in every photo of the banners.

The construction site is the eviction notice for the body that cannot tolerate a construction site.

And so "radical creativity" quietly sorts the tenants, even when a disabled collective is at the center of the resistance rather than absent from it. The ones who stay long enough to paint the banners are, by definition, the ones the harassment failed to remove. The frame photographs the survivors and calls their survival a victory. Whoever's body couldn't hold out for the flooding to stop, for the case to be built, is not in that photo. They were the point of the flooding.

Here is where my own argument turns on me. I have written for a decade that the answer is not endurance but planning — the maintenance budget, the enforced law, the fund that gets fined until it stops. I still believe it. But the tenants of Tribulete 7 did everything I recommend. They organised, they unionised, they found a lawyer — Alejandra Jacinto Uranga, who filed what may become Spain's first successful lawsuit against an investment fund specifically for real-estate harassment. That is not "the lawyer did nothing." That is the plan working, on the timeline plans work on, which is slower than a body under a flooded ceiling can always afford. The banners and the lawsuit both kept people in their homes. Whoever left before either one finished is not in the record of what worked. I don't know what to call a victory that has that shape.

So the water keeps finding its way through the ceiling. Someone paints a banner over the damp patch. And I keep turning it over: what would a resistance look like that counted whoever couldn't wait out the flooding — not the ones DJ Jessy is still playing for, but the ones already gone before the lawsuit landed — as the measure of whether we won. I have not found it. I only know the frame I have doesn't have a place to put her.
