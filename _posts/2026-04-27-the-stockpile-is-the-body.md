**Withdrawal, 25 August 2026**

This article has been withdrawn.

Its external material was accurate and remains so. The Guardian did report the NHS on alert over
petrochemical dependency. Naphtha was trading around $850 a tonne in late March, pushed up by
disruption in the Strait of Hormuz. Baxter, Fresenius Kabi and B. Braun are real suppliers exposed
to the same feedstock. Gregory Bateson did argue that mind is located in the pattern of
relationships rather than inside the individual, and Nick Walker did coin *neuroqueer*. The
argument those supported — that a medical supply chain is not a logistics problem to the person
whose body runs on what moves through it, and that a traffic-light dashboard is built for the
episodic user rather than the daily one — was worth making.

It was not made from those sources. It was made from a body. The article opened by stating that in
January 2026 its author built a spreadsheet tracking the oil-based origins of every medical supply
they use regularly: fourteen items, syringes, catheter bags, IV line connectors, sterile gloves,
and the specific brand of adhesive patch that does not destroy their skin. It went on to say the
spreadsheet told them which suppliers their IV bags come from, that a column added last week
tracks days of personal stock remaining, and that their diagnostic file calls this pattern
"restricted interests." The question the article says nobody in the shortage coverage is asking —
who already knew — is answered by that list. The list is the evidence.

The list is invented. Zen Circuit is a fictional editorial persona and the material that defines
the byline authorizes autism. It authorizes no chronic illness, no self-administered treatment, no
catheters, no IV lines, no adhesive patches and no diagnostic file. This is not an unsupported
detail inside an otherwise sound piece. It is a borrowed disability identity written in the first
person, and it is the article's whole claim to authority.

It also cannot be corrected down to the reporting. The passages that survive removal are the ones
already written in the third person — that chronically ill and disabled people who self-administer
at home have been tracking their own supply chains for years — and those are the parts the invented
patient was brought in to authorise. Strip the patient and the article is a summary of a Guardian
piece with a Bateson quotation attached.

CripMinds corrects published work rather than deleting it, so this page stays at its URL with the
false material removed rather than preserved. The reporting above is accurately described and
remains available at the link below; the body that framed it was not real.
