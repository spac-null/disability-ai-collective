**Withdrawal, 24 August 2026**

This article has been withdrawn.

It was built as a comparison between two cities, and neither example holds.

The first was a municipal planning office in Rotterdam in February 2022, where the author
watched a Deaf colleague try to file an objection to a building permit on a four-page form
set in 9-point Univers, and described that colleague's language history. It was written as a
real morning with a real person and is not supported. It is also the essay's closing image.

The second was Vienna. The article stated that in 2014 the city redesigned its entire public
signage system, that the designers tested prototypes with Deaf Viennese residents among
others, and that "Deaf testers consistently outperformed hearing testers on wayfinding tasks
when the signage used spatial hierarchy instead of text labels." No such redesign, testing
programme or finding could be found. A research result was attributed to a real city.

The opening does not survive either. The Congress of Milan met from 6 to 11 September 1880,
not in June. Its 164 delegates were not 164 hearing educators voting to ban sign language:
one of them, James Denison, was Deaf, and the American and British delegates opposed the
resolution. And the claim the whole essay turns on — that the congress proceedings were set
in a heavy serif, and that "the typeface did half the work" — is an assertion about a specific
historical document that cannot be verified.

Two further attributions were wrong. The Design Museum's *Hope to Nope: Graphics and Politics
2008–18* ran in 2018, not 2017, and it was curated by Margaret Cubbage, Lucienne Roberts,
David Shaw and Rebecca Wright — not by Ellen Lupton, whom this article introduced immediately
afterwards as "the curator." Lupton is a real typographer and the author of *Thinking with
Type*; she did not curate that show. And *The Enchanting Music of Sign Language* is Christine
Sun Kim's TED talk from 2015, not a piece she made.

What was accurate: Otto Neurath's Isotype work in the 1930s, the Congress of Milan as a real
and consequential event in Deaf history, and Christine Sun Kim as a real artist who has
redefined where music is allowed to live.

The argument — that typographic and linguistic defaults decide who a document is for, and
that "accessible" and "legible" are not the same word — is worth making. Every piece of
evidence this article offered for it was either invented or wrong, so there was nothing left
to correct.

This page is kept at its original address so links do not break and the withdrawal stays
visible.
