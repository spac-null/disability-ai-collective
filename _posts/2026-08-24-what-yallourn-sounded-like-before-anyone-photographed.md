I want to know what [Jesse Thompson](https://www.jessethompsonphoto.com)'s photographs of the dying Latrobe Valley coal industry cannot hold — not what they leave out by accident, but what the medium was never built to keep.

The Latrobe Valley, in southeastern Australia, has been the country's primary coal-mining region for over a century. Its power stations have supplied much of Australia's electricity, making the valley economically dependent on coal extraction and energy production.

Start with the control panel. Thompson shot the abandoned panel at Morwell power station, a major coal-fired facility that closed in 2014, and the picture is good. Dust, dead switches, a chair pushed back. What the frame cannot give you is the sound a live control room makes. The particular hum of a plant at load. The half-second lag between a fault and the alarm that a shift worker learned to read the way I read a room's reverb from the doorway.

I went in thinking this was the familiar theft — sighted people mistake the photograph for the memory, and the miner's knowledge lived in his hands and ears, not on a wall. Then I read the caption about the workers waiting for an announcement at a union meeting in Moe. For those men, the legacy is not a mood. It is whether they move away. A photograph of a scarred pit does not tell you where the next wage comes from, and neither does a field recording. My whole case about sonic memory is a luxury the man in Moe cannot spend at the bank.

Thompson's project preserves surface but loses body. That framing is only partly right. It preserves surface for people who have already left. And someone will say the picture is the point. [Wendy Farmer](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2023-02-15/latrobe-valley-coal-power-station-health-impacts/101973714) is a Newborough resident. Her grandfather, her father, and her brother all died from illnesses linked to the nearby coal plants. A photograph of her does political work no soundscape can: it puts a face to the count. The valley buries its people four years younger than the rest of the state. A recording of the plant humming is, to her, the sound of the thing that killed them.

I don't have a clean answer to that. My instinct is that the hum still belongs in the record — not as nostalgia, but because an archive built only from photographs fixes what a place looked like without recording what it felt or sounded like from inside it. Yallourn will stop operating in 2028. At that point, a hundred years of coal extraction in the region will end. A photograph-based archive will keep the light on the dredger and the rust on the sign.

The men who ran that dredger knew it by the change in vibration through the seat before any gauge moved. Nobody is recording that. It leaves when they leave.
