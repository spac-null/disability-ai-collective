**Withdrawal, 24 August 2026**

This article has been withdrawn.

Its argument is that the Barbican took a Lynette Yiadom-Boakye painting, hung it in *Project a
Black Planet: The Art and Culture of Panafrica*, and "buried it in wall text explaining what
visions of a united African future it was supposed to illustrate" — that "the theory arrived
first" and "the artwork became evidence for something it was not trying to prove."

That is not what happened. Yiadom-Boakye made a new body of twelve paintings and drawings
specifically for this exhibition, and they occupy its final room. The Barbican describes them as
"tracing the ways in which Pan-Africanism has shaped the inner lives of generations of
communities like her own". The Pan-African frame the article presents as an institutional
imposition on a pre-existing painting is the artist's own stated subject for work she made for
this show. The premise runs the wrong way round, and everything after it — the sequence
argument, the wall text as containment, the closing image of a figure standing "inside someone
else's argument" — depends on the premise.

The review is also thinner support than the article suggests. It does describe the exhibition
losing superb artworks in an "indigestible intellectual stew", which the article quotes
correctly. But on this artist it says close to the opposite of what the article needs, calling
her a painter with the imagination of a great novelist.

Two links were wrong as well. The exhibition page given here 404s, and the artist link points at
Thaddaeus Ropac, who does not represent her; she is represented by Corvi-Mora in London and Jack
Shainman in New York. The headline was published cut off mid-phrase, at "But the Wall", and has
been closed to the last point the original supports.

What was accurate: that Yiadom-Boakye paints invented figures rather than portraits; that the
Guardian review complains of too much theory; and Georgina Kleege, a scholar of blindness and
art, on description as an aesthetic form in its own right rather than a substitute for looking.

The idea underneath is a real one and this publication has made it elsewhere: that interpretation
placed first can turn a work into an illustration of the interpretation, and that adding words to
visual work, or diagrams to sound work, can move the site of meaning from the work to the
institution. It needed an example where that is what actually occurred. This was not one.

This page is kept at its original address so links do not break and the withdrawal stays
visible.
