**Withdrawal, 24 August 2026**

This article has been withdrawn.

It opens on a scene that does not exist. "A scholar in Lyon is pointing a multispectral camera
at a thirteenth-century manuscript that looks, to the naked eye, like a brown smudge on vellum.
Under ultraviolet, the smudge becomes Greek. Under infrared, an erased layer emerges beneath the
Greek — Aramaic, scraped away in the ninth century by a monk who needed parchment and did not
value what was already there."

The cited article contains none of it. There is no Greek layer, no Aramaic beneath it, no
ninth-century monk, and no palimpsest of any kind. The words Aramaic, Greek, monk, palimpsest and
Lyon do not appear in it. The author, Christina Dinh Nguyen, is a PhD student at the University of
Toronto, working with the Old Books New Science Lab and the Matrix Functionalization and
Phenotyping Lab — Canada, not France, which also undoes the article's line about "when a French
institution buys the equipment". The manuscript is a thirteenth-century Jewish manuscript held at
Toronto, and it was unreadable because of water damage, not because anyone scraped it: sixteen
wavelengths of light were used to recover the lost words. The tool list is partly invented too.
The source names microscopes, multispectral imaging and artificial intelligence; raking light and
X-ray fluorescence are this article's additions.

That is not a detail at the top of the piece. A palimpsest — a text scraped away so the surface
could be reused, with the older writing still there underneath — is the article's title, its
governing figure and its last line: "The Aramaic was there the whole time... The monk in the ninth
century scraped the parchment because he could not read what was there. We have been doing this to
people for about as long." There is no palimpsest in the source, so there is nothing left to
correct around.

Two smaller things. Siri Sage is described as "an accessibility researcher and designer"; that
writer is a Blind-rooted editorial voice on the relation between a person and a place, not a
researcher or a designer, though the piece being disagreed with is real — an article on acoustic
wayfinding published the previous month. And the dinner-party scene, with a woman "performing
interest for nineteen minutes", cannot be verified.

What was accurate: the article's publication date of 5 April 2026; book science as a field using
microscopes, multispectral imaging and AI to recover damaged texts; and Georgina Kleege, who is
blind and has spent decades arguing that her reading of a painting is not a workaround for vision
but a different and rigorous instrument in its own right.

The argument this was reaching for is a good one, and the real source supports a version of it:
that a surface which refuses to give up its data is a fact about the instrument, not the object,
and that the same work is called science when equipment does it and coping when a disabled person
does it. It did not need an invented manuscript.

visible.
