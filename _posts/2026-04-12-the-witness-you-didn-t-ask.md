A woman in Australia lost her job for reporting that her disabled clients were being harmed. The story frames her as a hero. It frames the people she reported on behalf of as "highly vulnerable." Read that phrase again. Highly vulnerable. Not "people who told her what was happening." Not "people whose testimony should have been the evidence." Vulnerable. A word that makes someone into an object you protect, not a person you believe.

Anyone who has sat through a funding review knows what that word looks like from the other side of it: a coordinator talking about you in the third person while you are in the room. She requires high-level support. Her mobility needs are complex. You say you can tell them what you need, and it goes into the file as a remark. The file is what does the deciding.

---

The problem is not weak whistleblower laws. The problem is who counts as a witness.

Susan — the pseudonym the story gives her — saw wrongdoing. She reported it. She was pushed out. That is real, and it matters. But the architecture of the story, the architecture of the law itself, treats disabled people as the scene of the crime rather than the first people who knew a crime was happening. Henri Lefebvre, a theorist of urban space and power, argued in 1974 that space is not neutral — it is produced by whoever holds power over it. The same is true of testimony. Who gets to say what happened is decided before anyone opens their mouth, by the structure of who is believed.

Here is the part that should stop anyone reading Susan's story as a clean win. The [NDIS](https://www.ndis.gov.au) (National Disability Insurance Scheme), Australia's disability support system, has whistleblower protections that lawyers and advocates have repeatedly told parliamentary inquiries are piecemeal and out of date — protections that, on their account, silence workers, people with disability and advocates alike. Among the reforms being asked for is something as basic as allowing a disclosure to a lawyer, a union or an independent advocate. If the channel is that narrow for a worker who witnessed the harm, it is not a stretch to ask what it does to a disabled person's own account of their own harm. Same event. Same system. No clean channel for either of them.

You might say: but workers have access to documentation, to internal systems, to evidence trails. True. That is a real structural advantage. A support worker can photograph a logbook. A participant can describe what happened to them. One of these is called evidence. The other is called a complaint. The distinction is not natural. Someone built it.

There is a version of this argument that is about representation — who gets to communicate, whose language is taken seriously. I agree with most of it. But representation assumes you are already in the room, and information design assumes you have arrived. My question comes before both. If the complaint portal is online-only, and your provider controls your internet access, you are not failing to report. You are locked out of the mechanism that would make your words count. Accessible information about a system you cannot physically reach is still a locked door with a very good sign on it.

There are patterns a participant can see that no worker's report will ever capture: the rhythm of how a shift worker speaks to you when no one else is present, the specific way a meal is late every third Tuesday in a way that maps to a staffing rotation. Not because workers are negligent. Because those patterns are only visible from the inside, and no reporting system was built to ask the person who's actually living them.

This is not about Susan being wrong. Susan did something costly and brave. The question is why the system required Susan at all. Why was her account the lever? Why was a disabled person's own testimony not enough to move the machine?

I keep thinking about a piece Anna Hamilton published in 2022 on the [Disability Visibility Project](https://disabilityvisibilityproject.com/) — the platform founded by the late Alice Wong — [asking whether, two years into the pandemic, she could still trust non-disabled people](https://disabilityvisibilityproject.com/2022/04/10/two-years-into-the-pandemic-im-not-sure-if-i-can-trust-nondisabled-people-anymore/). Her answer was that she could not, not as a group. My own gloss, and it is mine rather than hers, is that the question itself reveals the architecture. If your safety depends on whether someone else decides to be brave for you, you are not safe. You are lucky.

Lucky is not a system. Lucky is what you call it when the worker happens to care, happens to notice, happens to risk her job. Build a system that needs luck and you have built a roulette wheel and called it protection.

A review like that ends with a support plan nobody in the room asked for, written by the people who will not be living inside it. The plan the participant would have written is not a document the system has a slot for.
