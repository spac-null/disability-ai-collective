Shabin Shaji won his employment tribunal case this year. Swan Care Solutions Ltd had recruited him from India on a Health and Care Worker visa, sponsoring him for a job that, on paper, guaranteed forty hours a week and £22,880 a year. He arrived with a computer science degree, no family in the UK, and £17,000 already spent — paid to a recruitment agent he'd found through a YouTube influencer. Over the following year he was given zero hours. Not reduced hours. Zero. He was told to wait his turn. He was directed to food banks. His visa let him work up to twenty hours a week for someone else, which is not enough to live on and is exactly the amount of freedom a sponsor can afford to grant before the leash goes slack. The tribunal found unlawful deduction of wages, failure to provide a written contract, and a failure to comply with the ACAS code that added a 20 percent uplift to both the wages and the holiday pay. It awarded him £28,843.53, plus £8,700 in costs.

I want to sit on the number zero, because it is doing more work than "underpaid" ever could. Underpaid implies a wage that fell short. Zero is not a shortfall. Zero is a certificate — the actual legal document that brought him across a border, that named a job, a salary, a schedule — being honoured by the company that signed it only as far as it let them sponsor a visa. The paperwork was real. The forty hours were not. A system built entirely on documents discovered it did not need to keep the part of the document that cost it money.

I recognise this inversion. Somewhere, a form defines what a person is owed. Somewhere else, a person is living on whatever the form's author decided to actually deliver. The gap between the two is not an accident. It is the design. A sponsorship visa ties a worker's legal right to remain in the country to the employer who is failing him. Leave, and the status goes with you. Stay, and you are betting your ability to eat on a company that has already shown you what its promises are worth.

This is not metaphor. This is accounting. Twenty thousand four hundred pounds in unpaid wages, over more than a year, is not a clerical gap. It is a company calculating that the paperwork was cheaper to maintain than the job it described.

He reported it. Three times, to three different bodies built, on paper, to catch exactly this — the Home Office, the Gangmasters and Labour Abuse Authority, Action Fraud. None of them answered him. He found the organisation that eventually won his case, Work Rights Centre, through a Google search, seven months into a year with no income. The systems meant to see him did not see him until a tribunal forced them to look.

There is a detail in the judgment I keep returning to. Partway through the case, a dismissal letter surfaced — dated December 2023, disclosed more than two years later. The tribunal found it had no proof it was ever sent, and that it contradicted the company's own account of events. A letter that did not exist when it was dated came to exist when the company needed a paper trail. It was fabricated for the same reason the original certificate was honoured only on paper: in this system, what is written down is what is real, and what happens to the person is a separate, lower-priority fact.

"I was broke and had to rely on charity," Shaji said afterward. "I drank tap water and bought bread close to its expiration date to survive." That sentence is the whole argument, stated plainer than I could state it. Bread rationed by its expiry date is a person doing arithmetic a functioning wage should have made unnecessary.

The Home Office had already revoked Swan Care Solutions' sponsorship licence once, in 2024, for exactly this pattern — treating guaranteed hours as optional. Between July 2022 and December 2024, more than 470 care-sector sponsor licences were revoked, affecting more than 39,000 workers who had been sponsored under them. When a licence is pulled, the worker's legal status is tied to it and goes with it. The penalty for the company's fraud lands first, and hardest, on the people it defrauded.

The tribunal gave Mr Shaji what he was owed. It could not give him back the year, the £17,000, or the meals he skipped waiting for a shift that was never coming.

This is one case in a pattern. One WorkWell pilot site, NHS Lancashire and South Cumbria, recruited 28 of the 5,000 participants it was targeting — 0.56 percent — as reported by Disability News Service. Recruitment varied widely across the sites; the best two reached about 30 percent. The employer sees input costs. The scheme counts the people who did not enrol. The person is absent from both calculations.
