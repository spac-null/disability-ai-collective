Picture two people at the same employer. I am constructing them; neither is someone I watched. One stands a shift in a gallery where the exhibits are on display, swallowing dry, waiting for a colleague to come and relieve her so she can go and drink. The other opens the style guide and spends the better part of an hour on the spacing of a single label, closing a gap between two letters by half a point because at reading distance it looked loose. She saves the file. She goes home proud, and she is right to be. The label is beautiful.

Neither woman is doing anything wrong. That is the whole problem.

On 23 July 2026 the PCS union opened a strike ballot covering more than 300 workers at four V&A sites. The grievances are short and physical. Pay, and accreditation as a London living wage employer. Heat — the union wants public areas closed when the temperature passes 30C, and heat mitigation for back-of-house staff. And water: it wants staff at V&A East Storehouse allowed to carry and drink water while working in areas where exhibits are on display, instead of waiting for another worker to come and relieve them so they can go for a drink. PCS's Nick Marro puts it plainly — management "refuses to let staff carry or drink water while they work."

I have read that ballot notice four times and it still doesn't parse. An institution that can adjust letter spacing to the half-point cannot draft a sentence that lets a human being drink water in a heatwave. And those two facts come from the same building, the same budget, the same design department that files everything under "human-centred."

I keep thinking about the gap between the two women. Same employer. One body is treated as a system to optimise. The other body is treated as a variable to control.

Here is what a designer means by human-centred. You study the person who will use the thing. You watch where their eye goes, where their hand reaches, where they get lost. You build the object around that body. The Victoria and Albert Museum is genuinely brilliant at this. Their wall labels rank among the best in the world. The line lengths are set so your eye does not fall off the edge. The type size assumes a real person at a real distance, tired, on their second hour. This is not decoration. This is care, made concrete, at the scale of a paragraph.

And then the same discipline stops dead at the ankle of the person standing next to the label for eight hours.

The gallery guard is not a user. She is furniture. She has been pulled out of the layout the way a typesetter pulls an orphaned word — a single lonely word stranded at the top of a column — off the page. Tidy the arrangement. Remove the orphan. Nobody notices the body that used to be there.

I am staying inside, in the grammar the museum actually speaks — the signage, the staff handbook, the label — because that is where the lie is legible.

Think about whose body this rule cuts first. A gallery assistant with diabetes, who cannot wait for the scheduled break to manage her blood sugar, stands in a room and is told the bottle in her bag is a policy problem. A conservator with a heat-sensitive condition stands in a room the sun has been cooking through leaded glass since ten in the morning and is told the same. The rule does not name these people. It does not have to. A policy that treats water as a discretionary privilege has already decided what the normal body looks like. It is one that can wait. One that can hold. One that never surprises the schedule.

I have heard that phrase — plan ahead — my whole life, aimed at Deaf people. Book the interpreter three weeks out. Email your access needs in advance. The unspoken clause is always the same: your body is a logistics problem, and the logistics are your job, not ours. The water ban is that clause applied to the person guarding the room instead of the person walking through it.

Now the part that does not fit.

Here is the objection I cannot get past, and I have to put it against myself because no one else is in the room to put it: back the strike, absolutely, but do not call the water rule a design failure. A no-liquids rule near the objects is itself care. It is human-centred design extended to the collection — to a fifteenth-century altarpiece that a single spill would ruin forever. Recast a wage dispute as an issue of physical access and you hand management a cheap fix. They install a water station in a back corridor, issue a memo, photograph it, and the pay demand quietly evaporates while everyone applauds the new hydration policy.

That objection is right, and it is the part I cannot design my way out of. The rule that protects the altarpiece and the rule that endangers the diabetic assistant are, in some rooms, the same rule. Care for the object and care for the body are not always the same care, and pretending they are is its own kind of dishonesty. Anyone who works inside both — the fragile panel and the fragile body — would refuse to let me collapse them for a cleaner argument.

So I will leave it standing.

What I keep is smaller and harder. Design literacy is real at the Victoria and Albert Museum. That is the charge against the institution, not the alibi. An institution that cannot arrange a label without thinking about a stranger's eye has proven it knows how to think about bodies. It simply decided which bodies count as the reader and which count as the page.

There is a familiar move in which a person's seeing is treated as a question, and the answer is held to live somewhere else, with someone qualified to hold it — perception taken as raw material to be processed by an authority upstream. I think about that move every time an institution tells a body what it is allowed to need.

The gallery guard in the Cast Courts is not asking to be understood. She is asking to hold a bottle of water. The wall text beside her still says, in perfect type, that Victorian reformers put the human body at the centre of everything.

And the objection stops me here too. A water station in a back corridor and a memo to photograph is not what the ballot is for. Fold the guard's thirst into an access clause that management can satisfy on the cheap and the wage stays exactly where it is: the heat unfixed, the pay unraised, the disabled body made into the strike's most sympathetic face. What that refuses is the trade — symbolic water rights for the thing the strike is actually about.

The question it leaves me with is not whether the museum can design for the gallery guard. It is whether every argument that names her body as the wronged one ends up spending her, again, to win something else. I do not have a clean answer to that. I have the guard, still standing, and the label beside her, still beautiful, and the gap between them measured not in points but in what the institution decided, at some unrecorded meeting, was worth getting right.
