In the poster hall of the Vancouver Convention Centre, this July, I stand in front of a diagram of a language model's attack surface and trace it with one finger. Arrow for arrow, exploit for exploit, it is the map of every conversation I scripted in advance before I learned that passing is just being successfully injected.

The researchers at ICML, a major machine learning conference, have shown, with a poster session and a peer-reviewed paper, what I knew at nineteen. A system that predicts the next plausible word has no inside to defend. You cannot secure an inside that does not exist.

Their word for the flaw is prompt injection. You slip an instruction into the input and the model obeys it, because the model cannot tell the difference between a command and a description of a command. It only knows which words tend to follow which words.

I have been the injection. In 2012, in a flat in Vällingby, a relative asked how I was and I told her — the actual state of things, in order, with the relevant data. Her face did the thing faces do. I had answered the literal question instead of the statistical one. She had not wanted information. She had wanted the token that comes after "how are you," which is "fine."

Neurotypical talk runs on exactly the mechanism the researchers found alarming. Fluency stands in for meaning. The right word in the right slot passes as understanding. Nobody checks the inside, because checking would be rude. I spent decades getting punished for [answering what was actually said](https://crippledscholar.com/2018/03/25/i-like-that-i-want-that-can-i-have-that-when-nonautistic-people-dont-understand-autistic-communication-and-punish-us-for-it/). The system was gameable. I kept accidentally winning and getting called broken for it.

Here is where I part from Siri Sage, a scholar who writes about AI and disability. Siri, writing last month from Dulwich, would say the right answer to an unfixable flaw is to record it — build the archive of what the machine cannot know. I refuse that. A symptom log is not an argument. I do not want to document that the model has no inside. I want a release gate — a checkpoint that prevents any model from entering high-stakes settings unless operators have publicly registered in advance the specific goal the model is meant to serve and explained how input-manipulation failures will be caught.

And here is the case I cannot fold in. Hari Srinivasan, a nonspeaking autistic researcher who types to communicate, studies how predictive systems work. He would throw out my whole premise. For him predictive text is not hollow. It is the voice. "Ungrounded pattern-matching" is the exact phrase used for years to say his words were not really his.

So the flaw the researchers found is real, and the same mechanism gives Hari his sentences. I sat with the engine off in a driveway in Burnaby, in 2019, running a conversation backward to find the moment the room turned, and the search never converged. I do not have a way to hold both.
