# Seven minutes, 28 degrees, one phone call

Every Parisian lives within a seven-minute walk of a cool spot — somewhere to go to cool down during a heatwave, a park, a garden, a public facility — and the city counts more than 1,400 of them. When temperatures climb, Paris keeps parks, swimming pools and public baths open for longer.

Extreme heat is the deadliest climate hazard; it claims more than half a million lives every year. Part of what makes a city dangerous in a heatwave is that it holds the day inside itself. Buildings, roads and pavements absorb heat through the daylight hours and release it long into the night, so residents struggle to cool down properly. Older residents, young children, outdoor workers and lower-income communities often face the greatest risks.

This summer, mayors met in London during a heatwave that threatened to derail London Climate Action Week. Temperatures broke records across the UK and Europe, some climate talks were suspended or cancelled, and more than 14,000 people across Europe died as a result of the heat. At the same time, Paris was overseeing its own crisis response as an intense heatwave swept across France.

What Emmanuel Grégoire, Giuseppe Sala and Kate Gallego, the mayors of Paris, Milan and Phoenix, published together is a case for copying one another. Extreme heat has shaped city government in Phoenix for decades, they write, while for Paris and Milan it is only now becoming a defining challenge of urban leadership. It may strike some people as unusual, they acknowledge, that two of Europe's oldest and most prestigious cities are looking to a city in the Arizona desert for ideas. But there is not time, they argue, for each city to work out on its own what works, and the mechanism they point to is C40, a network through which nearly 100 of the world's biggest cities share climate solutions with each other.

Phoenix established one of the world's first city offices dedicated to heat response and mitigation, the mayors write. The summer of 2024 was the hottest on record there. The Office of Heat Response and Mitigation is a City Manager's Office function, created by City Council in 2021, with 6.5 staff positions. Its shade plans require 75% shade coverage in new development in areas with high pedestrian activity; the City of Phoenix states that in the hottest months shade can make up to a 30-degree difference. The Shade Phoenix plan, the city's shade action plan, allocated $60 million in federal and city funds over five years to pay for 27,000 trees and more than 500 shade structures at schools, parks, bus stops and private properties.

In the first year after the plan was adopted, Phoenix planted 7,216 trees — 1,238 of them on 56 school campuses, and 1,984 at more than 1,000 private residences in low-income neighbourhoods — and new shade covers went up at 81 bus stops. About 78% of the city's bus stops now have shade, and David Hondula, the director of the heat office, said Phoenix remains on track to have shade at every bus stop in the city where it is feasible to do so by 2030.

Phoenix also writes heat into the law of the room. Landlords are required by law to maintain a safe indoor temperature of 28C, or 82F, or lower — a legal ceiling, a number a rented interior is supposed to stay under. Companies that contract with the city must implement heat safety plans for workers. The city provides a 24-hour cooling center with wraparound support connecting people to housing, medical services and job placement, and in the summer of 2024 its coordinated heat response measures contributed to a 20% year-over-year decline in heat-related 911 calls.

Milan is building its own version of the cool spot into street space. The city is expanding Piazze Aperte — Open Squares — together with its School Streets programme, integrating cool spaces by planting trees, replacing hard surfaces with permeable green space and creating shaded areas, and the mayors credit Barcelona and Paris cool-space initiatives as the inspiration. The Municipality of Milan also runs Milano Aiuta, Milano Helps: a helpline and support service for elderly and vulnerable residents. And Milan's Air and Climate Plan sets a binding goal of limiting local temperature increases to 2C by 2050 — a target the mayors say makes Milan one of the only cities in the world to have set a temperature-related goal.

Paris brings two more instruments. It has mobilised a network of 27,000 climate volunteers to raise awareness of the climate crisis and of heatwave adaptation strategies in their communities. And in October 2023 it ran an exercise called 'Paris at 50°C', designed to test the responsiveness of the municipality and its partners and to improve planning and coordination for future heat emergency responses. The exercise involved the whole community, utilities, media and schools, and it directly inspired Barcelona to carry out the same exercise in 2027. Paris and Milan are historic European cities, the mayors note, whose streets, buildings and public spaces were designed for a different time and a different climate.

No city can simply import another city's model, the authors write, because geography, history and politics differ everywhere. By the time that sentence arrives, three cities have each put a headline number on the table, and the numbers do not measure heat; they measure reach. Paris fixes safety as a distance: seven minutes on foot to one of 1,400 cool spots. Phoenix law fixes it as a temperature held inside a room: 28C or lower, whoever is in it. Milano Aiuta makes it a channel: a call placed, by an elderly or vulnerable resident, to a service that answers.

Each of those three rules has a person written into it — someone able to walk seven minutes in a heatwave, someone for whom 28C indoors is the standard that applies, someone who can identify themselves as vulnerable and pick up a phone. The greatest risks, as the same op-ed says, fall on older residents, young children, outdoor workers and lower-income communities. Of the three rules, the Phoenix bus-stop target is the one that counts the journey itself — the wait in the sun, at the stop, on the way somewhere. It also carries its own written limit, stated in the target: every bus stop where it is feasible to do so. What differs between cities, the mayors say, is geography, history and politics; the assumption about the body — what it can walk, what it can tolerate indoors, what it can ask for — travels with the policy without being named as one of the differences.

Their ask is directed upwards. Cities are often first to experience climate impacts, the authors write, and last to receive the powers, funding and flexibility needed to respond; they call on national governments to work with cities on adaptation strategies, expand early warning systems and ease local access to finance. The pipeline they are asking to be resourced is the one already running: nearly 100 of the world's biggest cities exchanging solutions through C40, each solution arriving with its own definition of who has been reached.
