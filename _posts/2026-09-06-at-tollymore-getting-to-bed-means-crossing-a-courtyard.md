At Tollymore, the journey to bed means stepping outdoors. The bedrooms sit apart from the main living areas, so getting from one to the other means leaving a building, crossing a courtyard and entering another volume. The Belfast practice McGonigle McGrath, founded by Kieran McGonigle and Aidan McGrath, describes this as a ritual experience of the views and sounds of river, mountains and sky, whatever the weather.

The house stands on the steep north bank of the Shimna river at Newcastle, County Down, on a triangular site; the practice dates the project 2014 to 2025. A stepped concrete plinth — the raised base the buildings stand on, cast against timber boards that leave their grain in the surface — meanders down from a narrow road to the water. Six rectangular forms stand on it. Above the concrete the walls are silver-grey brick with the mortar joints finished flush to the face; locally quarried granite gravel caps the roofs. Seen from the lane the house appears in fragments through the trees, brick and concrete and gravel giving the blocks a rocky, mineral character against the Mourne Mountains. They are meant to read as the glacial stones that line the river's edge — this is a spate river, one whose level rises fast and hard after rain.

The arrangement, the studio says, helps make the house part of the landscape. In 2026 it took a RIBA National Award. The architects worked with the site's contours rather than flattening them, limiting the impact on local ecology and accommodating the risk of river flooding.

The 325 square metres are distributed across six buildings joined by courtyards and short covered passages — the circulation, the routes by which anyone gets from one room to another. Each form lightly touches the next, and at those points the corners are left open, so outdoor space runs from courtyard to courtyard, evoking water flowing around the stones in the river below. The courtyards are not decorative leftovers but integral parts of the circulation, repeatedly turning views towards the river, the trees and the mountains. You hear the river, encounter the weather, register the changing of the seasons. The residents inhabit a sequence of movements as opposed to a box, and the resulting lifestyle is not the easiest.

Each element is built up from the plinth as a single volume holding a level datum at roof level — a constant height maintained across the whole house — while the floors below follow the steps of the plinth. So as the volumes cascade down the slope with their roofline held flat, they differ in scale. Openings face generally south and west, keeping a discreet presence towards the road and opening to the river on the garden side. Inside, exposed beams, hardwood, terrazzo and lime-washed plaster make a softer environment than the exterior suggests, and the hardwood-framed windows take views either outdoors or through to other rooms.

The blocks hold two living areas, three sleeping zones, and one that can serve as either. Each volume has its own function, and all of them were designed to be flexible, to adapt to the family's changing needs and to ensure usability well into the future. The practice presents the arrangement as a model for a particular way of living in rural settings. McGonigle names what the flexibility consists of: when the children are at college and the block currently let on Airbnb stands empty, the clients can close down three of the six blocks.

So the adaptation is a subtraction. Tollymore can adapt to a smaller household by closing parts of itself, and the route from a living area to a bed stays where it is — outside, across changing levels, through the weather.
