# Except For Calls And Meetings

You are busy with something, so you go looking for the setting that makes the machine quiet. Microsoft's own support page for Teams tells you to mute notifications when you're busy or in a meeting.[^F15] The switch it gives you for that is called Mute all notifications (except for calls and meetings).[^F04] Flip it and the messages stop. The calls don't — the mute excludes calls and meetings by design.[^F06] Whatever you were busy with when you reached for the switch, the product has already decided what busy means, and it isn't that.

The matching setting sits a few lines away and faces the other direction. Show notifications during calls and meetings does what it says: notifications appear even while you're in a call or a meeting.[^F11] Microsoft describes turning it off as a choice you make for fewer interruptions.[^F12] Between them, those two switches do one job. They decide which task is defended and what is allowed to pierce it.

In the whole of that panel, the activity the product will shield on its own account is a call or a meeting. Everything else you might be doing at the machine — reading something long, writing something difficult, following a thread of reasoning you cannot drop and pick up again in a second — is assumed to survive the interruption.

It mostly doesn't. Gloria Mark, who has studied this for two decades, finds that most people can't concentrate on a single screen for more than 47 seconds, and that recovering from an interruption can take almost half an hour.[^F71] When she started, more than twenty years ago, the figure for a single screen was two and a half minutes.[^F72]

 The price of coming back is a different question, and it is not one the panel asks.

Mark's own account of why people struggle includes something the panel has no field for: some people are very good at self-regulation, and others aren't.[^F80] Microsoft can write about unequal cost when it decides to. Its keyboard shortcuts page states plainly that for people with mobility or vision disabilities, shortcuts can be easier than a touchscreen and are an essential alternative to a mouse.[^F88] That sentence prices an action by who is performing it. Everything in the notification settings prices an interruption at one flat rate, the same for every reader of the page — a cost light enough that choosing which corner it lands in is a sufficient remedy.

The meeting, of all things, is the task that gets protected because it is the work the system can see. It's on the calendar. Teams has a toggle for meeting start notifications,[^F29] and the chat for a meeting you've accepted notifies you by default.[^F27] A meeting is legible to software in a way that nothing else you do is. It cannot be declared to the product, so it cannot be defended by the product, so the product treats the time as free.

That is a design decision about whose attention is expensive. For a worker who loses two or three minutes to a banner, the arrangement is irritating. For a worker whose re-entry takes the better part of half an hour — the thread gone, the long document scrolled back through line by line for the place where the sentence broke off — the same banner is a tax levied several times a day on work nobody is counting. The cost is paid privately. The nearest thing to a defence, the global mute, lets calls through, and is available on Windows only.[^F05]

The honest version of the panel's offer is this: you may adjust how you are interrupted, and you may protect one kind of task, the kind that consists of other people talking to you in real time. For every other kind, the remedy on offer is to be the sort of person who can absorb it.

So you reach for the switch, and the parenthesis in its own name has already told you the answer. Flip it, and the call still comes through. As far as Teams is concerned, nothing was happening.
