**Withdrawal, 25 August 2026**

This article has been withdrawn.

Parts of it are accurate. Simeon Barclay's *Farewell Sweet Innocence* did show at the John
Hansard Gallery in Southampton; he had been nominated for the Turner prize a few weeks
before; the review's phrase "drab modern Britain" is quoted correctly; and the entrance is
indeed flanked by cheap white PVC doors that do not go anywhere. Admission is free.

The premise is false.

The article opened by placing the gallery "inside Southampton University's Highfield Campus",
"reached by walking through a working campus built for people who already belong there:
students with cards, staff with keys", and closed on the same claim: "Southampton's campus,
around that same free and open room, is still built for people who already have a reason to
be there." The entire essay is that comparison — a free gallery reached only by crossing a
sorting, permissioned campus.

The John Hansard Gallery is at 142–144 Above Bar Street, in Southampton city centre opposite
Guildhall Square, in the city's Cultural Quarter. It is not on Highfield Campus. It moved off
the campus years before this exhibition. There are no checkpoints, no student cards and no
campus to cross; there is a high street. The cited review does not mention a campus either.
The spatial argument describes a route that does not exist.

Other claims do not hold either. The doors are said to be "built from the same cheap
materials... as the doors on the housing estates and shopfronts he grew up around"; the review
describes them as PVC doors stencilled with Imperial Guards from Star Wars, and gives them no
such provenance. The article says Barclay "shows figures who could enter... They stand
outside" — no such work is described in the source, which lists barriers, a locked enclosure,
an empty bin, taxidermy pigeons and a Donald Duck with its legs in the air. "Neon" is not in
the source.

The subject is also recoded. The review is explicit that the show is about growing up Black in
Britain and references Windrush; Barclay calls it "a lament of sorts, to access and loss". The
article recast this as "British working-class masculinity — tracksuits, terraces", words that
appear nowhere in the review, and dropped both Windrush and the artist's own statement about
access — in an essay about access.

Finally, the article asserted that "the British planning system treats institutional
grounds... as semi-public spaces" and that "this classification matters because it legally
allows these institutions to restrict who enters and how". No such planning classification
carrying those powers was found, and none is cited.

The underlying point — that a ramp behind a locked gate is only technically accessible — is
sound and this publication has made it elsewhere. It cannot be salvaged from a piece whose
central site was somewhere else.
