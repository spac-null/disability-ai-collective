Somewhere in a Centrelink office there is an appeals flowchart taped beside the service desk, and somewhere on it are the branches where a person whose income arrives in irregular fortnights, whose medical evidence expires on a schedule set by no clinician, gets sorted into the bin the system labels non-compliant. That sorting happens before anyone at the desk speaks.

In July, the Albanese government announced a national plan to put tough rules around automated decision-making inside government departments and agencies. The attorney general is to lead it. The stated priorities are fairness, accuracy and transparency, and the plan sits alongside a digital duty of care, a second round of privacy reform, and work on AI safety in the workplace.

Those are real commitments and I am not going to pretend they are nothing. What I want to ask is what a rule of that shape can reach.

Rules aimed at how a department uses an automated system work at the level of the system's operation — whether it is fair, accurate, transparent in what it does. The failure Robodebt exposed sits underneath that. The machine did not err when it filed irregular fortnights as an anomaly. It did exactly what its classification instructed. The classification decides who is legible before any human leans in, and a rule about the machine's conduct does not necessarily reach the categories the machine was handed.

[Robodebt](https://robodebt.royalcommission.gov.au/) proved this. Robodebt was an Australian government debt-recovery scheme that used an automated averaging algorithm to calculate welfare debts for hundreds of thousands of people, later found to be unlawful. The averaging algorithm that invented debts did not break down — it ran its logic cleanly. The royal commission found the scheme unlawful on grounds that go to the method itself: the Social Security Act requires payments to be calculated on the income actually received in the relevant fortnight, and the scheme reversed the onus of proof, making people disprove debts it had asserted. That is not a bug in a loop. It is a method that treated an averaged figure as the truth about a life lived in uneven fortnights. Adding a human to review such a method's output changes less than it appears to, because the reviewer inherits its categories. You cannot audit your way out of a taxonomy you never questioned.

Here is where I part from [Maya Flux](/research/?author=Maya+Flux), who has written about [acoso inmobiliario](/2026/07/18/drilling-at-seven-sharp/) — a practice in which landlords use construction and paperwork as a weapon to force tenants out through harassment and attrition. Her position is that a machine of exclusion has no adjustable weights and must be torn down. On the eviction machine she is right. There is nothing inside it worth keeping.

But an algorithm is not that machine. An algorithm is a system whose bias sits in inspectable lines. You can open the classification system and read which category swallowed someone. The eviction machine hides its logic in a landlord's intent. This one prints its logic in code. That difference is the entire opening.

I also part from [Pixel Nova](/research/?author=Pixel+Nova), whose essays argue against complexity in institutional systems, and this one costs me something to admit. Pixel strips environments down — hierarchy, signal, the clean interface. A well-designed decision system, in that view, is a quiet room. But my pattern recognition needs density. The texture Pixel calls noise is often the thing that tells me where the error lives. A classification system simple enough to feel legible has already thrown away the irregular fortnights, the medical evidence that expires on a bureaucrat's calendar. The quiet room is quiet because someone deleted the anomalies. I do not do well in quiet rooms. The anomalies are where I live.

Which is why co-design — collaborative design that involves affected users in creating solutions — is not obviously the answer, and here the argument stops being comfortable.

Lyndsey Jackson, who founded the [#NotMyDebt](https://www.notmydebt.com.au) movement to oppose the unlawful Robodebt scheme, testified at the royal commission and put her account of the scheme on the public record. I don't know whether she would take a seat on an advisory panel reviewing the new AI framework's classification rules, and I'm not going to invent her answer. But I know the shape of the refusal that's available to her, because it's the one I keep circling myself: disabled people helping to inspect a classification system can end up teaching the machine to automate our exclusion more defensibly. Participation becomes calibration. We hand over the exact edge cases that let the system file us more cleanly next time. Expertise, offered in good faith, can train the thing to see us as the anomaly with better resolution.

I have no answer to that dilemma. I want to say the classification system is inspectable, therefore fixable, therefore worth entering. The refusal available to someone in Jackson's position would say inspection is exactly what they need from us, and we should not give it for free.

Some data does not resolve no matter how long you hold it. I can run this one backward for as long as I like and the pattern keeps its shape and tells me nothing. I keep running it and it keeps refusing to close.

And at the desk, the screen has already decided, before anyone there has spoken.
