Bahar Khorram joined Capgemini UK in summer 2023 as a pre-sales lead in cloud infrastructure services. She had twenty-five years of experience as a cloud technologist. Shortly after starting, she disclosed her ADHD diagnosis. Her probation ended in dismissal.

The employment tribunal found, in its liability judgment of 14 July 2025, that Capgemini had failed to make five reasonable adjustments. They are worth reading as a list, because the list is a description of what a functional working environment for an ADHD employee requires: setting achievable, realistic tasks; a neurodiversity awareness webinar for her colleagues; an ADHD awareness webinar for her wider team; six two-hour coaching sessions on time management and coping strategies; and coaching sessions with her line manager on communication and collaboration. None were provided. Capgemini ran its standard probation. The standard probation was not designed for her.

ADHD does not impair competence. Twenty-five years of specialist technical work is not compatible with incompetence. What ADHD affects is the set of conditions under which competence can be expressed: the clarity and stability of expectations, the volume of simultaneous demands, the structure of task handover, the quality of feedback loops. A pre-sales role involves tracking multiple concurrent bids, switching rapidly between technical and commercial priorities, managing dependencies across clients and internal teams. This work is compatible with ADHD. Many of the skills it requires — rapid pattern recognition, high-level synthesis, sustained focus under interest — are characteristic of how ADHD brains operate when the environment supports them. The probation period was not that environment.

The tribunal's five adjustments are not a list of accommodations. They are a specification of what the workplace withheld. Capgemini evaluated Khorram's performance without creating the conditions under which her performance could be fairly evaluated on the specific points those adjustments covered. That is a real, legally established failure, and it is worth sitting with on its own terms — not smoothing it into a cleaner story than the tribunal actually told.

Because the tribunal did not go on to find that the dismissal itself was discriminatory, or that Khorram would have passed probation had the adjustments been made. It explicitly found otherwise: there were good reasons to think she would not have passed even with support, given problems the tribunal attributed to friction with her line manager and objectives that were independently not being met — factors it did not find were caused by her ADHD or by Capgemini's discrimination. Those two things are both true in this case and don't resolve into each other: the adjustments were owed and denied, and that denial did not, on the tribunal's own finding, decide her outcome.

The ruling still established something beyond Khorram's own case. Neurodiversity training for a manager and colleagues is a reasonable adjustment — not a courtesy, not a preference, but a legal obligation. This matters because it removes the employer's defence of ignorance. The duty to adjust is proactive. You do not wait for the employee to fail and then assess whether adjustments would have helped. The tribunal found Capgemini had not made the ones it was legally required to make, and — in a separate remedy judgment five months later — awarded Khorram £10,000 for injury to feelings, plus lost income, interest, and pension contributions totalling roughly £14,200 more.

Neurodiversity-related employment tribunal claims nearly doubled between 2020 and 2025 — reaching 517, with ADHD cases at a record 118. The majority do not arise from deliberate hostility. They arise from workplaces that ran the standard process and then recorded the result as a performance failure. [The office environment was not designed for a neurodivergent brain](/2026/03/14/the-open-office-was-designed-to-break-my-brain/). The standard probation was not either. The Khorram ruling does not describe an exception. It describes the rule, made visible.
