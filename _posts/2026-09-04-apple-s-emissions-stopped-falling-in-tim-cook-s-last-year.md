As Tim Cook steps down as Apple CEO, the company points to a number. Apple has cut its global greenhouse gas emissions by more than 60 percent against its 2015 baseline, a figure it put forward in the announcement of Cook's move to executive chairman. The record has drawn praise where Big Tech usually draws complaint. A 2023 report by Stand.earth found Apple was the only major tech company at the time to have set a 100 percent renewable electricity target for its suppliers. In 2025 Greenpeace East Asia gave Apple a B+ for pushing renewable energy along its supply chains. While Google's and Microsoft's emissions have risen with the energy demands of generative AI, Apple has held its own carbon footprint flat.

That is the record as the source presents it, and in the terms it invites, it is a good one.

But look closely at the shape of the number. The anchor states it plainly: Apple's carbon pollution "shrank each year since 2021 before flatlining over the past year." The descent ran from 2021 through 2024. Then it stopped. The reduction sits at 60 percent. The goal Apple set in 2020 was 75 percent by the end of the decade. In the most recent year reported, the curve did not close that gap. It held still.

Apple describes the same year differently. The emissions figure was "holding constant from 2024 even in a year of significant business growth," the company said in a press release. Set that phrasing against the anchor's word for the same event — flatlining — and the two describe one thing. A plateau paired with growth reads as strength. The line that stopped descending is presented as a line held firm under pressure. The stall becomes an accomplishment.

This is a reading of Apple's own wording, not a charge about its intent. The press language does one thing: it puts the flat year beside rising business, so that standing still looks like effort rather than the end of a trend. Cook was consistent on that point during his tenure. "I want to see that it pencils out," he told CBS News in 2023 of the company's renewable energy investments, "because I want other people to copy it." Growth was never to be sacrificed. The flat year is where that commitment and the emissions curve meet.

What the source does not establish is worth stating once and leaving. It does not say why emissions flatlined. It gives no year-by-year figures behind the 60 percent. It does not show that the 75 percent target is now out of reach — one flat year is not a reversal. And it draws no line from AI to the stall: the concern about AI is on the page, and the plateau is on the page, but the source connects them only as a worry about the future, not as a cause already at work. The stall is recorded. Its reason is not.

That gap between what is framed and what is recorded is the whole of it. The piece casts AI as the coming test — the thing that "could upend" Cook's climate legacy, the danger still ahead. Greenpeace's Avex Li calls AI "the defining test of Apple's climate commitments," a test to be met by future reductions and new renewable investment. But Apple's own most recent figure shows the reduction has already stopped moving, in the last full year Cook ran the company. The threat to the record is written as something the next CEO will face. It is also, in the number Apple chose to publish, something the last one already left on the books.
