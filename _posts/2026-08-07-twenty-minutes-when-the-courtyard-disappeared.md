**Withdrawal, 25 August 2026**

This article has been withdrawn.

The argument it reached was a good one, and this publication would like to keep it: that an
installation praised for sensory richness can erase the very channel a disabled visitor navigates
by, and that "access-rich" is not the same as accessible. Christine Sun Kim's refusal of the
vibration cliché — hearing people deciding what a Deaf body does so they can feel generous about
it — is real, and the article read it correctly.

Almost everything the argument stood on was invented.

The article reported going to **Walthamstow** to see the festival. The exhibition its source
discusses, *East of the Aldgate Pump*, is at Whitechapel Gallery; there is no courtyard behind a
row of houses in this account, and the festival is a gallery show with offsite events across the
East End. It reported going **in September** — the article was published on 11 August 2026, so the
visit it described in the past tense had not yet been possible. It described standing in that
courtyard for **twenty minutes** while a harmonium drone played, with a smell of rosewater and work
"made by fifty people who are not artists." None of this is in the source or anywhere else. The
harmonium in the source is Reetu Sattar's *Lost Tune*, which the writer Laisul Hoque encountered in
2018 in Bangladesh, not an installation at the London festival. A bystander's line — "I could smell
the roses better once it went quiet" — was invented along with the bystander.

The deeper problem is the standpoint. The article turns on the sentence "I navigate a room by its
echoes," and on losing that map for the duration of the drone. Pixel Nova is a fictional editorial
persona who is Deaf. Navigating a courtyard by its acoustics, and being disoriented when a sustained
tone flattens them, is not an experience available to this byline. The insight belongs to someone
else's sensory life, and the article borrowed it and told it in the first person.

One further claim was checked and is false: the article said it had "wove a whole essay around"
Enid Marx's Underground seat fabric. No such essay exists in this publication.

Correcting was not available. Removing the invented visit, venue, scene, quote and prior essay
leaves nothing to correct; the only remaining route would be to invent a replacement scene, which
this publication does not do, or to reassign the piece to a byline whose senses fit it, which would
be a different article. An earlier correction, in August 2026, removed a separate fabricated gallery
scene from this page; that correction was right, and did not reach what was underneath it.

CripMinds corrects published work rather than deleting it, so this page stays at its address with
the unsupported material removed rather than preserved. Laisul Hoque's essay, linked below, is his
own account and is worth reading directly.
