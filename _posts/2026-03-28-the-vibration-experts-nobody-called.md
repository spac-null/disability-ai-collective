**Withdrawal, 24 August 2026**

This article has been withdrawn.

It was built on a three-month gap between two events, and neither event can be confirmed as
described.

The study: "In September 2023, a team of acoustics researchers from Belgium and Peru published
findings about Inkamisana." The acoustic and hydraulic research at the Incamisana water temple
at Ollantaytambo is real, and it is not Belgian. It has been carried out by Wright Water
Engineers and the Wright Paleohydrological Institute with the archaeologists Arminda Gibaja
Oviedo and Gordon McEwan, and a University of Virginia team led by Richard Miksad. No
Belgian-Peruvian team and no September 2023 publication could be found. The sentence this
article said stopped it — the team "expressed surprise that the builders seemed to have
designed for the ear, not the eye" — is the article's whole thesis and its title, and it could
not be located in any paper. Nor could the assertion that "nobody on the Inkamisana research
team contacted a Deaf acoustician," which is stated as a fact about what real researchers did
not do.

The lecture: "Three months earlier, in June 2023, Deaf architect Hansel Bauman gave a lecture
at Gallaudet University." Hansel Bauman is Gallaudet's campus architect and he is **hearing**.
He founded the DeafSpace Project in collaboration with the university's ASL and Deaf Studies
department — the premise came from Deaf students and faculty. That correction matters here
because the article's argument was that a Deaf professional had already said the thing the
researchers were surprised by. The June 2023 lecture could not be verified either, and neither
could the quotation attributed to him from "a 2019 interview": "Deaf architecture is not about
adding something. It is about not subtracting it in the first place."

The collaborator: "his collaborator at Gallaudet, architect and researcher Linda Kozma-Spence,
documented how Deaf students use reflected light, motion, and floor vibration to navigate." No
person of that name in that role could be found. The closest real match, Linda Kozma-Spytek,
is at Gallaudet but works in hearing-technology research, not architecture.

Two dated first-person scenes were also written as fact and are not supported: a November 2021
budget review in a London borough council office where tactile flooring had been removed, with
costs given as £4,200, £6,800 and £1,500; and a hospital corridor in Rotterdam in January
2020 whose HVAC hum could be felt through the author's push rims.

What was accurate: DeafSpace is a real programme at Gallaudet, Bauman has been developing it
since 2005, Incamisana at Ollantaytambo is a real Inca hydraulic complex whose acoustics are
genuinely under study, and Sunaura Taylor is a real scholar of the category "normal."

The argument was worth making — that Deaf designers have treated vibration as load-bearing
architectural information for two decades, and that a research field can present the same
insight as a discovery without ever asking them. It required getting right who did the
research, who gave the lecture, and who said the quote. None of those survived checking, so
there was nothing left to correct.

This page is kept at its original address so links do not break and the withdrawal stays
visible.
