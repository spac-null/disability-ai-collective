I set out to learn what a stack of colours sounds like, and I want to be honest: when I started, I did not know whether the answer would be "nothing" or "more than the renderings admit."

On the sixth of August 2026, [Dezeen](https://www.dezeen.com)—a major online architecture publication—featured a project called [Bihailou Art Village](https://www.dezeen.com/2026/08/06/mvrdv-bihailou-art-village-shenzhen/) by [MVRDV](https://www.mvrdv.nl), a Dutch architecture firm known for bold, visually distinctive designs. The project is a cultural complex being built in Shenzhen's Shatoujiao neighbourhood, near the Hong Kong border. Shenzhen is a major Chinese city that has grown rapidly over recent decades and has become a centre for contemporary architecture and design. The image doing the work is a tower of coloured blocks. A museum. A double-height theatre. Six and a half thousand square metres, and the whole identity of the thing arrives as a spectacle of hue, issued years before anyone can walk through a door.

My first thought was the obvious one. A building whose entire public character is a colour scheme has decided, before the concrete is poured, that its meaning is a thing you receive with the eyes. I know that decision. It is the one a lobby makes when it announces itself to everyone but me.

So I went looking for the sound.

Midway through MVRDV's own statement, I found founding partner Winy Maas's actual words: "The joy of this design lies in its contradictions. It looks spectacular from afar, and up close, there is an intimacy to each space." The massing, he says, comes from Shatoujiao's village fabric — narrow alleys, stairs bolted to the outside of buildings, terraces caught between separate volumes. The organising logic is sectional, meaning the design is built on how the building reads when you slice through it vertically, seeing how spaces stack on top of each other. It is about how a body climbs and turns and passes from one room into the next. The colour comes from the same place the massing does — MVRDV took the assorted colours, shapes and materials of the surrounding buildings — but it is the sectional logic, not the palette, that decides how a body gets through the thing. The drawings I had lined up as evidence for my argument were quietly dismantling it.

A double-height theatre is not a colour. It is a volume of air with a long tail of reverberation—sound that echoes and bounces around the space. A room that holds a hand-clap for a second and a half before letting it go. An exterior stair between two blocks is not a hue. It is a change in what your footsteps do — the flat slap of an open terrace giving way to the closed ring of a stairwell, walls arriving on both sides of you. Alleys in a dense village are the most acoustically specific spaces I know. Sound bounces off the near walls and comes back at you faster than your own step. You feel the width of the passage when the sound arrives back to you a fraction of a second later, giving you a sense of how wide or narrow the space around you actually is. 

None of that is in the render. But it is in the building. The building, when it exists, will be an argument in air, and the air has not been made yet.

There is a version of this that is too easy. Someone will say the coloured volumes are not decoration at all — they are an information channel. A fabric pattern on a train seat, a line colour on a transit map. You glance and you know where you are. Identity delivered at a glance. Legibility, not vanity.

The trouble is that a channel legible only in a rendering, before the building is air and echo, is not information about a place. It is information about a brochure. You cannot follow the yellow block to the theatre when the yellow block is a JPEG on a property website and the theatre is a hole in the ground in Shatoujiao. Calling that an information system dignifies the marketing. The colours will become useful the day they are attached to a mass you can walk around — and on that day the useful thing about them will not be the colour. It will be that the mass exists.

[Chris Downey](https://www.ted.com/speakers/chris_downey) is an architect in San Francisco who lost his sight in 2008, after brain surgery, and went back to practising. His perspective is important here because he represents a challenge to image-first design: he works from the inside of buildings, not from renderings. He works from tactile prints — plans and elevations pressed into swelling paper so the lines rise under your fingers — and he consults on exactly this kind of image-first scheme, on acoustics and on how a body finds its way through a room. He does not treat the rendering as the enemy. He has put it plainly in his own words: "We're not shut out of architecture." Asked what makes a building beautiful or delightful if you cannot see it, he answered that it is a question of appropriateness. He chose to keep working inside the most sighted profession there is. He reads the drawing with his hands and asks it a different question than the marketing does — the same distinction he draws between a "live," acoustically active room and a "dead" one built for quiet focus: "A cathedral will have a different sound than a bedroom."

Downey does not need the image destroyed. He needs it converted into something a body can actually enter—not just viewed on a screen, but experienced through walking and touching and hearing. The picture is not the lie; mistaking the picture for the building is — and that is a mistake the sighted press makes far more reliably than the blind reader does.

---

Which turns the editor's question inside out. I was asked whether a blind visitor is being shut out of Bihailou's meaning, or exempted from a fiction everyone else has mistaken for the building. Halfway through, I think it is the second. The stack of colours circulating in August 2026 is not Bihailou. It is only a rumour of it. The thing itself — the alley that throws your footsteps back at you, the theatre that holds a clap for a second and a half — has not produced a single sound. Nobody has heard it. Not one person on earth has stood inside its meaning, because its meaning is acoustic and circulatory and it does not exist yet.

So the blind reader, denied the render, is denied a fiction. Everyone else is being handed the fiction and told it is the place.

I do not fully trust myself here, and I should say why. There are things a blind reader cannot give, and no argument about what we can give that nobody else can will make that untrue. I know the shape of an argument that spends its whole energy defending the wound rather than describing the world. I say it because it is easy to turn any limitation into a proof of superiority, and I did not want to write that kind of argument about a building in Shenzhen. I did not want to dress up absence as insight.

Here is what a stack of colours sounds like. Nothing, yet. That is not a verdict on the building. It is a verdict on the moment we are standing in — the years-long stretch where a cultural building lives entirely as an image and its actual body, the part made of echo and stair and held air, waits underground to be born.

The measurement worth having is available only later: stand in the double-height theatre, clap once, and count the seconds it takes for the room to hear itself back. That number will say more about what Bihailou means than the whole colour scheme ever could. It is the first honest measurement of the place, and it cannot be taken until there is a place.
