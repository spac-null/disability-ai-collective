# What the certificate was allowed to decide

Omkar Ramchandra Gond, a student from Latur in Maharashtra, filled in his application for the national undergraduate medical entrance exam. He entered himself under two categories: Other Backward Classes, and Persons with Disability. The paper behind the second was a disability certificate. It recorded hypernasality with misarticulation in a known case of repaired bilateral cleft of palate, and it put a figure on that: 45 per cent permanent disability (44 in some papers). Lower down, the application form carried a disclaimer saying that eligibility under the PwD category was only provisional, and would be governed by the medical regulator's guidelines. He cleared the exam. He was then refused admission, on the ground that his disability was quantified at 44/45 per cent.

Forty is the line. Under the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act of 2016, a disability of not less than forty per cent makes you a person with benchmark disability, and Section 32 of that Act requires government and government-aided institutions of higher education to reserve at least five per cent of their seats for exactly those people. In 2019 the body then running medical education in India — the Board of Governors of the Medical Council of India, the predecessor of the National Medical Commission — amended the regulations governing MBBS admission and replaced the old Appendix H with a new one. Clause 1(D) of the new appendix: persons with forty per cent disability or more were not eligible for the medical course.

So take a certificate in your hand and read the table. If it says 45, you are a person with benchmark disability, which is what the reserved seats are for, and you are not eligible for the course. There is a column in that table for candidates who are both eligible for the medical course and eligible for the PwD quota. It is blank.

Nobody had to write down a rule barring speech-disabled candidates from medicine. The bar is an empty cell where two conditions cross. The Supreme Court called the position absurd: under this category, nobody is rendered eligible for the five per cent quota at all.

Omkar's 45 per cent is the reason he had any claim to a reserved seat, and the reason he was told he could not take one. Same three characters, same sheet of paper, pointing both ways. What I wanted to know was what the Court put in the number's place, and whether what it put there is any less fixed.

He went first to the Bombay High Court, arguing that the medical regulator had no power to set eligibility criteria that strip away the benefits of the RPwD Act altogether. The High Court did not grant interim relief. In the Supreme Court, an interim order of 2 September 2024 directed that a seat be kept vacant and set up a medical board at Maulana Azad Medical College to assess whether this disability would actually impede the pursuit of MBBS. The board's opinion was that it would not.

The judgment came from three judges. A constitutional court looking at a claim of discrimination, they said, has to ask whether real equality exists, and not be carried away by a projection of equality on the face of things. Reading the appendix to make the percentage decisive would make it overbroad, because it treats unequals equally. Quantified disability, by itself, does not dis-entitle a candidate with benchmark disability from being considered for admission. The appeal was allowed and he was ordered admitted against the reserved seat.

The other side is not weak. The regulator's job includes keeping academic standards, professional competence and patient safety intact; the guidelines now say so in as many words, and set out, for the surgical specialties, what a candidate actually has to be able to do — fine motor skills, both hands working together, mobility, depth perception, hand-eye coordination. Those are real requirements for a person holding an instrument inside somebody else's body. They are also, every one of them, a description of a task. A percentage is not. It is a measure of how much, produced by a board, for a card.

While Omkar's case was being decided, another one was running in Delhi. Kabir Paharia, an admitted case of congenital amputation of multiple fingers on both hands and of the second and third toes of the left foot, was assessed at 42 per cent permanent disability on a certificate dated 9 December 2023 — two points over the line, and therefore a benchmark disability under the same Act. He had scored 542 out of 720, which put him at rank 176 in the PwD category, well above the cut-off. A single judge ordered a medical board at the All India Institute of Medical Sciences in New Delhi, which evaluated him and reported on 6 September 2024. On the basis of that report, the judge dismissed his petition four days later. His appeal, which also attacked a footnote to the appendix as unconstitutional, was reserved on 15 October 2024.

Then there is a report dated 28 November 2024, signed by five members of a medical board. They wrote that the current National Medical Commission guidelines needed revision, and that under those guidelines, as they stood, they were not able to declare the candidate fit to pursue MBBS. The same report recorded that the candidate's disability is permanent in nature, not likely to worsen or improve.

Five doctors sitting as the assessing authority, saying the instrument they have been handed is the wrong one and using it anyway. Two Supreme Court judgments that October had already ordered the guidelines revised, on the ground that they made no provision for functional assessment. Dr Satendra Singh, of Doctors with Disabilities: Agents of Change, has pointed to the demand for optimum use of both upper limbs, and the refusal to recognise sign language, as plain examples of ableist criteria in the guidelines; what he wants instead is individualised, evidence-based assessment of function, counting assistive technology and clinical accommodation in.

Something of that has now arrived, on the postgraduate side. Under the new guidelines for admission to postgraduate medical courses, the percentage alone will not determine whether a candidate can take a course, and no candidate is to be declared eligible or ineligible solely on the basis of disability category or percentage. An assessment board considers demonstrated functional competency, whether accommodation and assistive technology are feasible, whether the course can be completed, and patient safety — and then returns one of three answers: eligible, eligible with reasonable accommodation, ineligible.

And the paper itself has been renamed. What used to be called the Disability Certificate for these admissions is now the Eligibility Certificate, on the stated reasoning that it certifies eligibility on a functional assessment rather than merely certifying that a disability exists, or how much of it there is.

Read that alongside the requirement that still stands. To apply under the benchmark category you must have at least forty per cent disability and a valid Unique Disability Identity card. The figure must be on the card. It is simply no longer the answer.

Omkar's certificate from May 2017 has not changed a digit. What changed is what anyone is permitted to do with it.
