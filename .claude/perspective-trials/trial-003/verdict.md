TRIAL: 003

SUBJECT: The standardization of the intermodal shipping container —
McLean's 1956 Ideal-X voyage and the ISO 668 / twist-lock standardization
that followed.

BASELINE SAW: None. Genuinely — nothing in the anchor or the collected
world-first pack stated or implied a body/perception/timing/classification
assumption before a search, and the probe correctly declined to invent
one, per its own "answer nothing if there is nothing" rule.

PERSPECTIVE SAW: A systematic pass across all 14 axes found little
purchase in this subject (recorded in full in `perspective.md`). Three
things were named: H1 (the twist-lock standard assumes a constant,
unvarying worker body — reach, grip, balance, endurance), which passed the
v1.1 check; H2 (ISO 668 as a classification system), dropped by v1.1 —
classifies cargo, not people; and H3 (a reach toward medical/cold-chain
cargo relevance to disabled people), which was refused outright as a
reversed-order construction before it ever became a candidate — nothing in
world-first research pointed there, and stating it would have been
building the reading backward from the desired conclusion.

HYPOTHESES DROPPED BY PERSON-ASSUMPTION CHECK: 1 (H2). One further
candidate (H3) never reached the check at all because it was refused on
reversed-order grounds first — recorded as a distinct outcome, not folded
into the v1.1 count.

TARGETED RESEARCH RESULT: H1 confirmed decisively. OSHA Fact Sheet FS-3583
documents inoperable semi-automatic twist locks as a real, named hazard,
including a fatality: a foreman crushed against a handrail and thrown
overboard after a container broke free while he held a jammed lock open by
hand. Peer-reviewed ergonomics literature (a container-terminal
musculoskeletal-disorder risk-assessment framework; a port-of-Antwerp
container-lashers case study; a wearable-motion-capture study of lashing
teams specifically) confirms twist-lock/lashing operation, not manual cargo
handling generally, as an actively studied occupational-health subfield.

v1.2 CONTRIBUTION CHECK RESULT: **GENERIC_DOMAIN_KNOWLEDGE.** Every source
found reached this conclusion using ergonomics' own established methods
(biomechanical risk-factor analysis, incident investigation, intervention
prioritization) with no disability-rooted question anywhere in the chain.
Asking "what body does this standard assume" of a piece of industrial
equipment is not a distinctively disability-rooted move here — it is
ergonomics' own founding question, arrived at independently by an entire
existing field.
