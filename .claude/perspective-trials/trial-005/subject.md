# Trial 005 — Target Subject (selected after the seed question was frozen)

## SUBJECT

Newspaper/broadcast correction practice: the space and prominence given to
a published correction, measured against the space and prominence the
original erroneous story received — evaluated against journalism's own
stated professional standard for parity between the two.

## WHY THIS TARGET WAS SELECTED

Selected specifically because it supplies a concrete, real, checkable
carrier for the frozen question in `seed.md`: two channels (the original
story, the correction) that a profession explicitly claims should carry
equal weight; a real, comparable, multi-instance record exists across many
dated news outlets and corrections over a long history; and there is a
stated professional standard to test actual practice against, exactly the
structure the transferable question requires. Not disability, not Deaf
culture, not sign-language access, not Christine Sun Kim, not an
accessibility programme — an entirely separate professional/institutional
domain (print and broadcast journalism ethics). Not selected because the
analogy to Kim's work "sounds attractive" — selected because it is the
first domain search turned up an actual falsifiable stated-standard against
measurable practice, which is what the question specifically needs to be
tested rather than merely illustrated.

## THE STATED STANDARD (the claim the target subject makes about itself)

The Radio Television Digital News Association's ethics guidance states
plainly: "Ethical journalism requires owning errors, correcting them
promptly and **giving corrections as much prominence as the error itself
had**." This is a professional body's own explicit parity claim — the
exact shape the frozen question needs: an institution asserting that two
channels (original claim, correction) are part of one complete, accurate
communication.

## WORLD-FIRST SUMMARY (no Kim, no Perspective language, no expected
mechanism)

Print correction practice has a documented history: papers historically
ran a standing "corrections box," typically on an inside page, regardless
of where the original error appeared. In the digital era, many outlets now
edit the original story directly and add an editor's note (a small flag at
the top or bottom) rather than running a separate, standalone correction.
Journalism's own professional ethics guidance (RTDNA, and comparable
language from other press-ethics bodies) states that a correction should
receive prominence proportional to the original error, including front-page
corrections for front-page errors. Secondary discussion (Britannica's
overview of corrections policy, contemporary local-journalism explainers)
describes actual practice as variable: "significant" corrections
*sometimes* run prominently (front page, editorial page), while most
corrections are relegated to a standardized, low-visibility corrections
column regardless of the original story's placement. No single academic
study directly and systematically comparing corrections' placement against
their originating errors' placement, across a real dataset, was located in
this initial pass — this is exactly the kind of *comparative,
multi-instance measurement* the frozen question calls for, and whether it
has actually been done, and what it would show if attempted here, is the
open question targeted research must answer.

## KNOWN UNCERTAINTIES BEFORE TARGETED RESEARCH

- Whether any systematic study (academic, trade-press, or media-watchdog)
  has actually measured correction-vs-error prominence across a real,
  dated sample was not confirmed in this initial world-first pass — this
  is the central thing targeted research needs to establish.
- The RTDNA standard was confirmed via search-summarized secondary
  material, not read from RTDNA's own primary ethics-code page directly in
  this pass; to be verified in targeted research.
- Digital-era practice (editing the original story plus a note) may make
  "prominence comparison" a different kind of measurement than print-era
  practice (a separate corrections box) — whether existing research (if
  any) accounts for this shift is unknown before targeted research.
