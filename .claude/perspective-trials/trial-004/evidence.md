# Trial 004 — Targeted Research

## HYPOTHESIS 1 (selected as the strongest candidate)

**HYPOTHESIS.** Differential physical capacity to move fast, extract
oneself from a viscous trap, or be found and freed before suffocating
shaped who survived and who didn't — and no general-audience retelling of
the flood has asked the question, treating the dead as an undifferentiated
list.

**EVIDENCE SOUGHT.** A primary or contemporary record discussing capacity
to escape/move/be extracted as a differentiating factor in who died.

**EVIDENCE FOUND.** Direct, and it cuts the other way. Boston.com's
individualized 2019 profile of the 21 victims ("'There was no escape from
the wave': These are the 21 victims of the Great Boston Molasses Flood,"
drawing on contemporary 1919 reporting and investigation records) gives
circumstances for each named victim, and the pattern across them
**explicitly contradicts a differential-capacity mechanism** rather than
merely failing to confirm one:

- **Pasquale Iantosca, 10, and Maria Distasio, 10** — children gathering
  firewood/scraps near the tank (a known leak site) on a lunch break.
  Maria's brother Antonio, also a child, *did* outrun the initial surge —
  "the molasses was up around my knees and threw me down" — but survived;
  the two who died were caught differently, not because of an age-based
  capacity gap between siblings.
- **Bridget Clougherty, 65**, described as elderly — died when "the
  tremendous rush of air...pulled the house into Commercial St," the roof
  collapsing on her. A structural-collapse death, inside a building, not a
  street-escape-speed death.
- **James Lennon, 64, and Patrick Breen, 44** — city paving-yard workers
  eating lunch at a fixed office location. The source directly quotes
  investigators: **"they never had a chance to escape so suddenly did
  disaster strike."**
- **Peter Shaughnessy, 18**, a teamster — young, mobile, with a horse and
  wagon (maximal available speed/mobility for the period, relative to
  someone on foot). His horse died too; "the wagon was found wrecked." A
  young, fit, horse-mounted worker died exactly as the elderly, the
  children, and the fixed-location office workers did.
- The source's own summary line: **"Running in it was impossible. Snared
  in its flood was to be stifled."** — stated as a categorical fact about
  the wave and the substance, not conditioned on who was caught in it.

**SOURCE STATUS.** Secondary (2019 retrospective journalism) drawing on and
quoting contemporary 1919 investigation language ("they never had a chance
to escape") — not the Ogden hearing record itself, which was not located
or read directly in this pass. Sufficient to establish the pattern; not a
primary legal document.

**SUPPORTS / WEAKENS / KILLS: KILLS.** This is not an absence of evidence —
it is evidence that directly contradicts the hypothesis's premise. The
disaster's own contemporary investigators and the individualized victim
record describe the event as one where physical capacity did not
meaningfully differentiate outcomes: a fit young man with a horse died the
same way an elderly woman inside her own house died and children on foot
died — the mechanism (a 35 mph wave with no warning, followed by a
substance in which "running... was impossible" for anyone) was fast and
total enough that the differential-capacity mechanism this hypothesis
proposed does not appear to have operated. Per Calibration Case 1's logic
in reverse: this is not a record about a neighboring subject wrongly
adjacent to this one — it is a record about exactly this subject that
happens to refute the specific mechanism proposed, which is a legitimate
and clean kill, not a research gap.
