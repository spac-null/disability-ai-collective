**The Number That Went Out Before It Was True**

Twenty-seven minutes after the ground stopped moving near Van, in eastern
Turkey, on October 23, 2011, a number appeared on screens in emergency
operations centers, aid agencies, and newsrooms around the world. It came
from a system called PAGER, run by the U.S. Geological Survey, and it was
red — the color the system reserves for earthquakes expected to need an
international response. Red means: this could be very bad.

It was not, in the end, quite as bad as that first number said. A day
later, once more data had come in, the alert was revised down, closer to
what the losses actually turned out to be. For about a day, the world's
best fast guess about Van was wrong, and it went out anyway — on purpose.

PAGER — the Prompt Assessment of Global Earthquakes for Response — exists
because of a different disaster. The USGS built it starting in 2005, the
year after the Indian Ocean tsunami, in part because no one had a fast
way to say, within the first hour, how many people a given earthquake was
likely to hurt. Since 2010, PAGER has issued a public alert for every
earthquake large enough to matter, usually within about half an hour. It
estimates deaths. It estimates economic damage. It does this before
anyone can possibly know either number for certain.

That is not a flaw the system's engineers are trying to fix. It is close
to the point. PAGER's own published explanation of how it works states
plainly: "Demanding or awaiting observations or loss estimates with a
high level of accuracy may delay response and increase the loss." Waiting
for a good number is not free. People who need help do not benefit from a
more accurate death toll arriving a day after the help does.

So PAGER does something unusual for an instrument that produces a single
official-looking number: it tells you, in the alert itself, how likely
that number is to be wrong. Each color-coded alert carries a small
histogram — a spread of probabilities across the neighboring alert
levels — so a red alert doesn't just say "bad." It shows how confident
the system is that "bad" is the right word, and how plausible
"catastrophic" or "moderate" would also have been, given what was known
twenty-seven minutes in. The agency tells the people who use these alerts
plainly: account for the uncertainty, and always check back for the
latest version, because the one in front of you might not be it for long.

Van was the system working as designed, and also the system being wrong.
A separate, in-country estimate, built on better local source data and
prediction models tuned to that specific area rather than PAGER's
broader, country-level models, turned out to have been closer to the true
toll from earlier on. That is a second, quieter finding in PAGER's own
retrospective on the event: sometimes the more accurate number already
exists, just not inside the system that reaches the world's aid agencies
first.

There is exactly one place PAGER deliberately slows itself down. Green
and yellow alerts, the routine, lower-stakes majority, go out the moment
the automated system produces them. The rare orange or red alerts, the
ones that mean a serious international response might be needed, are
held back — not for long, up to about twenty minutes, while a seismologist
on call watches the incoming data settle. It isn't a hidden delay,
either: anyone waiting on an alert during that window sees a "pending"
message instead, a heads-up that something significant may have happened
and is currently under manual review.

Their own reasoning for the pause is direct: "a minor delay for very
critical events outweighs the increased potential for sending out a
premature, erroneous alert." Twenty minutes, they have decided, is a
price worth paying to keep the rarest, highest-stakes alerts from being
wrong the way Van's was.

What that twenty-minute pause doesn't cover is everything it exists to
prevent once an alert has already gone out. The same engineers have
written, from their own experience running the system, that an initial
high alert later revised downward "can potentially initiate
organizational response in the absence of non-collaborating information
or revised PAGER alerts" — an aid agency or government somewhere might
already be moving on the strength of the first number before anyone tells
it the number has changed. It is a plainly stated risk, not a claim about
one event in particular. Nothing in PAGER's own account says that is what
happened at Van. It is what the system's own designers say can happen,
in general, every time a fast number turns out to have been the wrong
one — which, by the system's own design, it sometimes will be.

PAGER's engineers were never trying to answer a question about knowledge
in the abstract. They were trying to get a number to the right people
fast enough for it to matter. But the twenty minutes they were willing to
spend, and the far larger number of minutes and hours they weren't,
describe a boundary nobody drew on purpose so much as arrived at by
trial: below it, a wrong number costs too little to be worth the wait
that would have prevented it; above it, a wrong number can move an
organization before anyone can tell it to stop. Van sits on the wrong
side of that line for reasons that have nothing to do with how careful
anyone was. It is how long the ground takes to finish telling you what
happened.
