# Trial 006 — Primary Seed

## SEED QUESTION 1 (the only one used; a second was considered and set
aside — see below)

### ARTIST OBSERVATION

Christine Sun Kim, asked directly about "the role translation or
interpretation plays in your practice more broadly" (interviewer Brandon
Eng), distinguished the two as genuinely different processes, not degrees
of the same one:

> "Interpretation is simultaneous; it's happening in the moment. The gist
> is there, hopefully. The translation process gives you more time. You
> can stop and think, 'What does this term mean?' You can think about the
> context, when this happened in history, in time. With the luxury of
> time, you can create a translation with accuracy."

### SOURCE / PROVENANCE

Walker Art Center, "A Practice of Many: A Conversation with Christine Sun
Kim" (walkerart.org/reader/christine-sun-kim-on-sound-translation-and-the-space-between/),
2026. **DIRECT ARTIST INTERVIEW, MEDIATED THROUGH ASL INTERPRETATION /
ENGLISH TRANSCRIPT** — the published page identifies the piece as
"[Interpreted by Denise Kahler-Braaten and Beth Staehle]." This is not an
unmediated verbatim original; it is Kim's own answer, in her own
interview, carried into English by named interpreters. That provenance is
recorded here, not smoothed over — and it does not weaken the source: it
is Kim's direct response to a direct question, not a curator's paraphrase
of her practice (the gap Trial 005 identified).

### TRANSFERABLE QUESTION

When a system produces two different outputs about the same underlying
event — one fast and made under real-time constraint ("the gist is there,
hopefully"), one slow and made with time to verify ("with the luxury of
time, you can create [it] with accuracy") — does the system, and whoever
relies on its output, actually mark which epistemic category a given
output belongs to? Or does whichever version arrives first get treated,
by default, as simply "the record" — with the slower, more accurate
version arriving later into a position where it has to displace something
already settled, rather than into an empty space waiting to be filled?

### WHAT MAKES THIS MORE THAN A TOPIC

This is not "fast information is less accurate than slow information" (a
banal truism). Kim's specific claim is sharper: interpretation and
translation are not the *same activity at different speeds* — they are
different *kinds of claim*, each legitimate on its own terms ("the gist,
hopefully" is not a failed translation; it is what simultaneity actually
buys you, honestly named as such). The transferable question is not about
speed vs. accuracy in the abstract; it is about whether a system's own
record-keeping *distinguishes the kind of claim it is making* — and, if
not, what happens to the slower, more accurate output when it arrives
into a space the faster one has already occupied and named as settled.

### PREDICTED KIND OF EVIDENCE

A real institutional practice that formally issues two distinct outputs
for the same event — one explicitly provisional/real-time, one explicitly
reviewed/final — where it is possible to check whether the two are
institutionally marked as different kinds of claim, and what actually
happens (in public understanding, in downstream institutional decisions,
in later records) when the final version differs from the provisional one.

### FALSE MOVE

Treating any instance of "a preliminary number got revised" as sufficient.
That is Calibration Case 4's shape one level up — an accuracy fact, not a
mechanism. The mechanism requires showing that the *provisional* output
was treated, by the system or its users, as epistemically equivalent to
the *final* output — not merely that revision happened, but that nothing
in the record-keeping marked the difference in kind, so the correction had
to fight for the record rather than simply complete it.

### DISCONFIRMING SHAPE

If the target institution's provisional and final outputs are already
clearly, formally, consistently labeled as different categories
(e.g., "preliminary — automated, not reviewed" vs. "reviewed — final"),
and downstream users demonstrably treat them accordingly, the mechanism
does not hold — the system already does the distinguishing Kim's
observation asks whether anyone does.

### RELATION TO EXISTING PERSPECTIVE LIBRARY

**SHARPENS, does not duplicate.** Closest existing territory: Axis 12
(measurement — "what a system can register... what it drops") and Axis 3
(communication/mediation — "what sits between this thing and the version
that actually arrives"). Neither axis, nor any of the 16 durable
instruments, names the specific mechanism Kim's material supplies: that
*two legitimate but different kinds of claim* (fast-approximate,
slow-accurate) can be collapsed into one undifferentiated category by a
record-keeping system, with arrival order — not epistemic status —
deciding what counts as "the record." PR001-07 ("legibility enables and
constrains") is adjacent but is about presentation form, not about
whether a system distinguishes *claim types*. What the raw artist material
adds beyond the existing library: the specific, non-obvious framing that
"the gist, hopefully" is not a *defective* version of translation but a
different and equally legitimate epistemic mode — which is what makes the
transferable question about *marking the difference*, not about speed
being bad.

### A second candidate considered and set aside

Kim also described, discussing *Ghost(ed) Notes*, how musical staff lines
are officially "fixed" while people spend enormous unrecorded effort
bending around them ("You'll see the staff line move around — even
avoid — that note or that person... even though they're supposed to be
'fixed.'"). This is real and strong, but sits closer to existing
territory already covered by PR003's independence/assistance cluster and
Axis 6's "affective ledger" (hidden labor around a nominally fixed
system) — less clearly a fresh transfer than the translation/interpretation
distinction. Set aside per the "prefer one" instruction, not discarded.
