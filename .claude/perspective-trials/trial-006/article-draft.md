**Working title:** The Number That Went Out Before It Was True

---

Twenty-seven minutes after the ground stopped moving near Van, in eastern
Turkey, on October 23, 2011, a number appeared on screens in emergency
operations centers, aid agencies, and newsrooms around the world. It came
from a system called PAGER, run by the U.S. Geological Survey, and it was
red — the color the system reserves for earthquakes expected to need an
international response. Red means: this could be very bad.

It was not, in the end, quite as bad as that first number said. A day
later, once more data had come in, the alert was revised down — closer to
what the losses actually turned out to be. But for about a day, the
world's best fast guess about Van was wrong, and it went out anyway — on
purpose.

PAGER — the Prompt Assessment of Global Earthquakes for Response — exists
because of a different disaster. The USGS built it starting in 2005, the
year after the Indian Ocean tsunami, in part because no one had a fast way
to say, within the first hour, how many people a given earthquake was
likely to hurt. Since 2010,
PAGER has issued a public alert for every earthquake large enough to
matter, usually within about half an hour. It estimates deaths. It
estimates economic damage. It does this before anyone can possibly know
either number for certain.

This is not a flaw the system's engineers are trying to fix. It is close
to the point. In PAGER's own published explanation of how it works, the
USGS states plainly: "Demanding or awaiting observations or loss
estimates with a high level of accuracy may delay response and increase
the loss." Waiting for a good number, in other words, is not free. People
who need help do not benefit from a more accurate death toll arriving a
day after the help does.

So PAGER does something a little unusual for an instrument that produces
a single official-looking number: it tells you, right there in the alert,
how likely that number is to be wrong. Each color-coded alert comes with
a small histogram — a spread of probabilities across the neighboring
alert levels — so a red alert doesn't just say "bad." It shows how
confident the system actually is that "bad" is the right word, and how
plausible "catastrophic" or "moderate" would also have been, given what
was known twenty-seven minutes in. The agency's own guidance to the
people who use these alerts is correspondingly blunt: account for the
uncertainty, and always check back for the latest version, because the
one you're looking at might not be it for long.

Van was the system working as designed, and also the system being wrong.
A separate, in-country estimate, built on better local source data and
prediction models tuned to that specific area rather than PAGER's
broader, country-level models, turned out to have been closer to the true
toll from earlier on. That is a second, quieter finding buried in PAGER's
own retrospective on the event: sometimes the more accurate number
already existed, just not inside the system that reaches the world's aid
agencies first.

There is exactly one place PAGER deliberately slows itself down. Green
and yellow alerts — the routine, lower-stakes majority — go out the
moment the automated system produces them. But the rare orange or red
alerts, the ones that mean a serious international response might be
needed, are held back. Not for long: up to about twenty minutes, while a
seismologist on call watches the incoming data settle before the alert is
released. It isn't a hidden delay, either — anyone waiting on an alert
during that window sees a "pending" message instead: a heads-up that
something significant may have happened and is currently being checked
by a person, not just a program.

The USGS team's own reasoning for building in that pause is direct: "a
minor delay for very critical events outweighs the increased potential
for sending out a premature, erroneous alert." Twenty minutes, they've
decided, is a price worth paying to keep the rarest, highest-stakes alerts
from being wrong in the way Van's was.

What that twenty-minute pause doesn't cover is everything the pause is
built to prevent from happening after the alert already goes out. The
same engineers who designed it have written, from their own experience
running the system, that an initial high alert which later gets revised
downward "can potential[ly] initiate organizational response in the
absence of non-collaborating information or revised PAGER alerts" — that
is, an aid agency or government somewhere might already be moving, on the
strength of the first number, before anyone tells them the number has
changed. It's a plainly stated risk, not a claim about any one event in
particular. Nothing in PAGER's own account says that is what happened at
Van specifically. But it is what the system's own designers say can
happen, in general, every time a fast number turns out to have been the
wrong one — which, by the system's own design, it sometimes will be.

PAGER's engineers were never trying to answer a question about knowledge
in the abstract. They were trying to get a number to the right people
fast enough for it to matter. But the twenty minutes they were willing to
spend, and the far larger number of minutes and hours they weren't,
describe a boundary nobody drew on purpose so much as arrived at by
trial: below it, a wrong number costs too little to be worth the wait
that would have prevented it; above it, a wrong number can move an
organization before anyone can tell it to stop. Van sits on the wrong
side of that line for reasons that have nothing to do with how careful
anyone was. It is simply how long the ground takes to finish telling you
what happened.
