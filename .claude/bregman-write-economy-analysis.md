1. Sentence economy

  Bregman's default is short declarative → short declarative → payload sentence. His lists run to three. One modifier, not three.

  Article has this exactly right in its best moments: "The South London venue had a page. Berlage had an architecture." Bregman
  would keep this unchanged.

  But the default mode is wrong. "The physical, spatial, sensory reality of approaching a place that claims to want you inside" — 14
   words doing the work of five. Bregman: "what the building actually feels like when you walk up to it." He stacks single
  adjectives, never triples.

  The triple-repetition structure ("The page tells you which bus. The page tells you which train. The page tells you...") is also
  un-Bregman. He would write: "The page tells you which bus and which train. Not where the door is."

  3. Observation quality

  The South London building sequence — "The photograph on the website had been taken from inside a car park that was closed to the
  public" — is at Bregman's level. One concrete fact that does the work of an abstract argument.

  The fire alarm scene — "I did not understand the pattern" — is original and precise. Stronger than Bregman in this specific
  moment.

  What falls short: the Berlage passage reads like research, not lived observation. Bregman would have anchored it with the visit:
  "I stood in front of this building in heavy rain in..." That detail exists but is buried 400 words later.

  The wayfinding definition ("Directions tell you what to do. Wayfinding tells you where you are.") is textbook, not discovered.
  Bregman makes even research feel like something he couldn't believe when he found it.

  4. What Bregman does that's completely absent

  - Named antagonist turned ally — Bregman quotes the FT, quotes Friedman, quotes people who would normally oppose his argument. The
   article has zero converted opponents.
  - Temporal anchors — Bregman dates everything. "It was the fall of 2013." "On 4 April 2020." The article has "last autumn" and
  "nine years old." Bregman would write the date and the school name.
  - Direct questions to the reader — "What's going on here?" Bregman pivots with a reader question every few hundred words. The
  article never asks one. It lectures where Bregman converses.
  - Irreverence — one absurd detail per section, as trust-building. Standing outside a building for eleven minutes in the dark
  circling it is genuinely absurd. The article treats it as poignant. Bregman makes it funny first, then lets the seriousness land.
