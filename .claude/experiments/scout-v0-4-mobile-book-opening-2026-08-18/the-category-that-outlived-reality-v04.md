A state inspector can walk up to a gas turbine and see everything about it that exists in the present: the machine, the trailer underneath it, the ground it sits on. What no inspector can see is whether the turbine will still be there a year from now. For that, the state has to take someone's word.

In the summer of 2025, xAI gave Mississippi its word. The turbines the company was bringing to a new data center site outside Southaven, xAI said, would each be gone within twelve months.

The state accepted that.

At the time, there were three turbines on the property.

A year later, there were sixty-nine.

That word from xAI arrived in writing. On July 29, 2025, Jaricus Whitlock, who runs the air division at Mississippi's Department of Environmental Quality, wrote to Brent Mayo, a vice president at xAI, confirming how the turbines would be classified. Each one sat on a flatbed trailer rather than a poured foundation, which made it mobile under state rules. Each one, xAI said, would be off the site within twelve months, which made it temporary. A machine that was both mobile and temporary didn't need the permit a permanent one would.

The first fact, Whitlock's office could see for itself. The second, it could only take xAI's word for.

By early September, xAI told the state to expect seventeen turbines. By the end of October, aerial photographs — thermal imaging confirming that at least nine of them were already running — counted eighteen on site. That December, xAI told the agency nine more were on the way. The total reached twenty-seven.

On February 13, 2026, the NAACP, the Southern Environmental Law Center, and Earthjustice sent xAI formal notice that they intended to sue over those twenty-seven turbines. Six more turbines appeared in the following weeks. The count reached thirty-three.

By that May, the total had passed forty-six. Asked about the growing number, Mississippi's environmental agency said xAI did not need to notify it each time another turbine was added under the temporary-mobile designation.

No number was attached to that answer. Nothing in the record says how many turbines would have had to arrive before the state wanted to be told.

By August, one count put the total at sixty-nine.

In July 2026, the agency issued a retirement schedule for the Southaven turbines. The original deadline for them to be gone was December 31, 2026. Thirteen of the turbines were granted permission to keep running past that date. Three of those thirteen were granted more time again, enough, by the agency's own accounting, to keep them in place until roughly July 2027.

In July 2025, Mississippi had to decide what would happen over the next twelve months at a site with three turbines on it. Two years later, sixty-nine of them were still there, and three were cleared to stay on toward the middle of 2027.
