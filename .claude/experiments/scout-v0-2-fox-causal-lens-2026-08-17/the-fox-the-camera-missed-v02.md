In June 2013, a team of ecologists set fifty-six cameras across half a hectare of pine forest in North Carolina. They spaced them ten metres apart, in a tight grid, and left them running for a month. Roland Kays, Allison Hody, David Jachowski and Arielle Parsons wanted to know something that sounds almost too basic to be a research question: when an animal walks past a camera trap, does the camera actually see it?

The honest answer, once they counted, was no. Not reliably. A deer walking through the grid produced a clear, identifiable photo 86 percent of the time. A gray fox managed 29 percent. Across the four species in the study, cameras failed to produce a usable photo of a passing animal somewhere between 14 and 71 percent of the time. The single most common failure was an animal moving out of frame before the shutter caught up with it.

It would be easy to assume a camera either records an event or it doesn't, and that the misses would scatter more or less evenly across whatever happened to walk by. That assumption survives about as long as it takes to read Table 4.

Camera traps fire on a passive infrared sensor, tuned to catch a change in heat in front of the lens. Ranked by weight, the four animals in the study lined up exactly by how reliably the camera triggered: gray fox lowest, then raccoon, then coyote, then deer highest — 0.42, 0.61, 0.72, 0.91. A smaller, faster animal throws off a smaller, briefer heat signal, and is more likely to already be gone by the time a slow shutter responds. The camera wasn't failing at random. It was failing in a shape, and the shape tracked size and speed almost perfectly.

There's a fact easy to miss unless you've had to live inside it: an interpreter cannot translate simultaneously. A sentence has to land, be understood, and then be rendered — which means a Deaf audience member gets the same information as everyone else in the room, accurately, and still half a beat behind, laughing at the joke just after the room already has. Nothing was lost. Something arrived late. The same fact shows up from the other direction on a support line that only takes phone calls: the information exists, technically, and stays permanently out of reach for a Deaf caller trying to use it at the moment it's actually needed. Both cases teach the same distinction, and it's the distinction the fox now needs. "The information didn't come through" and "the information was never there" are not the same sentence — and a device that only reports what it managed to catch will happily let you forget there was ever a difference.

That's what Table 4 is actually asking the reader to hold onto. The fox was there. The camera's failure to register it isn't neutral static scattered across the data — it's shaped exactly like the animal's own size and speed, which means the miss rate and the thing being measured are quietly the same variable wearing two different names.

The paper's own authors flag the consequence in one line, easy to walk past: "This problem would be worse with rare species, which are often the target of faunal surveys." Camera-trap counts are one of the standard tools ecologists use to estimate how common or scarce a species is in a given forest. If a camera's detection rate tracks body size and speed rather than staying constant across species, a "rarity ranking" built from that count isn't only measuring how many foxes are out there. It's measuring how bad the camera is at foxes specifically — and that number gets folded into the statistics until it looks indistinguishable from population data.

None of this is a brand-new problem, exactly. Ecologists have had a name for the general category for more than two decades: "imperfect detection," a term established by MacKenzie and colleagues in 2002, with a whole modeling architecture built to let detection probability vary case by case instead of assuming it stays constant. What the Kays paper adds is narrower and sharper. This particular variable — body mass, tied to speed — isn't something that architecture corrects for by default. A 2023 follow-up, by DeWitt and Cocksedge, built an entire experimental framework just to measure it, species by species and camera model by camera model, because it still isn't a routine step. Twenty years of formal vocabulary for "we might be missing things," and the actual shape of what gets missed is still something a research team has to go looking for on purpose.

---

Underwater, nobody worries about shutter speed. A hydrophone doesn't have a frame to miss. It only needs an animal to be loud enough, at close enough range, to rise above the ambient noise floor — a physical threshold, not a behavioral one, and it turns out to sort species by almost the same variable that decided which land animals got caught on camera: how big, and how loud. A 2020 review in the Royal Society's open-access journal states the consequence without hedging: acoustic biodiversity surveys are "biased towards more gregarious, larger, louder or easily detected species leading towards erroneous richness measurements." Two completely different instruments, one built to watch and one built to listen, both quietly favoring the animal that's easiest for the instrument itself to hold onto — and both producing a "diversity" number that is partly a description of the recording equipment.

Every recording instrument has a shape: a set of things it's built to notice, and a set of things that fall outside its aperture. That shape doesn't announce itself. It just quietly becomes the record, and the record quietly becomes "what happened," until nobody handling the data remembers there was a step in between.

The forest didn't get less foxy in the weeks those cameras ran. A gray fox moving through half a hectare of loblolly pine at three in the morning left the same footprint on the world whether or not any camera caught it — the animal was real, the passage was real, and none of that depended on a sensor's opinion. What varied, camera to camera, night to night, was something else entirely: whether the fox's own body was big enough and slow enough to leave a heat signature the shutter could catch before the fox had already moved on. Somewhere on that grid, in July 2013, a fox almost certainly passed within ten metres of a lens and left nothing behind — no photo, or a blank one, filed the same as if nothing had passed there at all. The fox happened. The frame doesn't say so. Those are two different facts, and only one of them is about the fox.
