## DOMINANT READING

Two teenagers ran into the path of a tram without looking; the driver probably did not
sound the bell he was required to sound; the signage was not effective. A combination of
inattention on both sides at a badly designed crossing. The fix is better risk management,
consistent warnings, clearer signs — which is, in substance, what RAIB recommends.

This reading is correct. Nothing below contradicts it.

## DISTURBANCE / MISMATCH

Paragraph 62 states the crossing's whole safety case in one sentence:

> "Safe use of the crossing is reliant on pedestrians correctly responding to the signage
> and markings, and observing **or** hearing approaching trams."

The `or` presents looking and hearing as two routes to the same knowledge — redundancy, so
that either alone is sufficient. The rest of the report, without ever returning to that
sentence, documents that the two are not the same kind of thing at all:

- **Looking** depends on a sightline. It is a property of the place. It is there whenever
  anyone is there.
- **Hearing** depends on a person, in that second, deciding to press a button. Paragraph 49:
  outside one named location, "it is the driver's decision whether to sound the bell or the
  horn." Paragraph 53: the rules and training carry no specification of type or length, and
  RAIB's own post-accident observation found passing drivers using either device, with
  "drivers generally opting for louder and longer warnings during darkness."

So one of the two channels is a feature of the crossing and the other is an act of
authorship. The safety case treats them as interchangeable.

## PERCEPTUAL INSTRUMENT

A deafness-derived way of perceiving does not treat sound as an ambient guarantee about the
world. It treats hearing as a **channel** — something that can be off, aimed elsewhere,
too quiet, or simply not used — and it holds, as ordinary working knowledge, that
**not hearing something is not evidence that it is not there.**

That instrument does two things to this material that the dominant framing cannot:

1. It separates *emitted* from *authored*. The warning at this crossing is not a by-product of the
   machine but a message someone chooses to send: the report treats the bell and the horn,
   not the tram itself, as the thing a pedestrian is meant to hear (paragraphs 44-49, 54). The report's own evidence keeps that distinction invisible because the
   on-tram data recorder logs bell and horn "on the same channel" (paragraph 45) — the
   record cannot say which sound was made, only that a sound was.
2. It makes an inference visible. If sound were a guarantee, silence would mean no tram.
   The crossing asks everyone using it to make exactly that inference, and the inference is
   unsupported.

The instrument is causally necessary to see the mechanism. It does not require a deaf
person to appear in the piece, and none does: nothing in the report suggests either
pedestrian was deaf or hard of hearing, and the article must not suggest it.

## EVIDENCE THAT CREATES THE NEXT QUESTION

Each fact below makes the next one matter.

1. The audible warning board sits about 30 metres before the crossing. It was installed
   after a 2005 accident at this same crossing (paragraphs 46, 90).
   → *So the remedy for a previous accident here was a sign reminding a person to perform
   an act.*
2. What the sign requires is undefined: an "appropriate audible warning" of "adequate
   length" (paragraph 49); the training document repeats the same two words (paragraph 50).
   → *So the reminder does not specify what is to be done.*
3. The recorder logs bell and horn on one channel (paragraph 45).
   → *So the system cannot afterwards distinguish which of the two permitted acts occurred.*
4. On this occasion no bell was recorded at the board; the driver's hand was on the control
   and the in-cab footage cannot resolve whether it moved; RAIB concludes it is "probably
   the case" no warning was sounded (paragraph 47).
   → *So the channel carried nothing, and the record can only say so probably.*
5. The horn was recorded less than a second before impact, when the pedestrians were
   already on the crossing — "with insufficient time to act as an effective warning"
   (paragraph 48).
   → *So the channel did carry something, too late to be information.*
6. The prior occurrences fail in both directions. At Woodbourn Road tram stop in 2016 — where the two
   pedestrians had themselves crossed the tramway earlier on the same cool-down run
   (paragraph 22) — a pedestrian was struck and fatally injured and "the tram did not give an
   audible warning" (paragraph 93). At Morden Hall Park in 2008 a cyclist died although the
   driver did sound warnings, which the cyclist may not have heard through headphones
   (paragraph 91). At Saughton in 2018 a pedestrian was fatally injured although the driver
   "had used the tram's bell to sound repeated warnings," which were "not sufficiently loud
   for it to be heard and acted upon ... until it was too late" (paragraph 94).
   → *So the channel has failed when unused and when used.*
7. RAIB's 2017 recommendation, after the 2016 death, asked that all tram drivers drive to
   the same set of requirements. RAIB now states that more effective implementation of it
   "could have addressed the inconsistencies in audible warnings ... which were a factor in
   this accident" (paragraphs 100–101).
   → *So the remedy class has not changed in twenty years: specify the act better, remind
   the person better.*

## WHAT BECOMES KNOWABLE

**The crossing's safety rests on a channel whose silence carries no information, and every
remedy in twenty years has been addressed to the act rather than to the channel.**

Two pedestrians had crossed this tramway fewer than ten times and "had never encountered a
tram while crossing" (paragraph 57). Every previous crossing was silent, and every previous
silence was correct. A channel that is almost always silent, and almost always silent
truthfully, teaches the people who use it that silence is safe. It is not the failure of the
signal that produces the danger; it is the signal's reliability on all the other occasions.

That is why the remedies keep missing. If hearing is a sense, a missing warning is a lapse
of attention, and you fix it by reminding the person: install a board, tighten the rules,
train more consistently. If hearing is a channel, a missing warning is a state the channel
can be in at any time, indistinguishable from the world being empty — and no amount of
reminding changes what silence means. Twenty years of fixes have addressed the first
diagnosis. Both fatal precedents in this report involve the second.

## WORKING DISCOVERY

A crossing that offers "observing **or** hearing" is not offering two ways of knowing the
same fact. It is offering a sightline and a message. The message has no author on most
approaches, no fixed content when it has one, and no record that can distinguish its two
permitted forms. Its ordinary state is silence, and silence at this crossing is
indistinguishable between *no tram* and *a tram whose driver did not send the message*. The
crossing therefore trains its users, correctly and repeatedly, in an inference it cannot
support — and the twenty-year sequence of remedies has been aimed at the sender, because
the safety case never treated hearing as something that could simply be off.

**Mechanism family:** not FOX (nothing real is lost in a recording), not HOUR (no
calculation assigns a property), not MOBILE (no category becomes materially productive),
not Edinburgh (no word carries two meanings). This is a fourth thing: **a discretionary
signal whose ordinary state is silence teaches its audience that silence is information,
and every remedy addressed to the sender leaves that lesson intact.**

## GROUNDING BOUNDARIES

The full, binding list is `GROUNDING-BOUNDARIES.md`. In summary, the source does **not**
establish, and the article may not assert:

- Why the driver did not sound the bell, what they were thinking, or that they were at
  fault. RAIB says only that it is "probably the case" no warning was sounded.
- That either pedestrian was deaf, hard of hearing, or disabled in any way. Nothing
  suggests it.
- Any acoustic measurement not in the report: no decibel figures, no audibility distances,
  no frequencies.
- Anything about the five earlier accidents beyond what this report says about them.
- Any claim that a different remedy would have prevented this accident, or any of the
  earlier ones.
- Anything from press coverage of this accident.
