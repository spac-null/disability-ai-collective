This is a story about a word that helped build a power plant.

The word was "temporary."

Temporary is one of those words that seems to contain its own ending. A temporary job will end. A temporary bridge will eventually come down. A temporary home is somewhere you expect to leave. The word doesn't tell you exactly when something will disappear. It tells you that it isn't supposed to stay.

On July 29, 2025, that ordinary word appeared in a letter from Mississippi's air division to Brent Mayo, a vice president at xAI. Jaricus Whitlock, who runs the division, was responding to a request about the gas turbines xAI wanted to run at a new data center site outside Southaven. Each one, Whitlock wrote, sat on a flatbed trailer rather than a poured foundation, which made it mobile under state rules. And xAI intended each one to stay on site for less than twelve months, which made it temporary. Mississippi accepted both.

Four days earlier, that same word had already done its work. On July 25, Mayo had written to the state that xAI planned to bring "temporary turbines on site to be used prior to receiving the air permit," and that the company believed this was allowed.

So "temporary" was doing more than describing how long a turbine might stay. It was part of how the turbines could arrive before the ordinary permitting process was finished.

Three days after Mississippi's determination, on August 1, the turbines began arriving.

By early August, three turbines sat on the property. Within weeks, xAI told the state to expect seventeen. By the end of October, aerial photographs — thermal imaging confirming that at least nine of them were already running — counted eighteen turbines on site. That December, xAI told the agency nine more were on the way. By early February, satellite images confirmed twenty-seven, with a combined generating capacity of at least 495 megawatts.

On February 13, 2026, the NAACP, the Southern Environmental Law Center, and Earthjustice sent xAI formal notice that they intended to sue over those twenty-seven turbines. A court filing that May alleged that six more turbines had gone up after the notice, bringing the total to thirty-three.

According to later reporting, Mississippi's environmental agency had told xAI it did not need to notify the state each time it added another turbine under the temporary-mobile designation. No number came attached to that answer. Nothing in the record says how many turbines would have had to arrive before the state wanted to be told.

Then, in July 2026, the paperwork changed direction.

Mississippi issued a retirement schedule for the turbines it had once approved as temporary. They were supposed to be gone by December 31, 2026. Thirteen of them were granted permission to keep running past that date, and three of those thirteen were granted more time again, enough to keep them in place until roughly July 2027.

A year earlier, Brent Mayo had written that xAI wanted to bring temporary turbines onto the site "prior to receiving the air permit." Four days later, Mississippi agreed. The first document made room for them to arrive. The last one began counting down to when they would leave.
