This is a story about a word that helped build a power plant.

The word was "temporary."

In the summer of 2025, xAI wanted to start running gas turbines at a new data center site outside Southaven, Mississippi, before its air permit had come through. To do that legally, the turbines needed to count as something other than a permanent power plant.

On July 25, an xAI vice president named Brent Mayo wrote to the state to say the company planned to bring "temporary turbines" onto the site in the meantime, and that xAI believed this was allowed.

It was not the first time xAI had tried this. Ten days earlier, an infrastructure manager overseeing the new site had told a reporter that Southaven — often called Colossus II — would essentially be "copying and pasting" what the company had already done at its first data center, across the state line in Memphis, Tennessee, where dozens of unpermitted turbines had already drawn a legal notice of their own.

Four days after Mayo's email, Mississippi agreed. On July 29, 2025, Jaricus Whitlock, who runs the air division at the state's Department of Environmental Quality, sent xAI a written determination. The turbines would count as mobile, he wrote, because each one remained affixed to a flatbed trailer. They would count as temporary because xAI intended each one to stay on site for less than twelve months. A machine that was both mobile and temporary did not need the permit a permanent one would.

Three days later, on August 1, the turbines began arriving. xAI installed and ran them without a preconstruction or operating permit of any kind — because, under Whitlock's letter, it did not need one yet.

By early August, three turbines sat on the property. Within weeks, xAI told the state to expect seventeen. By the end of October, aerial photographs — thermal imaging confirming that at least nine of them were already running — counted eighteen turbines on site. That December, xAI told the agency nine more were on the way. By early February, satellite images confirmed twenty-seven, with a combined generating capacity of at least 495 megawatts.

On February 13, 2026, the NAACP, the Southern Environmental Law Center, and Earthjustice sent xAI formal notice that they intended to sue over those twenty-seven turbines. A court filing that May alleged that six more turbines had gone up after the notice, bringing the total to thirty-three.

According to later reporting, once the count kept climbing past that point, Mississippi's environmental agency told xAI it did not need to notify the state each time it added another turbine under the temporary-mobile designation. No number came attached to that answer. Nothing in the record says how many turbines would have had to arrive before the state wanted to be told.

In July 2026, the agency issued a retirement schedule for the turbines it had once approved as temporary. They were originally supposed to be gone by December 31, 2026. Thirteen of them were granted permission to keep running past that date, and three of those thirteen were granted more time again, until roughly July 2027.

A year earlier, Brent Mayo had asked Mississippi to let xAI bring temporary turbines onto the site. In July 2026, Mississippi was writing about the same turbines again — not about letting them in, but about when they would finally have to leave.
