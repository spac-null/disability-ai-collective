---
title: "What 'Temporary' Made Possible"
byline: Zen Circuit
draft_status: scout-v0.5.1-shadow-only
---

On July 25, 2025, an xAI vice president named Brent Mayo wrote to Mississippi's environmental regulators to describe exactly what the company planned to do. xAI intended to bring temporary gas turbines onto its new data center site outside Southaven and begin using them before it had received an air permit. In Mayo's own words, the company believed this was allowed.

It was not the first time xAI had taken this approach. Ten days earlier, an infrastructure manager overseeing the new site had told a reporter that Southaven — often called Colossus II — would essentially be "copying and pasting" what the company had already done at its first data center, across the state line in Memphis, Tennessee. There, xAI had run dozens of unpermitted turbines for months before regulators caught up, drawing a legal notice of its own. Southaven's temporary turbines were not a plan discovered after the fact. They were the plan.

Four days after Mayo's email, Mississippi answered. On July 29, 2025, Jaricus Whitlock, who runs the air division at the state's Department of Environmental Quality, sent xAI a written determination. The turbines qualified as mobile, Whitlock wrote, because each one remained affixed to a flatbed trailer. They qualified as temporary because xAI intended each one to stay on site for less than twelve months. A machine that was both mobile and temporary did not need the permit a permanent one would.

Three days later, on August 1, xAI began installing and running turbines at the site. It did so without a preconstruction or operating permit of any kind — because, under Whitlock's letter, it did not need one yet.

The category came before the machines did. "Temporary" was not a word applied to turbines that had already arrived. It was part of how xAI got them there while the ordinary process was still unfinished.

By early August, three turbines sat on the property. Within weeks, xAI told the state to expect seventeen. By the end of October, aerial photographs — thermal imaging confirming that at least nine of them were already running — counted eighteen turbines on site. That December, xAI told the agency nine more were on the way. By early February, satellite images confirmed twenty-seven, with a combined generating capacity of at least 495 megawatts.

On February 13, 2026, the NAACP, the Southern Environmental Law Center, and Earthjustice sent xAI formal notice that they intended to sue over those twenty-seven turbines. A court filing that May alleged that six more turbines had gone up after the notice, bringing the total to thirty-three.

According to later reporting, once the count kept climbing past that point, Mississippi's environmental agency told xAI it did not need to notify the state each time it added another turbine under the temporary-mobile designation. No number came attached to that answer. Nothing in the record says how many turbines would have had to arrive before the state wanted to be told.

On July 25, 2025, before any of this had happened, Mayo had written that xAI wanted to bring temporary turbines onto the site "prior to receiving the air permit." Four days later, Mississippi agreed.

A year later, the question the state was answering had changed. It was no longer deciding whether the turbines could arrive. It was deciding how much longer they could stay. In July 2026, the agency issued a retirement schedule: the turbines were originally supposed to be gone by December 31, 2026, but thirteen of them were granted permission to keep running past that date, and three of those thirteen were granted more time again, until roughly July 2027.

The first document in this story was about getting temporary turbines onto the site. The last one was about getting them off.
