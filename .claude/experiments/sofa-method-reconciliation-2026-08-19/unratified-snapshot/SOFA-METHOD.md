# Sofa Method

A method for turning a real disturbance in the world into a CripMinds article,
using a disability-derived perceptual lens to find a hidden mechanism, and
plain, sophisticated prose to carry a general reader through it.

## Pipeline

```
WORLD / SOURCE → DISTURBANCE → PERCEPTUAL LENS → DISCOVERY
→ READER CONTRACT → RESEARCH / EVIDENCE SELECTION → ARTICLE FORM
→ WRITER / PROSE → BYLINE
```

Each stage below is operational, not a checklist of required elements.

## 1. Disturbance

Do not begin from a subject or topic. Begin from something in the world or
source material that does not fit the ordinary explanation: a measurement, a
sentence, a contradiction, a document, an object, a classification, an event,
a physical process. This list is illustrative, not a closed taxonomy — do not
force new material into one of these buckets.

## 2. Perceptual Lens

Ask: what does this disability-derived way of perceiving make visible here
that the dominant framing misses? The lens must change what the disturbance
*becomes*, not just add color to it. Do not treat any persona as owning a
subject territory (mobility ≠ transportation stories, Deaf ≠ communication
stories, etc.). Do not retrofit biography merely to justify an angle.

## 3. Discovery

State internally: what hidden mechanism has become visible? It does not need
to resemble any prior benchmark. Three confirmed mechanisms, given as
calibration examples — not a menu to select from:

- **FOX** — mediation selectively loses reality (a recording system fails to
  preserve something real, based on properties of the target).
- **HOUR** — a calculation assigns a property reality never contained (an
  administrative category manufactures a fact, then is treated as if that
  fact were natural).
- **MOBILE** — a category becomes materially productive; it helps make an
  action possible rather than merely describing it after the fact.

A fourth story should be expected to surface a mechanism not on this list.

## 4. Lens ≠ Writer

Canonical separation of function:

- The **persona/lens** owns discovery — noticing the disturbance and
  surfacing the mechanism.
- The **prose writer** owns writing.

The writer must not roleplay disability, imitate supposed autistic/Deaf/
mobility/neurodivergent sentence patterns, insert biography as credential, or
write "in character" because the byline is a persona. **Byline ≠ prose
persona.** A persona name can remain the public author identity while the
prose itself follows ordinary CripMinds editorial standards.

## 5. Reader Contract

Before drafting, answer separately from the mechanism: *why should a general
intelligent reader care about this story?* This is not necessarily the same
sentence as the hidden mechanism, and it must exist before subject-level
detail (names, agencies, technical terms) is introduced.

Examples (do not copy the form, no aphorism is required):

- FOX: what happens and what reaches the record are not always the same
  thing.
- HOUR: some clocks come from the world; others are imposed by paperwork.
- MOBILE: an ordinary administrative word can help make something physical
  possible.

## 6. Opening

Opening ≠ hook. A hook earns one more sentence; an opening earns the whole
story — it should give the reader a reason to follow before asking them to
carry many unfamiliar names, dates, or institutions. Then it should supply a
concrete handhold quickly.

Valid openings include a physical scene, a person, an object, a document, a
clear proposition, a strange fact, a contradiction. There is no mandatory
form. If the opening makes a large promise, do not immediately retreat into
abstract explanation — give the reader something concrete to hold almost
immediately after.

## 7. Research / Evidence

Research must support two things: (A) the disturbance/mechanism, grounded in
real source material, and (B) enough concrete material to carry a readable
article — people, documents, measurements, objects, actions, dates, images,
interfaces, physical processes, contradictions.

Not mandatory: human testimony, a second case/world, quotes, first person, an
interview subject.

Absolute: never invent facts, quotes, experiences, scenes, or motives.

Search more only when the packet cannot yet support both (A) and (B). If both
are already present, the remaining work is selection and writing, not more
research.

## 8. Evidence Hierarchy

Do not assume the loudest fact is the story. Mobile's raw turbine count (3 →
69) was striking but weaker than the actual narrative spine: a company
proposes a temporary classification before its permit exists → the regulator
accepts it → the turbines arrive. The strongest material is often a sequence
of documents/dates, not the biggest number.

If repeated prose revisions cannot answer *why does this story exist*, stop
writing. Return to the evidence and reconsider the evidence hierarchy, the
disturbance, the perceptual discovery, and the narrative spine — in that
order of likely fault. Do not try to solve a research/selection problem by
polishing prose further.

## 9. Article Form

Form follows the material. None of the following are required: a second
world, an analogy, an interview subject, first person, an explicit disability
paragraph, a delayed thesis, a thesis in sentence one, a circular ending, a
fixed word count, a fixed paragraph count. The lens must be causally
necessary to the discovery; it is narratively optional in how visibly it
appears on the page.

## 10. Writing Standard

Make the thinking sophisticated. Make the reading easy. Easy ≠ short.

Prefer: developed paragraphs, explicit referents, concrete verbs, facts and
actions doing the intellectual work, one unfamiliar idea introduced at a
time, ordinary book prose.

Avoid: staccato simplification, academic or legal-report compression,
clever metaphors that require decoding, repeated paraphrases of the same
thesis, ambiguous pronouns (especially near an ending), explanation offered
after the reader has already understood.

Beauty should usually live in how a paragraph moves — concrete thing →
complication → new fact → consequence — not in every sentence trying to be
memorable.

## 11. Source / Quote Standard

Quotes earn their place when they add position, conflict, character,
surprise, unusual bluntness, or language paraphrase would weaken. Do not
quote technical prose merely as an authority badge — paraphrase it, and
prefer a named source-handhold ("DIFS Director Anita Fox") over an anonymous
attribution ("a report found") wherever the evidence supports naming it.

## 12. Ending

Do not restate the thesis. Land on the strongest concrete fact, object,
document, action, or unresolved absence available in the evidence. If
something from the opening can naturally return changed by the ending, use
it — but do not force a circular return where the evidence doesn't support
one.

## 13. Stop Conditions

Stop researching when the evidence packet is sufficient (Section 7). Stop
drafting when remaining changes are cosmetic. Do not endlessly polish
benchmark-level work.

If a draft is losing the reader, diagnose which layer is actually broken
before rewriting prose: writing, material, reader contract, discovery, or
form. Most failures are a wrong layer diagnosis, not a sentence-craft
problem.

## 14. Three Benchmarks (calibration, not templates)

**FOX** — Mechanism: a recording system selectively fails to preserve a real
event, based on the target's size/speed. Reader contract: what happens and
what gets recorded are not the same thing, and the gap is bigger than you
think.

**HOUR** — Mechanism: a calculation assigns "age" to labor, which never
physically aged, then treats that assigned age as natural. Reader contract:
some clocks come from the world; others are put there by paperwork.

**MOBILE** — Mechanism: a category, proposed before deployment and accepted
by the authority that could grant it, becomes materially productive — it
helps build the thing rather than just describing it afterward. Reader
contract: an ordinary administrative word can help make something physical
possible.
