Following the Nose Through Edinburgh

By the time you're done with Edinburgh art festival, the reviewer writes, you'll have uncovered so many hidden stories you'll feel like Columbo. The festival sprawls: curated displays, satellite exhibitions, performances, off-site projects, and, in the reviewer's account, it is impossible to figure out how any of it fits together. There is no map that resolves it. You've just got to follow your nose and hope you sniff out something you like. And the nose keeps turning up the same kind of thing. You can't walk into a gallery, the review says, without learning about some subcultural genius or longlost creative you've never heard of. That is the texture of moving through it — a sequence of rooms, each producing a name that arrives without preparation, unfamiliar to the visitor.

Two of those names, taken here because they can be set beside each other.

Sandra George (1957-2013) was a black photographer and photojournalist who documented life on the margins of inner city Edinburgh — protests and action groups working out of living rooms and town halls, people in hostels and housing schemes, kids playing in bleak places, herself appearing throughout in self-portraits, often with her young son. Like a lot of people in EAF, the review notes, she never showed her work publicly in her lifetime, and was only discovered after her death in 2013. Frank Walter (1926-2009), the Antiguan painter, writer, poet and polymath at the centre of the group show at Ingleby Gallery, died having never shown any of the thousands of paintings and drawings he amassed over his lifetime. Small works on ripped cardboard and fading paper, rainbows and stars and smiling faces, trees bending at weird angles.

Midway through the review comes a demand. If contemporary artists and institutions want to tell hidden stories, the reviewer argues, they have a duty — to viewers and to their subjects — to tell them clearly. It is stated as an obligation running two ways at once, and the second direction seems to me the harder one. Clarity toward viewers is a matter of craft, of handouts that don't melt the mind, of context that isn't wispy. Clarity toward subjects is something else. It asks what the telling owes to the person being told about, and that question does not settle just because the room is well hung.

Hold that beside the festival's characteristic verb. The review reaches repeatedly for discovery and its family: undiscovered archives, another largely undiscovered artist, the visitor who sniffs out something they like, the gallery-goer scolded for never having heard of this previously undiscovered marginalised artist. The word does real work in the piece. It describes the shape of an afternoon — the moment a visitor walks into City Art Gallery, or Ingleby, or Custom Lane, and meets images they had no way of anticipating. It is an event that happens to the person looking, and it happens now, in the present tense of the visit, on the reviewer's own nose-first route through a city too large to be read as a whole.

Set that against the two dates. George never showed her work publicly in her lifetime and was found after 2013. Walter died having never shown any of it. Most of Sadao Hasegawa's work, the review adds, stayed unpublished and unseen in his lifetime.

The word discovery, then, is describing the visitor's side of the encounter. It marks the moment work becomes newly visible to someone who did not know it existed. What it does not describe, and cannot, is the other span of time these dates measure: the years in which that same work went unshown while its maker was alive.
