Following the Nose Through Edinburgh

By the time you are done with the Edinburgh art festival, the reviewer writes, you will have uncovered so many hidden stories you will feel like Columbo. The line is a joke about the accumulation, but it also describes the basic condition of moving through the thing. You cannot walk into a gallery here without learning about some subcultural genius or long-lost creative you have never heard of. That is the texture of the visit, gallery after gallery.

The festival itself, in the reviewer's account, resists being held in one hand. There are curated displays, satellite exhibitions, performances, off-site projects, and it is impossible to figure out how any of it fits together. No route through it presents itself. You have just got to follow your nose and hope you sniff out something you like — which is less a strategy than an admission that the alternative, a coherent map, is not on offer. The visitor navigates by appetite. What arrives, arrives in the order the walking produces it.

Two of the artists the review takes up can be set down beside each other, not because the review ranks them or gathers them into a group, but because of what it happens to record about each. Sandra George, 1957–2013, was a black photographer and photojournalist who documented life on the margins of inner city Edinburgh, and her work is at City Art Gallery. The review notes that, like a lot of people in the festival, she never showed her work publicly in her lifetime, and was only discovered after her death in 2013. Frank Walter, 1926–2009, is the centre of the group show at Ingleby Gallery: an Antiguan painter, writer, poet and polymath who died having never shown any of the thousands of paintings and drawings he amassed over his lifetime. Two artists, two bodies of work, and in each case the same recorded fact about when the showing did not happen.

Against that, the reviewer sets an explicit demand. If contemporary artists and institutions want to tell hidden stories, the review argues, they have a duty — to viewers and to their subjects — to tell them clearly. It is a two-sided obligation, and the second side, on the reading offered here, is the one that presses. Clarity to viewers is a matter of craft, of handouts that are not mind-meltingly indecipherable, of work that is contextualised rather than left as wispy lumps and scrawls. Clarity to subjects is something else. It asks what is owed to the person whose story is being told, and it does not resolve simply by being well-lit. The demand arrives while the visit is still in progress, and it does not sit easily with a mode of encounter organised around following your nose.

Return, then, to the word the review keeps reaching for. Hidden stories. Secret histories. Undiscovered archives. The whole festival, the reviewer writes, is a celebration of the underground and unheralded, and the recurring pleasure it offers is the one named in that opening joke: uncovering. The verbs belong to the visitor. You uncover, you sniff out, you learn about the long-lost creative you have never heard of. Sandra George was only discovered after her death in 2013. Sadao Hasegawa is described as another largely undiscovered artist. The word travels easily between the visitor's afternoon and the artist's life, and it is the same word in both places.

But the facts about George and Walter fix what the word can carry. George never showed her work publicly in her lifetime. Walter died having never shown any of the thousands of paintings and drawings he amassed. Whatever the visitor is doing in the gallery at City Art Gallery or at Ingleby, it is happening now, and it is happening to a person who may never have heard of the work. What the record says about George and Walter is about a span of years that ended before the festival opened.

The review's discovery is the visitor's present encounter with work previously unknown to them. What it records about George and Walter is that the work went unshown while they were alive.
