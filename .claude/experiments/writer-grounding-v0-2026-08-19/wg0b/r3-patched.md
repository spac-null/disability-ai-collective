Following Your Nose Through Edinburgh

By the reviewer's account, the Edinburgh art festival is something you move through rather than read. There are curated displays, satellite exhibitions, performances, off-site projects, and it is impossible to figure out how any of it fits together. No map resolves it. The reviewer's method, stated plainly, is to follow your nose and hope you sniff out something you like — a way of proceeding that treats the festival as terrain rather than argument. And the terrain has a consistent weather to it: you can't walk into a gallery, the review says, without learning about some subcultural genius or long-lost creative you've never heard of. The encounter repeats. Room after room, the visitor arrives at a name they have never heard of.

Two of those names are worth setting down with their facts attached. Sandra George, the review notes, was a black photographer and photojournalist who documented life on the margins of inner city Edinburgh, and who — like a lot of people in the festival, the reviewer observes — never showed her work publicly in her lifetime, and was only discovered after her death in 2013. Frank Walter, the Antiguan painter, writer, poet and polymath at the centre of the Ingleby Gallery group show, died having never shown any of the thousands of paintings and drawings he amassed over his lifetime. Thousands. The number is the review's, and it sits there without commentary.

Against this, the reviewer's own position on the matter of hidden things: if contemporary artists and institutions want to tell hidden stories, the review argues, they have a duty — to viewers and to their subjects — to tell them clearly. It is a demand about the telling, and it arrives with two parties named in it. Viewers are one. Subjects are the other. The duty is owed in both directions at once, which means the clarity being asked for is not only a service to the person walking through the door. It also answers to whoever the story is about. Read one way, that second obligation is the harder of the two to discharge, since the subject is not in the room to say whether it has been met.

The reviewer's word for what happens in these rooms is a specific one, used more than once. Uncovered. Undiscovered. Sniffed out. You'll have uncovered so many hidden stories, the review opens, that you'll feel like Columbo — the detective's word, the finder's word, a word that puts the visitor at the moment of revelation and makes the revelation theirs. Sadao Hasegawa is described as another largely undiscovered artist. The whole festival, in the review's framing, is a celebration of the underground and unheralded. Discovery here is something that happens to a person who is walking around Edinburgh, and it happens in the present tense, on the day, in front of the work.

Set the same word beside George and Walter and it stops covering the same ground. She was only discovered after her death in 2013. He died having never shown the thousands of works he made. Whatever the word carries when the review uses it — the pleasure of the find, the sense of a story surfacing — none of that describes anything that occurred while either of them was alive. The finding happened, and it happened on one side of a line neither of them crossed back over. The word holds true for the person in the gallery. It does not describe an event in the artist's life, because in the artist's life the event did not take place.

That is where the two senses part. The review's discovery is the visitor's, present and first-hand, a story becoming known to someone who did not know it. The other fact is about the work: it stayed unshown for the whole of the life that made it.
