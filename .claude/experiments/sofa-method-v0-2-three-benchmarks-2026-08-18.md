# Sofa Method V0.2 — Extended to Three Frozen Benchmarks

Compiled 2026-08-18. V0.1 (below, Part 1) was built from two frozen benchmarks — Fox and
Hour. This version adds a third, Mobile, and records only what Mobile actually taught us
that the first two hadn't. Nothing here should harden into a mandatory structure, checklist
of required elements, or fixed word count. Three benchmarks now demonstrate three different
mechanisms, three different entrances, and three different ways the disability-derived lens
can appear — so this version becomes *less* formulaic, not more. Where Part 2 adds a rule,
it adds it as something confirmed transferable *despite* difference, never as a template
extracted from convergence that wasn't actually there.

# Part 1 — V0.1, from Two Benchmarks (Fox, Hour), preserved unchanged

## 1. What actually transferred (checked against both, not assumed)

- **Begin from a perceptual question, not a subject label.** Fox is not "about camera traps." Hour is not "about insurance." Each begins from a genuinely different question about how the disturbance is possible — Fox asks what happens when a recording system fails to receive something real; Hour asks what happens when a system invents a property a thing never had. Confirmed transferable as a discipline (start from a question about mechanism, not a topic), NOT transferable as a shared question — see the correction in Section 1a below.
- **Give abstraction a concrete handhold quickly.** Fox opens on cameras being carried into a forest and a person walking ten metres — a physical event first. Hour opens on a conceptual proposition ("some clocks come from the world, others are put there by paperwork") and reaches a roof within one sentence. Both are valid; what's confirmed transferable is not "start concrete," but "don't leave the reader suspended in abstraction for more than a sentence or two." An opening may be a physical thing, a person, an event, a strange sentence, or a clear conceptual proposition — as long as a concrete handhold arrives almost immediately.
- **Facts, documents, and actions carry the intellectual weight; the writer explains only once, briefly.** Fox: trigger percentages, blank photographs, the paper's own line about rare species. Hour: bulletin text, withdrawal notice, dates. Both articles state their core insight in one clear sentence and then advance through new material rather than restating it. Confirmed transferable.
- **Disability-derived knowledge changes what mechanism becomes visible, without needing biography.** Pixel's interpreter comparison and Maya's "the calculation made it older" both do real conceptual work and neither uses "I," "as a Deaf/disabled person," or autobiographical credential. Confirmed transferable — and this is the most important shared discipline, since earlier Hour drafts violated it (see Section 7) before correction.
- **The reader is allowed to know the thesis early; the discipline is not hiding it, it's not repeating it.** Fox states its "not recorded ≠ not there" idea plainly by the interpreter paragraph. Hour states "the calculation made it older" once, mid-article. Neither delays the point artificially, and neither returns to restate it in new words later. Confirmed transferable.
- **Real names, dates, and quotes are used when they move the story, not to perform authority.** Kays/Hody/Jachowski/Parsons and Anita Fox/Lori Wing-Heier appear because they signed, wrote, or issued something — not as citation decoration. Confirmed transferable.
- **The ending returns to a concrete thing rather than re-explaining the thesis.** Fox ends on the calibration test and the fox's misses, side by side, no summary sentence after. Hour ends on the roof/labor distinction and the two-sentence withdrawal, no summary sentence after. Confirmed transferable, and it's the clause most repeatedly violated and re-fixed during iteration (see Section 7).
- **Research depth should match what the story needs, not a target word count or citation quota.** Both benchmarks got shorter and better as unnecessary material (Fox: full Table 4 breakdown; Hour: goodwill section, dollar-arithmetic paragraph) was cut once it stopped adding a new fact/person/action. Confirmed transferable.

### 1a. Correction: do not collapse the two mechanisms into one "gap"

It is tempting to describe both benchmarks as versions of "a gap between what happened and what got recorded." That framing is too broad and starts manufacturing a single CripMinds master formula where none exists. The two mechanisms are actually different in kind:

- **Fox:** reality exists, but a mediation/recording system selectively fails to receive or preserve it (omission/delay/threshold).
- **Hour:** the system actively creates or assigns a property — age — that the thing never possessed (invented category/imposed classification).

The transferable principle is not "find the gap." It is: **start from a real disturbance, and let the perceptual engine reveal whatever hidden mechanism makes that disturbance possible.** That mechanism might involve omission, delay, translation, an imposed category, an invented property, sequencing, a threshold, a classification, a scale mismatch, a comparison, an institutional rule, or something not yet encountered in either benchmark. This list is illustrative, not closed, and should never be treated as an exhaustive menu to select from.

## 2. What did not transfer — and must stay variable

- **Number of "worlds."** Fox needed three (forest, interpreter, ocean) to earn its return. Hour needed one administrative sequence (Alaska) and explicitly did NOT need a second-world analogy — goodwill accounting was deliberately cut because it only reproved the thesis rather than opening a new one. A future article might need zero, one, or three; there is no correct number.
- **Where the disability lens appears.** In Fox, the interpreter comparison is a distinct paragraph, clearly marked as a comparison. In Hour, Maya's lens never appears as a separate comparison at all — it lives entirely in which facts got selected and in what order (why Alaska, why the withdrawal, why the unresolved gap is the ending rather than a frustration to resolve). Both are legitimate, forcing every article to have an explicit "compare it to X" paragraph would flatten this.
- **Narrator presence.** Pixel is felt through a chosen analogy. Maya is felt only through selection and one plain sentence. Neither uses "I." A future persona might legitimately need first person if the canon and the material support it — this method does not forbid it, it only forbids using it as unearned credential (see Section 7).
- **What creates suspense.** Fox's suspense is physical (a fox crossing a grid unseen). Hour's suspense is bureaucratic (a rule appearing and being quietly withdrawn). The engine of narrative tension is subject-dependent and must be discovered per story, not imported.
- **Length and paragraph count.** Fox is roughly 900 words across dense paragraphs; Hour is roughly 480 words across leaner ones. Both are correctly sized for their material. There is no target length.

## 3. Perceptual engine before subject

**FOX**
- SUBJECT: a camera-trap ecology study on detection bias.
- DISTURBANCE: a person walking in front of the cameras triggered all of them; a fox crossing the same grid mostly did not.
- PERCEPTUAL QUESTION: what is the distance between something happening and something being recorded as having happened?
- HIDDEN MECHANISM: detection probability tracks body size/speed, so "rarity" in ecological data can be partly an artifact of the instrument, not the animal.
- DISABILITY-DERIVED KNOWLEDGE: understanding that a channel (an interpreter) can transmit something correctly and still arrive late — that delay or absence in the record does not imply absence in the event.
- WHY THE LENS IS CAUSALLY NECESSARY: Without this way of separating "event" from "record of event," the camera data reads as a simple accuracy problem. The interpreter comparison is what makes visible that the failure is structural and channel-specific, not incidental — the same distinction that explains Deaf audiences receiving a joke late without anything being lost.

**HOUR**
- SUBJECT: state insurance regulation of "actual cash value" depreciation.
- DISTURBANCE: insurers depreciate labor at the same rate as physical materials, though labor has no physical age.
- PERCEPTUAL QUESTION: what happens when a system assigns a property (age, in this case) to something that never had it, and then treats that assigned property as though it were natural?
- HIDDEN MECHANISM: a calculation, not the world, is the source of the labor's "age" — an administrative category manufacturing a fact about time.
- DISABILITY-DERIVED KNOWLEDGE: familiarity with systems that impose clocks, waiting periods, or eligibility windows on things/bodies that don't actually operate on those timelines, and recognizing when a category is doing that quietly, inside an otherwise ordinary-looking calculation.
- WHY THE LENS IS CAUSALLY NECESSARY: The insurance dispute could be read as a simple pricing disagreement. The perceptual question — that an institution can assign a clock and then act as if the clock belonged there all along — is what turns a rate dispute into the same kind of category error Fox found, and it is what makes the Alaska reversal, not just the depreciation math, feel like the center of the story.

This confirms personas do not own subject territory: Maya could have written Fox's mechanism (an instrument assigning "trigger readiness" that doesn't match the animal's actual presence) and Pixel could plausibly have written Hour's (a form's category creating a fact). What differs is which disturbance each persona actually noticed and selected — not a subject boundary, and not a shared underlying "gap." Fox's mechanism is an omission inside a recording system; Hour's is an invented property inside a classification system. Both are legitimate, different answers to the same open-ended instruction: find the real disturbance, then let the perceptual engine surface whatever mechanism is actually there.

## 4. Reading style: EASY ≠ SHORT

Derived directly from what repeatedly failed and was fixed in both benchmarks:

- A long sentence is easy when it moves through one continuous, concrete action or fact (Fox: the calibration-test paragraph; Hour: the Alaska withdrawal paragraph). A short sentence is hard when it compresses an abstraction ("the record became smaller than the event itself" — cut from an earlier Hour draft for exactly this reason).
- Chopping a paragraph into fragments does not make it easier; it was tried on Hour (V0.2.2) and Jascha's own reading flagged it as "cluttered," "more explainer than long-read." The fix was not shortening further — it was restoring paragraph movement while keeping each sentence's referents explicit.
- A metaphor that requires the reader to decode it before continuing must be replaced with the plain fact underneath it. "The record became smaller than the thing itself," "the age belonged to the roof, aging beside it" — all cut or flattened once identified as requiring rereading.
- State the insight once, in the plainest available sentence, and trust it: "not recorded is not the same as not there" (Fox) and "the labor did not get older; the calculation made it older" (Hour) each appear exactly once and are never paraphrased again nearby.
- Ambiguous pronoun reference ("the second," "those two facts") must be resolved explicitly, especially near an ending, where the reader has the least patience to look backward.
- Beauty should live in how a paragraph develops (concrete thing → complication → new fact → consequence), not in every sentence trying to be clever. The clever-sentence failure mode ("wrote it into law, then wrote it back out") was identified and cut precisely because cleverness required a second read.

## 5. Material/research: when to keep searching vs. when to write

- **Fox** improved most when previously-known but unused concrete material (the calibration walk, exact trigger time, blank-photo mechanism) was surfaced from an evidence packet that already existed — this was a *retrieval* problem, not a *research* problem.
- **Hour** improved most by *cutting* material (goodwill accounting, constructed dollar arithmetic) that had already been gathered but did not open a new mechanism — this was a *selection* problem, not a research gap.
- Rule: **search more** only when the current evidence packet cannot yet support both (A) the disturbance/mechanism in grounded source material, and (B) enough concrete material to carry the reader through an article. If both are already present, the task is writing/selection, not further search.
- Rule: **stop researching and write** once the packet contains (a) the mechanism stated in the source's own words, and (b) concrete material sufficient to dramatize it. That material does not have to be a named human actor/action sequence — it might be actions, documents, people, measurements, objects, images, interfaces, physical processes, contradictions, dated changes, or another subject-appropriate form. Human voices and actions are valuable when genuinely present and useful (as in Hour's Alaska bulletins), not a mandatory quota every article must satisfy. Additional supporting cases (a second world, a second court, a second regulator) should only be added if each one opens something new — not to reach a quota of examples or citations.
- A packet that is honest about its own gaps (Hour's evidence packet explicitly says the Alaska reversal's cause "could not be confirmed") is more usable than one that fills the gap with a plausible-sounding inference. The unresolved gap became the ending's power, not a weakness to patch.

## 6. Source / quote rule

- Quotes are not compulsory and testimony is not compulsory. Neither benchmark uses a first-person interview subject.
- A quote earns its place when it does something paraphrase cannot: reveals a position in the speaker's own words (Fox's "this problem would be worse with rare species"; Hour's "does not deteriorate, age, or decline in value"), or shows how blunt an official document actually was (Alaska's bulletin language).
- Technical/journal prose was deliberately NOT quoted at length once a plain paraphrase did the same job — Fox's trigger-probability numbers replaced a verbatim results-section sentence; Hour's Michigan bulletin was trimmed to a short verified fragment rather than the full sentence.
- An anonymous "a study/review found" was upgraded to a named source-handhold wherever the evidence packet supported it (Fox: "Mooney and colleagues... Royal Society Open Science"; Hour: "DIFS Director Anita Fox," "Lori Wing-Heier, Alaska's insurance director") — naming the human/institution behind a claim was consistently preferred over anonymous attribution once verification allowed it.
- Grounding is absolute in both cases: every quote used is traced to a fetched primary or clearly labeled secondary source in the evidence packet; nothing was invented or "improved" for rhythm.

## 7. Failure modes actually demonstrated during iteration

1. Restating the central insight in slightly different words across multiple paragraphs (flagged and removed in early Fox and Hour drafts).
2. Disability identity used as credential rather than as a way of noticing ("I am an immigrant with a spinal injury... nobody hands you a manual" — cut entirely from Hour).
3. An abrupt disability-derived comparison introduced with no bridge (early Fox drafts jumped straight into interpretation without transition; fixed with a one-sentence bridge).
4. Dense academic/legal compression requiring rereading (early Hour drafts stacked definition + exception + legal qualification + conclusion in one sentence; early Fox drafts quoted full results-section prose).
5. Over-correction into staccato fragments after a difficulty note, which reads as an explainer rather than a long-read (Hour V0.2.2).
6. A clever sentence that requires decoding before the reader can continue ("wrote it into law, then wrote it back out"; "the record became smaller than the thing itself").
7. A research-summary middle the reader wants to skip (early Hour drafts on the multi-state legal survey; trimmed to two courts, one sentence each).
8. Anonymous "a study/review says" retained where a real named source-handhold was available and should have been used instead.
9. Forcing a second-world analogy purely to match a prior benchmark's shape (goodwill accounting was flagged as decorative proof-of-thesis and cut from Hour, precisely to resist Fox-envy).
10. An ending that explains the thesis again instead of landing on a concrete image (multiple Fox and Hour ending drafts were flagged and simplified for this reason).
11. Excessive compression that strips book-like paragraph rhythm in the name of "easy reading" (Hour V0.2.2's cluttered/chopped feeling, corrected in V0.2.3–V0.2.5).
12. Ambiguous backward-reference pronouns near an ending ("the second," "those two facts") forcing a reread at the exact point the article should be landing.

## 8. What Sofa Method V0.1 does NOT require

A CripMinds article produced under this method does not need:

- first person
- autobiographical disability disclosure
- a second case or a second world
- a delayed thesis
- a thesis placed in sentence one
- human testimony or an interview subject
- exactly one quote, or any quote at all
- a "return to the opening image" structure
- a fixed paragraph count
- a fixed word count
- a Fox-like forest-and-analogy opening
- a Maya-like single-line aphorism
- a persona confined to a subject category (disability topics, accessibility topics, etc.)

The method governs how a real disturbance in the evidence becomes a piece of writing that a reader can follow on first read without losing the thread — not what the finished article is required to look like. Two benchmarks with almost nothing in common on the surface (a 2013 ecology field experiment; a 2024–25 state insurance-regulation dispute) converged not on a shared mechanism, but on a shared discipline: notice a real disturbance, let a disability-derived way of seeing show why its particular mechanism matters causally, and let real facts — not repeated explanation — carry the reader through it.

# Part 2 — What Mobile Added (V0.2 additions)

Mobile went through many more visible revisions than Fox or Hour before it froze. That
iteration history is itself informative: most of the failed drafts were not evidence
failures or sentence-craft failures. They were confusions about *which layer of the
pipeline* was actually broken. The additions below are what surviving that confusion
actually taught.

## 9. Lens ≠ Writer

The clearest new rule Mobile forced into the open, because early drafts violated it by
implication even while never doing it outright: **the persona/perceptual lens and the
prose writer are two different functions, and the pipeline should treat them as such.**

The lens's job is discovery. It asks: *what does this way of perceiving make visible that
the ordinary framing misses?* For Mobile, Zen's lens is what turned "a company got a
generous classification" into "the authority to name the thing and the information that
would keep the name true belonged to different parties, and the category itself did
material work before the machines existed." That is a real, non-obvious act of noticing,
and it is causally necessary the same way Fox's interpreter comparison and Hour's clock
metaphor are — without it, Mobile is just a regulatory-loophole story.

Once that discovery exists, the prose writer is a separate editorial function, and must
not:

- roleplay the persona
- imitate disability traits in sentence rhythm or diction
- add biography to prove the persona's authenticity
- make the prose "sound autistic / Deaf / disabled"

The public byline may still carry the persona's name. **Byline ≠ prose persona.** This is
a sharper statement of something Section 1 and Section 7(2) of Part 1 already gestured at
(disability-derived knowledge without "I," without credential) — Mobile is the benchmark
that made it necessary to say explicitly, because the failure mode it guards against is
not "used first person" but the subtler "wrote as though performing the persona's inner
experience," which Fox and Hour never attempted and therefore never had to rule out by
name.

Working architecture, stated plainly for the first time:

```
WORLD / SOURCE → DISTURBANCE → PERCEPTUAL LENS → DISCOVERY
→ RESEARCH / EVIDENCE → ARTICLE FORM → WRITER / PROSE → BYLINE
```

## 10. Why This Story? — the reader contract is separate from the mechanism

A technically correct hidden mechanism is not sufficient to justify an article. Mobile's
drafts kept producing accurate, well-sourced prose that a reader with no prior interest in
xAI, Mississippi air law, or turbine permitting had no reason to start reading. The
missing piece was not more evidence — it was an explicit answer to: *why should an
intelligent general reader care about this obscure story at all*, stated or implied early
enough that the reader keeps going.

Fox's answer: there is a distance between what happens and what gets recorded, and it is
larger than you think. Hour's answer: some clocks come from the world and some are put
there by paperwork, and institutions can quietly treat the second kind as the first. Both
of these answers were, in effect, already present in Fox's and Hour's respective openings,
which is likely why neither benchmark needed this section named separately during their
own development — the reader contract and the concrete opening happened to be the same
move for those two subjects.

Mobile's case makes the two separable: an ordinary administrative word can do more than
describe reality — it can help make a physical action possible before that reality exists.
That is the reader contract, and it had to be identified and written *before* the
subject-level facts (xAI, MDEQ, turbines, permits) could be safely introduced without
losing a general reader.

## 11. Large promise → concrete handhold, tested under a harder subject

Mobile repeatedly failed when it opened from inside the subject — starting with MDEQ's
authority, or a definition of mobile-versus-temporary, or a raw turbine count. Those
openings were accurate but inert: correct answers to questions the reader hadn't yet been
given a reason to ask.

The version that worked opened with the promise itself ("This is a story about a word that
helped build a power plant. The word was 'temporary.'"), then stayed with the ordinary,
non-technical meaning of that word for one short paragraph before any proper noun appeared,
and only then moved into the regulatory evidence.

Transferable rule: **an obscure or technical subject often needs a doorway that states, or
clearly implies, what larger thing the reader is about to discover, before the reader is
asked to carry the specific names and dates.** Then the concrete handhold should still
arrive quickly, per Section 1 of Part 1 — this doesn't relax that rule, it adds a step in
front of it for subjects where the concrete handhold alone doesn't yet explain why it
matters.

This is **not** a mandatory philosophical or aphoristic opening. Fox opened concretely with
no stated promise; Hour opened with a conceptual proposition that doubled as its promise;
Mobile needed the promise stated first and separately. All three are legitimate solves to
the same underlying requirement, arrived at differently because the subjects differ in how
much unearned interest they carry on their own. A camera trap and a fox are intrinsically
interesting; xAI's permitting paperwork is not, until the reader is told what it's really
about.

## 12. Category as actor — a third, distinct mechanism

Mobile is not a variant of Fox or Hour's mechanism, and should not be filed under either:

- **Fox:** reality exists, but a mediation/recording system selectively fails to preserve
  it (omission/delay/threshold).
- **Hour:** a system assigns a property to a thing that never possessed it (invented
  category/imposed classification).
- **Mobile:** a category, once proposed and accepted, becomes materially productive — it
  doesn't just describe the turbines afterward, it is part of what let the turbines arrive
  in the first place. "Temporary" was proposed by xAI *before* deployment, specifically to
  begin operating before the ordinary permitting process was finished, and the state's
  written acceptance of that word is a necessary link in the causal chain that put the
  machines on the ground.

This confirms and sharpens the standing instruction from Part 1, Section 1a: the list of
possible mechanisms (omission, imposed category, invented property, and now a
*materially productive* category) is illustrative and open, not a closed menu. A fourth
benchmark should be expected to surface something not on this list at all.

## 13. Research reset: when the fix is not a prose fix

Mobile only improved decisively once the evidence hierarchy itself was reconsidered, not
once the existing prose was polished again. The original emphasis on the raw accumulation
(3 → 69 turbines) was a real, verified fact but not the strongest available material. The
decisive material was a four-date documentary sequence that had been in the evidence packet
the whole time:

- **July 15, 2025** — an xAI infrastructure manager describes Southaven as "copying and
  pasting" the approach already used at the company's first site.
- **July 25, 2025** — xAI vice president Brent Mayo tells MDEQ the company plans to bring
  "temporary turbines on site to be used prior to receiving the air permit," and states
  the company believes this is "allowed."
- **July 29, 2025** — MDEQ's Jaricus Whitlock issues the written mobile/temporary
  determination.
- **August 1, 2025** — xAI begins installing and operating turbines under that
  determination.

Transferable rule, extending Section 5 of Part 1: **when repeated prose revisions cannot
produce an answer to "why does this story exist," stop revising prose and go back to the
evidence.** The actual fault may not be craft at all — it may be a wrong evidence
hierarchy (the loudest number in the packet is not the strongest story material), a wrong
narrative spine (chronological accumulation instead of a documentary sequence), or a wrong
perceptual discovery (the lens hadn't yet found the mechanism that was actually there).
Mobile's breakthrough came from re-reading the same evidence packet with a different
question in mind, not from finding new sources.

## 14. Opening ≠ hook

A good opening is not simply a sentence interesting enough to keep someone reading one
sentence further. It establishes a reason to follow the *whole* story before the reader is
asked to hold many names, dates, and institutional details in mind at once.

This sits beside, and slightly ahead of, Section 1's "concrete handhold" rule: the handhold
answers "is there something to picture," the reason-to-follow answers "is there something
at stake." Fox and Hour mostly answered both at once inside their opening image. Mobile
needed the reason-to-follow stated a beat before the handhold could do its work.

No specific opening form (aphorism, question, physical scene, conceptual proposition,
promise-then-word) is mandatory. Three benchmarks have now used four different shapes
between them (Fox: physical scene; Hour: conceptual proposition; Mobile went through a
promise-plus-word opening only after four rejected alternatives — a generic-regulator
opening, a raw-count opening, a "some categories..." opening, and a mid-document opening
all failed the same test).

## 15. Return without explanation

Mobile's frozen version establishes a natural return: the article opens on "temporary"
enabling turbines to *arrive* before a permit exists, and closes on the state issuing a
retirement schedule governing when those same turbines must *leave*. The same word, and
the same two document-types (a letter that admits, a schedule that expels), mean something
different at the end than they did at the beginning, without a sentence that says so.

This is not a new universal requirement. Fox and Hour both end on a concrete image rather
than a circular return to their own opening image (Fox: the calibration test beside the
fox's misses; Hour: the roof/labor distinction beside the two-sentence withdrawal). Mobile's
ending happens to be circular because its evidence supported it — the same institutional
actors wrote the first and last documents in the sequence.

Transferable principle: **if the opening establishes a concrete promise, object, or word,
check whether the evidence naturally lets it return changed at the end. If it does, use it.
If it doesn't, do not force a return — land on the strongest concrete fact instead**, per
Part 1, Section 1's ending rule, which still governs.

## 16. Preserve variation — do not template three successes

Fox, Hour, and Mobile now demonstrate three different mechanisms, three different kinds of
entrance, three different placements of the disability-derived lens, and three different
narrative centers (a field experiment, an administrative reversal, a documentary sequence
before deployment). The correct response to three successes is not to derive a visible
article template from them. It is to treat each new agreement as a discipline confirmed
*despite* difference — the same posture Part 1 already took toward two benchmarks, now
tested a third time and holding.

## 17. Three-Benchmark Validation

**FOX**
- Mechanism: a recording system (a camera trap) selectively fails to preserve real events, based on the target's size and speed — record ≠ event.
- Reader contract: there is a distance between what happens and what reaches the record, and it's larger, and more structural, than you'd assume.

**HOUR**
- Mechanism: a system assigns a property (age) to something — labor — that never physically possessed it, then acts as if the assigned property were natural.
- Reader contract: some clocks come from the world; others are put there by paperwork, and it's easy to miss which kind you're looking at.

**MOBILE**
- Mechanism: a category, proposed before deployment and accepted by the authority that could grant it, becomes materially productive — it doesn't just describe a thing afterward, it helps make the thing possible.
- Reader contract: an ordinary administrative word can do more than describe reality; it can help build something in it.

**Readiness assessment:** the method is ready to inform Scout/research/writer
*architecture* — specifically, the Lens ≠ Writer separation (Section 9), the requirement
that a reader contract be identifiable before subject-level detail is introduced (Section
10), and the instruction to reset to evidence rather than keep revising prose when a draft
can't answer "why does this story exist" (Section 13). These three are now confirmed across
three independent subjects and are safe to carry into pipeline design. The method is **not**
ready to inform a fixed article *template*, opening *form*, or mechanism *taxonomy* — every
attempt to name a shared shape (a "gap," a required worlds-count, a required opening type)
has been falsified by the next benchmark, and a fourth benchmark should be expected to do
the same to anything stated too specifically here. No production, prompt, or deployment
changes have been made based on this document.
