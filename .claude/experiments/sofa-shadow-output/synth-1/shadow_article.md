The Rule That Counts a Hallway as an Apartment

On January 14, a state auditor's office sent Fairview Housing Authority a two-sentence question. For three straight years, the authority had reported zero vacancies among its wheelchair-accessible units, even though the average wait for one of those units ran eighteen months. How was that possible.

The authority answered on January 29. Units under what it calls administrative hold, the letter said, are counted as occupied for reporting purposes, consistent with standard practice. Nothing in the sentence is false on its face. Nothing in it appears in any written policy the authority has published, either. It exists, as far as the record shows, only in that one letter, produced only because someone finally asked the question directly.

The hold status the letter is describing has a name in the authority's own ledger: Priority B, Temporary Hold. The ledger's own definition is specific. A unit can carry the status for the duration of an inspection or a repair, not to exceed forty-five days. Sixty-one of the authority's two hundred and fourteen accessible units have carried it continuously since at least January 2022. Of those sixty-one, nine show any inspection or repair logged in the past two years.

A forty-five-day hold, renewed without inspection or repair for two years running, is not a maintenance status anymore. It is a different thing wearing the maintenance status's name, and the difference is exactly what let the authority write zero vacancies on a form and mean it, technically, every time. Its hold cannot legally outlast forty-five days, and yet fifty-two of the sixty-one units have already run past that mark by close to two years.

Marcus Oyelaran has been on the waitlist since 2021. He told the authority's oversight board in February that he had called about one specific Priority B unit, on Delancey Street, four separate times since 2022. Each time, he was told the same thing: temporarily unavailable, not for the waitlist. Four calls, over two years, about a unit whose own file says its hold cannot legally outlast forty-five days. Nobody on the other end of those calls was lying to him. They were reading a status that had simply stopped describing anything true a long time before he first dialed.

The authority's annual compliance report, filed with the state every December, does not resolve this. It does not need to, because it never mentions Priority B at all. All two hundred and fourteen units appear in that report as available accessible inventory, full stop. The sixty-one held units are not flagged, footnoted, or subtracted. They are present in the inventory number and absent from the waitlist, at the same time, in two different documents that never have to be read side by side.

That is the actual mechanism sitting underneath the January letter. A number can be accurate on its own page and still describe nothing like the apartments. The compliance report is honest about its own arithmetic: two hundred and fourteen units exist, and the report says so. The letter is honest about its own rule: a hold counts as occupied. Neither document is lying. The distance between what a resident experiences calling about Delancey Street and what a state auditor reads in a December filing is not a distance either document was built to show, and for three years, nobody outside the authority had a reason to look for it.

No document reviewed says who first decided that a hold should be counted as an occupancy, or when. That absence is its own kind of fact. A rule specific enough to survive one direct question from a state auditor's office did not appear by accident, and it did not arrive with a name attached to it either. It simply existed, already standard practice, the first time anyone official asked.
