## 1. Core regulatory and case-law facts (re-verified, deepened, corrected)

### 1a. Michigan — the bulletin is not what the disturbance card cites, and there are TWO bulletins

**Bulletin 2024-18-INS** — **PRIMARY VERIFIED**. Full text fetched directly from Michigan DIFS' own PDF (mirrored at the Property Insurance Coverage Law Blog): https://www.propertyinsurancecoveragelaw.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/Michigan-Bulletin_2024-18-INS.pdf

- Issued/entered **July 3, 2024**, signed by DIFS Director Anita G. Fox.
- Title: **"Depreciation of Nontangible Items."**
- Exact operative language: *"ACV is ordinarily understood to mean the loss of value due to wear and tear, deterioration, and obsolescence to physical or tangible items. Nontangible items such as labor are not physical objects and do not deteriorate, age, or decline in value regardless of any potential decline in value to the physical property in need of repair."*
- Statutory basis: **MCL 500.2236(5)** (forms may not contain "inconsistent, ambiguous, or misleading clauses" or conditions that "unreasonably or deceptively affect the risk purported to be assumed").
- **This is not an outright ban.** For policies issued/renewed on/after Jan. 1, 2025, insurers may still depreciate labor/nontangible items, but only via a **standalone, opt-in endorsement sold at a reduced premium** — it cannot be buried silently inside the base ACV/depreciation definition.
- Contact for verification: DIFS-OIRF@michigan.gov.

**CORRECTION FOR THE WRITER:** The disturbance card's exact quoted fragment — "nontangible" components "defined to include 'labor, taxes, fees, and overhead and profit'" — is **not in Bulletin 2024-18-INS**. That bulletin only names "labor" as its example of a nontangible item. The fuller four-item enumeration comes from a **second, later bulletin, 2024-26-INS** ("DIFS Bulletin Readdresses Practice of Depreciating Nontangible Items," per NAMIC's Nov. 15, 2024 summary — **SECONDARY SOURCE ONLY**; Michigan's own CDN returned "Access Denied" to automated fetches of the primary PDF, so the full primary text of 26-INS could not be pulled). Per secondary sources, 2024-26-INS:
- Explicitly enumerates nontangible items as **"labor, taxes, fees, and overhead and profit."**
- Narrows scope to **personal lines homeowners and dwelling insurers** specifically (18-INS reads more broadly).
- Adds a requirement that insurers obtain **prior DIFS approval** for the depreciation endorsement, beyond what 18-INS required.

**Recommendation: cite Bulletin 2024-26-INS as the operative rule when quoting the "labor, taxes, fees, and overhead and profit" language**, and note 18-INS as the original bulletin it superseded/amended. Flag to any editor that the primary text of 26-INS itself could not be independently verified — the enumeration is corroborated by trade-press summary only.

### 1b. The four "split court" cases — none of them is what it first appears to be

All four cases in the disturbance card are federal diversity-jurisdiction rulings (a federal judge predicting how a state's own courts would rule), not state supreme court holdings — except one Missouri appellate case found in follow-up research that is a stronger citation. This distinction matters for how confidently the article can say "Colorado law is X" versus "a federal judge guessed Colorado law is X."

**Colorado — upholds depreciating labor**
- **Basham v. United Services Automobile Association (USAA)**, No. 1:16-cv-03057 (D. Colo., filed Dec. 13, 2016; decided July 28, 2017), 2017 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 118729.
- **PRIMARY SOURCE IDENTIFIED** (docket confirmed via Justia: https://dockets.justia.com/docket/colorado/codce/1:2016cv03057/167498); holding/reasoning **SECONDARY-SOURCE CONFIRMED** across independent summaries, not read verbatim from the opinion itself.
- Court: U.S. District Court, District of Colorado — **federal**, not a Colorado state court.
- Holding: "actual cash value" is unambiguous under the policy; the depreciation clause was not limited to "physical deterioration and obsolescence," so USAA could depreciate labor along with materials.

**Kansas — upholds depreciating labor ("windfall" language)**
- **Graves v. American Family Mutual Insurance Co.**, No. 2:14-cv-02417 (D. Kan., decided July 22, 2015), 2015 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 95127; **affirmed**, 10th Cir., April 21, 2017, 2017 U.S. App. LEXIS 6980 (also cited in some secondary sources as 686 F. App'x 536 (10th Cir. 2017) — **this specific reporter citation could not be independently verified on a primary database**, flag as unconfirmed).
- **PRIMARY SOURCE IDENTIFIED**; holding **SECONDARY-SOURCE CONFIRMED**.
- Court: U.S. District Court, District of Kansas, affirmed by the 10th Circuit — **federal**, not a Kansas state court.
- Holding/quote (consistent across two independent legal-blog summaries): depreciating only materials and not labor "would give the insured] a windfall based on labor costs she never incurred," which conflicts with the indemnity principle underlying insurance.

**Missouri — bars depreciating labor**
- **Riggins v. American Family Mutual Insurance Co.**, No. 2:14-cv-04171 (W.D. Mo., Central Division), decided 2015; cited as 106 F. Supp. 3d 1039 (W.D. Mo. 2015), also 2015 WL 3421653 / 2015 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 70839.
- **PRIMARY SOURCE IDENTIFIED** — docket confirmed real via CourtListener: https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/4300798/riggins-v-american-family-mutual-insurance-company/. **Could not pull the actual opinion text** (paywalled/not rendered on fetch) — citation and outcome corroborated by two independent legal-blog sources plus the confirmed docket, but the opinion's exact words were not read directly.
- Court: U.S. District Court, W.D. Missouri — **federal**, not a Missouri state court.
- Holding (secondary-source, consistent): where policy language limits depreciation to "physical deterioration and obsolescence," labor — not being physical — cannot be depreciated.
- **Stronger, more recent citation found in follow-up research:** **Franklin v. Lexington Insurance Co.**, 2022 Mo. App. LEXIS 412 (Mo. Ct. App., June 28, 2022) — an actual **Missouri state appellate court** reaching the same anti-depreciation result. Recommend citing Franklin alongside or instead of Riggins for "Missouri law," since it's a state court, not a federal guess at state law.

**Washington — bars depreciating labor by regulation, not just case law**
- **WAC 284-20-010(4)**, effective Jan. 1, 2022. **PRIMARY VERIFIED** — fetched directly from https://app.leg.wa.gov/wac/default.aspx?cite=284-20-010.
- Exact text: *"Except for the intrinsic labor costs that are included in the cost of manufactured materials or goods, the expense of labor necessary to repair, rebuild, or replace covered property is not a component of physical depreciation and may not be subject to depreciation or betterment."*
- This is a **Standard Fire Policy regulation** — a blanket regulatory ban, not an endorsement-based opt-out like Michigan's, and not case law at all.
- Note: Washington has a separate, distinct motor-vehicle-claims betterment/depreciation provision (in the WAC 284-30 range) — do not conflate the two; the fire-policy labor ban above is the one matching the disturbance's property-insurance framing.

**Additional case found in follow-up research (not in the original disturbance card, worth flagging to the writer):**
- **Henn v. American Family Mutual Insurance Co.**, 295 Neb. 859 (2017) — **Nebraska Supreme Court** (a genuine state high-court holding, unlike the four above). Permits labor depreciation even absent explicit policy language authorizing it. This is arguably the single most citable "permits" precedent in the set, since it's the only state supreme court ruling among all cases found.

## 2. The Alaska reversal — what's confirmed, and an explicit statement of what is NOT

### Original bulletin — PRIMARY VERIFIED
**Bulletin B 24-07**, Alaska Division of Insurance (Dept. of Commerce, Community & Economic Development), addressed to "Admitted Insurers Writing Property and Casualty Business in the State of Alaska," RE: "Depreciation of Labor," dated **May 7, 2024**, signed by **Lori Wing-Heier, Director of Insurance**.
Fetched directly: https://www.commerce.alaska.gov/web/Portals/11/Pub/B24-07.pdf

- Effective date specified in the bulletin: as of **January 1, 2025**, new/renewing property policies could not define ACV to include labor depreciation.
- Exact quote, confirmed verbatim against the primary PDF: *"In property loss, labor does not lose value and remains unaffected by any material property decline. Labor does not physically deteriorate and cannot be subjected to depreciation."*
- Statutory basis cited in the bulletin: **3 AAC 26.090** (Alaska Administrative Code).

### Withdrawal notice — PRIMARY VERIFIED
**Bulletin B 25-03**, dated **January 23, 2025**, RE: "Withdrawal of B 24-07 – Depreciation of Labor," effective **January 24, 2025**, signed by the same director, Wing-Heier.
Fetched directly: https://www.commerce.alaska.gov/web/Portals/11/Pub/B25-03.pdf

- The operative text is **two sentences long**.
- **It states zero reason.** No rationale, no reference to litigation, no reference to industry pushback, no reference to pending legislation — nothing beyond the fact of withdrawal itself.

### Cause of the reversal — COULD NOT CONFIRM
A dedicated search for the actual cause turned up **no** Alaska Division of Insurance press release, **no** news coverage (checked Alaska Beacon, trade press, general search) specifically about the reversal and its cause, **no** litigation record challenging B 24-07, and **no** Alaska-specific APCIA or NAMIC comment letter addressing this particular bulletin. This should be reported to readers as genuinely unknown from the public record, not glossed over.

**One documented correlation was found — explicitly NOT a confirmed cause:**
- Alaska **Senate Bill 132** (an omnibus insurance bill) was introduced on **March 14, 2025** — seven weeks *after* the bulletin was withdrawn — and contains labor-depreciation language substantively similar to the bulletin's (barring depreciation of labor "as a component of physical depreciation," with a narrow manufactured-goods carve-out).
- Sources conflict on when it was signed into law: one source says **June 24, 2025**, another says passed/signed around **July 30, 2025** — this discrepancy is itself unresolved and should not be repeated as a single fact without flagging the conflict. Effective date cited: **January 1, 2026**.
- **No source states the withdrawal happened because the legislature was taking up the issue** — and since the bill wasn't even introduced yet when B 25-03 pulled the bulletin, "the state deferred to the legislature" is a plausible-sounding but **unverified inference**, not a documented fact. Do not present it as the reason in the article; present it, if at all, as an unexplained-but-notable sequence of events.

**Bottom line for the writer:** the honest version of this thread is "Alaska adopted the rule, then rescinded it two sentences later with no stated reason, and nearly identical language resurfaced in a state bill seven weeks after that — but no public record connects those two facts, and the actual cause of the reversal is unknown." That unresolved gap is itself part of the disturbance, not a research failure to paper over.

## 4. Second real, different-domain analogous case

**Result: NONE FOUND that meets the verification bar.** Multiple candidate domains were searched specifically for a genuine mechanism match (a physical-decay formula applied to something with no physical substance to decay, functioning as a one-directional payout reduction). None produced a real, specific, sourced case matching that mechanism. Reporting the negative result honestly, with what was checked and why each candidate was rejected, per the task's own instruction not to force a match:

1. **DME/Medicare wheelchair-repair labor** (the disability-relevant candidate, checked most carefully). Verified via a real KFF Health News/SlashGear right-to-repair article: Medicare/insurers pay for parts and labor actually billed for equipment repair, but **exclude** reimbursement for technician time spent examining, picking up, or returning equipment. **Rejected** — this is a coverage exclusion, not an age-based depreciation formula applied to labor. No source found describing DME labor cost being reduced by a percentage tied to equipment age.

2. **Medicare's DME "reasonable useful lifetime" (RUL) rule** (42 CFR 414; CMS transmittals): a real 5-year depreciation-like schedule governing repair-vs-replacement decisions — but it depreciates the **tangible equipment itself**, which genuinely does wear out physically. **Rejected** — this is actually an on-mechanism, legitimate use of depreciation (the physical item really ages), so it's not an example of the misapplied-formula problem; it's closer to a real counterexample of correct usage.

3. **Extended warranty / home warranty labor reimbursement disputes**: real and litigated within the property-insurance domain itself (recurring Allstate labor-depreciation suits), but that's the *same* domain, not a different one. Non-insurance warranty disputes found (HVAC/auto dealer warranty reimbursement laws) concern disputes over labor **rate** (what an hour of a mechanic's time is worth for reimbursement purposes), not age-based **depreciation** of labor. **Rejected** — no depreciation-of-labor mechanism found here, only rate disputes.

4. **Intangible asset depreciation in accounting (e.g., goodwill)**: found the **opposite** of a match. US/international accounting standards deliberately do **not** depreciate goodwill or indefinite-life intangibles, precisely because they have no physical substance to wear out — they instead undergo annual impairment testing. **Rejected as a counter-example**, not an analog: this is a real domain that explicitly built its rules to avoid the exact category error at the center of the disturbance, which is worth noting to the writer as an interesting contrast even though it's not "a second case of the same problem."

5. **Eminent domain "betterment" / business goodwill compensation in condemnation law**: searches did not surface any documented case or statutory provision applying a physical-depreciation-style formula to intangible compensation (business goodwill, relocation costs) in this area. **Genuine gap, not a rejection** — could not confirm or rule this out; a differently-worded, deeper search (state eminent-domain treatises specifically) might still turn something up, but as researched here, nothing verifiable was found.

**Recommendation for the writer:** do not include a fabricated or half-verified "second case." If a structural analog is wanted for the piece, the honest options are either (a) use the accounting-goodwill finding as a real contrast case ("here is a domain that got this right by design"), or (b) commission a deeper, separate research pass specifically on eminent-domain betterment/goodwill law, which is the one candidate that was neither confirmed nor ruled out.

## 5. Dollar-scale figures (roof replacement, the representative claim type)

**Total roof replacement cost — PRIMARY VERIFIED (direct fetch):**
NerdWallet (updated Sept. 16, 2025, citing Angi data): average **$9,500**, typical range **$5,800–$46,000**; per-square-foot breakdown roughly **$4,000–$11,000 per 1,000 sq. ft.**
https://www.nerdwallet.com/home-ownership/home-improvement/learn/roof-replacement-cost

Secondary corroboration only (not individually fetched/verified — consistent range but lower confidence): Fixr/Modernize 2026 figures cite $7,500–$30,000, average $9,000–$18,000.

**Labor share of total roofing cost — WebSearch-aggregated, lower confidence, not individually fetched from primary sources:** multiple roofing-industry sources (SquareDash, RoofingCalculator, SubcontractorHub) put labor at roughly **40–60%** of total job cost, several converging closer to 50–60%. No single authoritative figure exists — report as a range, not a point estimate.

**Three real, fetch-verified worked examples of the depreciation math itself, all using the standard straight-line formula (years of age ÷ expected useful life = depreciation %):**

- **IRMI** (fetched directly): $25,000 roof, split $12,500 materials / $12,500 labor; 20-year expected life, 10 years old → 50% depreciation. ACV depreciating materials only = **$18,750**. ACV depreciating materials *and* labor = **$12,500**. Difference attributable purely to whether labor is depreciated: **$6,250**.
- **MWL Law**, discussing *Mitchell v. State Farm* (Arizona) (fetched directly): $10,000 roof, $5,000/$5,000 split, same 50% rate. Materials-only ACV = **$7,500**. Materials+labor ACV = **$5,000**. Difference: **$2,500**.
- **1000SquaresRoofing** (fetched directly): a plain illustration of the formula itself — $18,000 replacement cost value, 10-year-old roof on a 20-year expected life → 50% depreciation → $9,000 deducted → $9,000 ACV (materials-only example, no labor split shown).

United Policyholders' "Depreciation Basics" page (uphelp.org), a real consumer-advocacy nonprofit resource, returned an HTTP 403 on fetch attempts and could not be used as a source for figures — noted here so it isn't silently dropped, but nothing from it is cited above.

**Constructed reader-scale translation (built transparently from the real figures above — this specific combined number is NOT itself a single verified real-world case outcome, it is arithmetic applied to real inputs, and should be labeled as illustrative in the article):**

Using NerdWallet/Angi's real average total cost ($9,500) and the industry-cited labor-share range (40–60%), with the same straight-line 50%-at-10-years-of-20 formula used in the sourced worked examples above:

- Labor cost on an average roof job ≈ **$3,800–$5,700**
- Depreciating that labor at 50% withholds roughly **$1,900–$2,850** from the payout — money a homeowner still has to pay a roofer at today's full labor rate to get the job done, on top of whatever is separately withheld for materials depreciation.

This gives a writer a concrete "a couple thousand dollars, on an average job" scale, while being explicit that the combined number is a constructed illustration from real inputs, not a single sourced real-world claim outcome.
