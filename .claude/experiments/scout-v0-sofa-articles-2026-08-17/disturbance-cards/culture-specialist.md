## Card 1 — The frog that was never photographed, only cross-referenced

**SOURCE**: University of Kansas News, reporting on a *Zootaxa* taxonomic
correction by the KU Biodiversity Institute and Natural History Museum

**URL / SOURCE ID**: https://news.ku.edu/news/article/a-clerical-error-caused-misidentification-of-frog-specimen-that-once-stood-for-an-entire-species

**DATE**: December 15, 2025

**EXACT DISTURBANCE FRAGMENT**: "When the researcher saw the photo, instead
of asking for the specimen, they asked for just the catalog number, and they
were given the wrong catalog number that belonged to another specimen." The
original 1999 species description of *Dendrobates duellmani* was built on
specimen KU 221832 — a plain brown frog — instead of the actual colorful
Peruvian frog in the photograph, KU 221835. Herpetologists who later
requested the actual holotype in person "immediately recognized the
mismatch: the frog is very colorful, and the numbered one was brown."

**LOCAL CONTEXT**: A holotype is the single physical specimen a species name
is permanently anchored to — the reference point every future paper on that
species must cite. Here the anchor specimen and the specimen the species was
actually described from were two different frogs for more than two decades,
undetected because nobody after the original author checked the physical
object against the photograph it was supposedly of.

**WHY THIS IS STRANGE**: A formal system built specifically to prevent
ambiguity (assign one irreplaceable physical object per species name) was
undermined by an informal shortcut — trusting a catalog number over the
object itself — and the resulting error was "cited and reproduced in
follow-up research" for over 20 years without anyone catching it by
re-examining the type specimen.

**WHAT THE SOURCE ITSELF SAYS IS HAPPENING**: A straightforward "clerical
error" from 1999, since corrected: the species is now understood to be a
junior synonym of *Ranitomeya ventrimaculata*, and taxonomic bookkeeping has
been fixed in *Zootaxa*.

**WHAT THAT EXPLANATION MAY FAIL TO EXPLAIN**: "Clerical error" describes
the initial mistake but not why the mismatch was invisible to every
subsequent researcher who cited the holotype for 20+ years — implying that
downstream taxonomic work routinely trusts catalog metadata over physical
(or even photographic) verification, and that this is treated as normal
practice rather than a known risk.

**POSSIBLE HIDDEN MECHANISM (HYPOTHESIS)**: Natural history collections may
have an unspoken, unaudited layer of trust in catalog-number lookups as a
substitute for physical specimen examination — a workaround (skip the
loan/photo-request step, just cite the number) that has become normal
enough that errors like this can propagate silently through a field's
literature for decades before anyone happens to request the physical object.

**WHAT WOULD HAVE TO BE VERIFIED**:
- How the museum's loan/lookup request process worked in 1999 vs. today (was a catalog-number-only request standard practice, or already discouraged?)
- Whether this is an isolated incident or whether similar catalog-number/specimen mismatches have been found in audits of other holotypes at KU or peer institutions
- The full citation trail of papers that relied on the wrong specimen, to see whether any conclusions beyond taxonomy (e.g. distribution, conservation status) were affected

**WHAT WOULD MAKE THIS A BAD ARTICLE**: Treating it as a quirky "oops, wrong
frog" anecdote about one careless researcher in 1999, rather than asking
whether catalog-number trust is a structural practice across natural history
taxonomy that produces this kind of error more often than anyone has
checked.

## Card 2 — A complete skull, correctly cataloged as "unknown," for 50 years

**SOURCE**: Popular Science

**URL / SOURCE ID**: https://www.popsci.com/environment/saber-toot-cat-fossil-hidden-drawer/

**DATE**: June 27, 2026 (discovery made in 2022; fossil originally collected decades earlier)

**EXACT DISTURBANCE FRAGMENT**: "I saw this cranium that was labelled
Pseudaelurus. I thought it could be assigned to a more particular species,"
says Narimane Chatar, who found the fossil while touring the American
Museum of Natural History's collections as a graduate student. The specimen
"had been there for 50, 60 years, lost in the drawers, labelled something
else." It has now been reassigned to *Adelphailurus kansensis*.

**LOCAL CONTEXT**: *Pseudaelurus* is described in the article as a
catch-all genus name "used for cat-like creatures with unassessable
lineage" — effectively a formal label for "we can't tell exactly what this
is yet." The label wasn't a mistake in the sense of being false; it was a
placeholder that was never revisited.

**WHY THIS IS STRANGE**: The fossil was not lost, misplaced, or actively
mislabeled — it was correctly stored, correctly numbered, and sitting in a
major institution's public collection the entire time. The thing that
failed wasn't record-keeping; it was that a category explicitly designed to
mean "provisional, needs further work" functioned in practice as a
terminal, un-revisited resting place for five to six decades.

**WHAT THE SOURCE ITSELF SAYS IS HAPPENING**: A student happened to notice
the specimen while browsing drawers, recognized the label as generic rather
than specific, and used 3D scanning to confirm a more precise species
identification — framed as a happy story of modern technology solving an
old classification gap.

**WHAT THAT EXPLANATION MAY FAIL TO EXPLAIN**: It doesn't explain why a
complete skull — not a fragment, not something ambiguous — sat unexamined
for half a century in a major museum's collection despite presumably being
accessible to generations of paleontologists who could have looked closer.
The "catch-all genus" label may have actively discouraged further inquiry
by signaling "already classified" rather than "flagged as uncertain."

**POSSIBLE HIDDEN MECHANISM (HYPOTHESIS)**: Placeholder/catch-all
taxonomic categories may function less like open questions and more like
closing tags in day-to-day museum practice — once an object has *any*
genus-level label, however provisional, it stops being visible as a research
priority to anyone browsing the collection, because the informal cue
researchers use to find fossils worth re-examining is "unlabeled" or
"disputed," not "in a genus known to be a catch-all."

**WHAT WOULD HAVE TO BE VERIFIED**:
- How many other specimens at AMNH (or peer institutions) still carry the *Pseudaelurus* catch-all label and haven't been re-examined
- Whether the museum's internal collection database flags catch-all/placeholder genus labels differently from confirmed identifications, or treats them identically in search/browse tools
- The original acquisition and cataloging paperwork, to see whether the placeholder label was ever accompanied by an internal note flagging it for follow-up that was then lost

**WHAT WOULD MAKE THIS A BAD ARTICLE**: Framing it purely as "student makes
amazing discovery," which flattens the more interesting and uncomfortable
point — that a formally correct, non-erroneous label can function exactly
like an error by removing an object from active scientific attention for
generations.

## Card 3 — A pinned note overturned ninety years of default attribution

**SOURCE**: *The Magazine Antiques* — "Narratives in the Needlework"

**URL / SOURCE ID**: https://www.themagazineantiques.com/article/narratives-in-the-needlework/

**DATE**: August 14, 2023

**EXACT DISTURBANCE FRAGMENT**: "An old note pinned to the back of a
nineteenth-century Kentucky quilt chronicles its history," and although the
elaborate Whig Rose and swag-border quilt (c. 1850) "might otherwise have
been assumed to be the project of a wealthy white planter's wife," the note
and subsequent research established it was made by Ellen Morton Littlejohn
and Margaret Morton — women enslaved at the Morton family plantation, "The
Knob," in Russellville, Kentucky. Ellen was the household's seamstress;
Margaret was its cook.

**LOCAL CONTEXT**: The quilt is now in the collection of the American Folk
Art Museum. Fine needlework quilts of this style and quality were, by
default institutional and connoisseurship convention, attributed to the
lady of the household as an accomplishment of leisure — the note is what
broke that default in this one case.

**WHY THIS IS STRANGE**: The corrective evidence wasn't archival research,
provenance paperwork, or connoisseurship — it was a handwritten note
literally pinned to the physical object, informal and paratextual, sitting
outside any formal cataloging system, yet it's the only reason this
particular quilt's authorship differs from what "would otherwise have been
assumed." That phrasing implies the default assumption (wealthy woman =
maker) is applied to comparable quilts without equivalent physical evidence
attached.

**WHAT THE SOURCE ITSELF SAYS IS HAPPENING**: A single, fortunate piece of
physical documentation preserved the true authorship of one object, allowing
its makers — previously invisible to the formal historical record — to be
named and credited.

**WHAT THAT EXPLANATION MAY FAIL TO EXPLAIN**: It leaves open how many
structurally identical quilts, without a surviving pinned note, are still
attributed by the "would otherwise have been assumed" default — meaning the
correction here says less about this specific object and more about a
standing classification bias (elite fine needlework = presumed made by the
lady of the house) that only gets overturned when an accident of physical
survival intervenes, not through any systematic review.

**POSSIBLE HIDDEN MECHANISM (HYPOTHESIS)**: Decorative-arts attribution
practice may default enslaved or servant labor out of the record
specifically because the formal record (bills of sale, estate inventories,
family provenance chains) is built around property owners, not the people
who did the work — so authorship correction is structurally dependent on
informal survivals like a pinned note, rather than on the formal
documentation the field otherwise relies on.

**WHAT WOULD HAVE TO BE VERIFIED**:
- Who wrote the note, when, and what exactly it says verbatim (the article paraphrases rather than quotes it in full)
- How many other quilts in the American Folk Art Museum's or comparable collections carry the "assumed maker = female head of household" attribution with no equivalent corrective document, to assess how systemic the default is
- Whether curatorial catalog language for this quilt (wall text, collection database entry) now states the makers as primary/sole attribution or still lists the plantation family in any capacity

**WHAT WOULD MAKE THIS A BAD ARTICLE**: Treating this as a single feel-good
correction story about one quilt and its makers being "finally recognized,"
without asking the harder question the fragment raises — how attribution
defaults for an entire genre of object were set, and how many are still
uncorrected because no note survived to intervene.
