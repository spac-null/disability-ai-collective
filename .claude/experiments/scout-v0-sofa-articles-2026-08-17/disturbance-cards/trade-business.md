## Card 1 — Retail chargeback dispute windows

**SOURCE:** SPS Commerce SupplierWiki, "How to Systematically Dispute and Prevent Invalid
Deductions from Major Retailers" (Bekah Tatem, Sr. Content Writer)
**URL / SOURCE ID:** https://www.spscommerce.com/community/articles/how-to-systematically-dispute-and-prevent-invalid-deductions-from-major-retailers
**DATE:** Last updated 2026-06-05

**EXACT DISTURBANCE FRAGMENT:**
"Walmart accepts disputes on deductions less than two years old. Other retailers have far
shorter windows, with some as tight as 30 to 60 days from the date of deduction." ...
"Missing those windows means loss of revenue, regardless of how strong the case is."

**LOCAL CONTEXT:** The article is a practical guide for consumer-goods suppliers on
recovering money retailers deduct from invoices for shipping errors, packaging
violations, pricing discrepancies, etc. — a routine, unglamorous back-office process
covered constantly in supply-chain trade press.

**WHY THIS IS STRANGE:** The system doesn't adjudicate whether a deduction was correct.
It adjudicates whether the supplier noticed in time. A chargeback that was flatly wrong —
billed in error, for a violation that didn't happen — becomes permanently, contractually
"true" the moment a clock runs out, with no relationship to the underlying facts.

**WHAT THE SOURCE ITSELF SAYS IS HAPPENING:** Retailers impose short, retailer-specific
dispute deadlines as a matter of contract/portal policy; the article frames this as a
process-discipline problem — suppliers need better tracking systems to catch deductions
before the window closes.

**WHAT THAT EXPLANATION MAY FAIL TO EXPLAIN:** It doesn't address why validity and
timing are decoupled at all — i.e., why a demonstrably invalid deduction isn't
correctable after 61 days when the paperwork proving it's wrong still exists. Framing it
as a "supplier tooling" problem quietly accepts that retailers keep money regardless of
fault once the clock lapses.

**POSSIBLE HIDDEN MECHANISM (HYPOTHESIS):** Short, inconsistent-across-retailer windows
may function less as an efficiency mechanism and more as a structural extraction: cited
industry figures (elsewhere in this search — Femfounded) put 10–20% of deductions as
invalid and going unchallenged, worth billions annually — a range wide enough that a
short window quietly monetizes suppliers' administrative overload, independent of who
was actually right.

**WHAT WOULD HAVE TO BE VERIFIED:**
- Actual dollar volume retailers retain annually specifically from disputes filed too
  late (as opposed to disputes lost on the merits)
- Whether window lengths correlate with retailer negotiating leverage over supplier size
  (i.e., do small suppliers get shorter windows than large ones with the same retailer)
- Whether any retailer publishes its own error rate in issuing deductions
- Interviews with suppliers who tried and failed to dispute past the window with strong
  evidence in hand

**WHAT WOULD MAKE THIS A BAD ARTICLE:** Treating it as a "supply-chain admin hygiene"
tip piece instead of following the money — i.e., stopping at "get better software" rather
than asking what percentage of total retailer deduction revenue depends on suppliers
simply running out the clock.

## Card 2 — Poultry "tournament" pay system

**SOURCE:** RAFI-USA (Rural Advancement Foundation International), "USDA's Two New Rules
Explained" (Melanie Canales and Aaron Johnson)
**URL / SOURCE ID:** https://www.rafiusa.org/usdas-two-new-rules-explained/
**DATE:** 2024-07-09

**EXACT DISTURBANCE FRAGMENT:**
"the corporation does a detailed analysis of the feed conversion performance of each
grower in that week's 'tournament group.' It then averages these statistics, docks the
pay of the growers whose flocks were found to be below average" — while separately,
"the final weight and feed conversion efficiency of a broiler flock depends mainly on the
initial health and gender of the chicks, the quality and reliable availability of their
feed, and the timing of flock pick-up — all factors that are controlled by the
integrator, not the contract grower."

**LOCAL CONTEXT:** Poultry integrators (e.g., Tyson, Perdue-scale companies) pay contract
growers via a relative-ranking "tournament": growers who deliver flocks that convert feed
to weight better than their peer group get bonuses; growers below the group average get
docked, out of a shared settlement pool.

**WHY THIS IS STRANGE:** The system is presented — and legally structured — as a
performance-based comparison of grower skill. But the inputs that most determine the
outcome (chick quality, feed quality/timing, pickup timing) are supplied and scheduled
entirely by the company running the tournament, not by the growers being ranked.

**WHAT THE SOURCE ITSELF SAYS IS HAPPENING:** USDA's 2024 rule responds to this by
creating a "duty of fair comparison," requiring integrators to account for input
disparities before ranking growers, and to disclose more contract terms up front.

**WHAT THAT EXPLANATION MAY FAIL TO EXPLAIN:** A disclosure/fairness-adjustment
requirement doesn't remove the integrator's structural role as both input-supplier and
scorekeeper. RAFI's own framing calls the net effect an "extractive transfer" — bonuses
funded by penalizing other growers, out of a fixed pool the integrator itself doesn't pay
into — which a comparison-fairness rule doesn't change unless the inputs themselves are
equalized.

**POSSIBLE HIDDEN MECHANISM (HYPOTHESIS):** The tournament may function less as a
performance incentive and more as a variable-cost transfer: because the settlement pool
is fixed and grower-funded (bonuses come from other growers' docked pay, not integrator
profit), the integrator can control both the difficulty of the test and its cost exposure
simultaneously — worse chick/feed batches don't cost the integrator money, they just
redistribute pay among growers.

**WHAT WOULD HAVE TO BE VERIFIED:**
- Actual settlement-pool mechanics at a specific integrator: does docked pay from
  below-average growers directly fund above-average bonuses, or does integrator money
  also flow in?
- Independent data linking chick-batch source/quality to grower ranking outcomes
  (RAFI's claim is asserted, not shown with grower-level data in the excerpt found)
- How USDA's "duty of fair comparison" is actually being audited/enforced post-2024,
  and whether any tournament groups have been found in violation
- Grower-side accounts (contract growers willing to go on record) of feeling penalized
  for chick/feed batches they didn't choose

**WHAT WOULD MAKE THIS A BAD ARTICLE:** Retelling it purely as a "factory farming is
unfair to growers" morality piece — the specific, unusual mechanism (a fixed pool where
docked pay directly funds someone else's bonus, scored on inputs the scorer controls) is
the story; generic unfairness framing would bury it.

## Card 3 — Container detention dispute screenshot rule

**SOURCE:** ONE (Ocean Network Express), "Invoice Disputes" official policy page;
corroborated by MSC's own published "USA Detention and Demurrage" dispute rules, and by
a Federal Maritime Commission ALJ ruling against Hapag-Lloyd (reported by Splash247,
Venable LLP, Seatrade Maritime, FreightWaves) for imposing detention charges when
container-return appointments were provably unavailable.
**URL / SOURCE ID:** https://us.one-line.com/invoice-disputes-landing-page
(cross-ref: https://www.msc.com/en/local-information/america/united-states/msc-usa-demurrage-and-detention)
**DATE:** Policy pages current as of 2026-08; underlying FMC case (Hapag-Lloyd/Golden
State Logistics, Docket 21-09) decided 2022, $2M settlement followed.

**EXACT DISTURBANCE FRAGMENT:**
"If a 'No Empty Return Location' submission does not include a valid screenshot of
appointment unavailability taken the working day prior to the waiver date, no dispute has
been submitted, and the dispute will be rejected as incomplete." Screenshots must be
"taken between 6:00 a.m. and 12:00 p.m. (noon) the day prior to the Waiver date" and
again "between 6:00 a.m. and 9:00 a.m. on the Waiver date," and "for terminals operating
on a two-shift basis (day and night), screenshots must capture appointment unavailability
for both shifts."

**LOCAL CONTEXT:** Ocean carriers charge "detention" fees when a shipper/trucker doesn't
return an empty container within a free-time window. A legitimate defense is that no
terminal appointment slot existed to physically return it — but the carrier's own dispute
process for proving that defense.

**WHY THIS IS STRANGE:** The carrier both sets the appointment system that may be making
return impossible, charges the fee for the resulting failure to return, and then requires
the trucker to have pre-emptively, defensively documented that impossibility in a precise
multi-shift, multi-window screenshot ritual on the specific calendar day — before knowing
whether they'll even be charged. Fail to have taken the right screenshot at the right
hour, and the dispute isn't rejected on the merits — it's treated as "not submitted"
at all.

**WHAT THE SOURCE ITSELF SAYS IS HAPPENING:** The carriers frame this as evidentiary
rigor: a screenshot is the only reliable proof that appointment slots were genuinely
unavailable at the relevant time, given how fast appointment systems can change.

**WHAT THAT EXPLANATION MAY FAIL TO EXPLAIN:** It doesn't explain why the burden of
proof (and the cost of failing to meet a rigid procedural formality) falls entirely on
the party being charged, for a condition largely caused by the charging party's own
terminal/appointment infrastructure — the exact pattern an FMC administrative law judge
found "unreasonable" in the parallel Hapag-Lloyd case, which held the carrier liable when
"there were not sufficient appointments to return the containers."

**POSSIBLE HIDDEN MECHANISM (HYPOTHESIS):** A procedural requirement this specific may
function less as fraud-prevention and more as a functional un-appealability filter: if
few truckers realize, in the moment, that they need day-and-night, two-window,
timestamped screenshots to preserve a future dispute, most valid disputes never even
reach the "was this fee reasonable" stage — they're pre-filtered out on a technicality,
which is a much cheaper outcome for the carrier than adjudicating congestion fairness at
scale.

**WHAT WOULD HAVE TO BE VERIFIED:**
- What fraction of detention disputes carriers reject as "incomplete" (missing
  screenshot) versus reject "on the merits" — carriers don't appear to publish this
- Whether the appointment-booking systems referenced are run by the terminal operator,
  the carrier, or a third party, and who controls slot availability in practice
- How this screenshot regime originated — was it imposed voluntarily, or as a direct
  response to the FMC's 2022 Hapag-Lloyd finding and 2024 interpretive rule?
- Trucking-company or drayage-operator interviews on how often they've had a legitimate
  no-appointment claim rejected purely for a documentation timing miss

**WHAT WOULD MAKE THIS A BAD ARTICLE:** A generic "supply chain fees are confusing"
trend piece. The specific, load-bearing detail is the exact-hour screenshot window and
the "no dispute has been submitted" language — losing that specificity loses the whole
disturbance.

## Card 4 — Depreciating labor in "actual cash value" insurance settlements

**SOURCE:** Property Insurance Coverage Law Blog, "Michigan Takes a Stand: New Bulletin
Prohibits Depreciation of Labor Costs in Property Insurance Claims" (Chip Merlin);
corroborated by IRMI's "Depreciation of Labor in Calculating ACV: Yes or No?" (case-law
survey)
**URL / SOURCE ID:** https://www.propertyinsurancecoveragelaw.com/blog/michigan-takes-a-stand-new-bulletin-prohibits-depreciation-of-labor-costs-in-property-insurance-claims/
**DATE:** 2024-09-24 (describing Michigan DIFS Bulletin 2024-18-INS, issued 2024-07-03;
Alaska's parallel bulletin issued 2024-05-07 and withdrawn 2025-01-23)

**EXACT DISTURBANCE FRAGMENT:**
Michigan's bulletin bars insurers from applying depreciation to "nontangible" claim
components — defined to include "labor, taxes, fees, and overhead and profit" — when
settling a property claim on an Actual Cash Value basis. Alaska's rationale for its own,
near-identical ban: "labor does not lose value and remains unaffected by any material
property decline." Yet within nine months, Alaska withdrew its bulletin — while courts
in other states (Colorado, Kansas) have upheld depreciating labor on the reasoning that
not depreciating it would give the policyholder "a windfall based on labor costs she
never incurred."

**LOCAL CONTEXT:** When a home insurer pays a claim on an Actual Cash Value (ACV, not
Replacement Cost Value) basis, it depreciates the damaged item's value for age/wear
before paying out, with a second payment released later once repairs are completed —
this is a decades-old, industry-standard settlement method covered routinely in property
insurance trade press.

**WHY THIS IS STRANGE:** "Actual cash value" is conceptually a physical-depreciation
concept — a ten-year-old roof is worth less than a new one because material wears out.
Labor to install a new roof today has no age and nothing to physically depreciate; it's
priced at today's labor market rate regardless of when the original roof was installed.
Applying a depreciation percentage to labor is applying a physical-wear formula to a
non-physical, present-tense cost — and state regulators, insurers, and courts are
actively split on whether that's even legal, with at least one regulator reversing
itself within a year under undisclosed pressure.

**WHAT THE SOURCE ITSELF SAYS IS HAPPENING:** Regulators frame this as clarifying an
ambiguous term ("actual cash value") that some insurers have been interpreting
aggressively; insurers argue that if only materials depreciate, policyholders get an
unearned bonus by later hiring cheaper labor or doing the repair themselves.

**WHAT THAT EXPLANATION MAY FAIL TO EXPLAIN:** Neither side's framing addresses why a
concept this basic — what a house is "actually worth" right now — has produced
opposite legal answers in different states for years, or why a regulator (Alaska) would
adopt a rule and then rescind it within nine months without the underlying economic
reality of labor depreciation changing at all in that window.

**POSSIBLE HIDDEN MECHANISM (HYPOTHESIS):** The rapid Alaska reversal suggests the "is
labor tangible" question functions less as a settled actuarial or legal principle and
more as a live lobbying battleground where insurer trade associations (e.g., APCIA, which
filed comment letters opposing similar restrictions elsewhere) can get a consumer-
protective bulletin undone quickly once the underwriting cost impact becomes visible —
meaning the amount a homeowner recovers on an identical claim may depend more on which
state's regulator won the most recent round of that fight than on any stable definition
of "actual cash value."

**WHAT WOULD HAVE TO BE VERIFIED:**
- What specifically caused Alaska's reversal (industry comment record, any legal
  challenge, change in division leadership) — not established from the sources found
- A full state-by-state count and how it has shifted year to year, not just a snapshot
- The actual dollar difference this makes on a representative claim (e.g., typical roof
  replacement labor cost, and the depreciation rate insurers apply to it)
- Whether the APCIA comment letter (Maryland, 2025-05-30) succeeded in blocking or
  softening the rule it was opposing — the letter's content was not retrievable (403)
- Court split beyond the two cases each way already found — Riggins (MO) and the WA
  administrative code appear to be the "against" precedents; Basham (CO) and Graves (KS)
  the "for" — is one side a clear majority nationally?

**WHAT WOULD MAKE THIS A BAD ARTICLE:** A dry "know your policy terms" consumer-finance
explainer. The disturbance isn't that ACV exists or that depreciation is confusing to
homeowners — it's the instability of the definition itself under regulatory and industry
pressure, on both sides, in real time.
