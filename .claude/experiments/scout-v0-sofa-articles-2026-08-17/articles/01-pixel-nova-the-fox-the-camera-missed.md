In June 2013, a team of ecologists set fifty-six cameras across roughly half a hectare of pine forest in North Carolina, spaced ten metres apart in a tight grid, and left them running for a month. Roland Kays, Allison Hody, David Jachowski and Arielle Parsons wanted to know something that sounds almost too basic to be a research question: when an animal walks past a camera trap, does the camera actually see it?

The honest answer, once they counted, was no. Not reliably. A deer walking through the grid produced a clear, identifiable photo 86 percent of the time. A gray fox managed 29 percent. Across the four species studied, cameras failed to produce an identifiable photo of a passing animal — no photo at all, or a blank, unusable one — somewhere between 14 and 71 percent of the time, and the most common single failure was an animal moving out of frame before the shutter caught up with it.

I read papers like this for the sentence about what the instrument couldn't hold, not the sentence about what it found. That habit isn't abstract for me. An interpreter can't translate simultaneously — they have to take in a stretch of what's said, then choose the best rendering, which puts me a small distance behind everyone else in the room, following events a beat late. Laughing at a joke just after everyone else, because it only reaches me through that gap, is a small, recurring version of the same fact: something can be real, said, happening, and still not arrive in a form that registers on time. I've also sat on the other end of an official channel that was technically available and useless in practice — a support line that only takes phone calls, which for a Deaf person is information that exists somewhere and cannot be reached at the moment it's actually needed. You learn, from enough of that, that "the information didn't come through" and "the information wasn't there" are two different claims, and that a mediating system will happily get mistaken for a window.

Here's where the fox comes back in. The paper doesn't just report a failure rate — it explains where it comes from. Camera traps are triggered by a passive infrared sensor that reads a change in heat signature in front of the lens. Trigger probability, the researchers found, "appeared to increase with the body mass of the target species." Ranked by weight, the four animals in the study lined up exactly by how reliably the camera fired: gray fox lowest, then raccoon, then coyote, then deer highest. A smaller, faster animal generates a smaller, briefer heat signal and is more likely to have already left the frame by the time a slow shutter catches up. The camera isn't failing at random. It's failing in a shape.

That's the sentence I actually wanted, and it took me a beat to understand what it meant. I had assumed — the way you assume things you've never had reason to check — that a device either records an event or it doesn't, and that the misses would scatter roughly evenly across whatever passed by. Reading Table 4 disabused me of that. The device's blind spot has the same shape as the variable it exists to measure. Small, fast, rare animals are exactly the ones the sensor is worst at holding.

Which the paper's own authors flag, in one line easy to walk past: "This problem would be worse with rare species, which are often the target of faunal surveys." Say that plainly and it stops being a footnote about instrument calibration and starts being a problem about what "rare" means. Camera-trap surveys are one of the standard tools ecologists use to estimate how common or scarce a species is in a given forest. If detection probability tracks body size and speed rather than staying constant across species, then part of what a "rarity ranking" measures isn't how many foxes are out there. It's how bad the camera is at foxes specifically, laundered through statistics until it looks like population data.

This is not a new problem dressed up. Ecologists have had a formal name for the general category for more than two decades — "imperfect detection," established by MacKenzie and colleagues in 2002, with a whole modeling architecture built to let detection probability vary by covariate rather than assume it's constant. The architecture exists. What the Kays paper shows is narrower and sharper: this particular covariate — body mass, correlated with speed — isn't a routine input. A 2023 follow-up by DeWitt and Cocksedge built an entire experimental framework specifically to measure it species by species, camera model by camera model, because it still isn't something a standard survey corrects for by default. Two decades of formal vocabulary for "we might be missing things," and the actual pattern of what gets missed is still something a research team has to go looking for on purpose.

---

I went looking for the same shape somewhere entirely different, partly to see if I'd talked myself into something that only holds for cameras in forests. I found it in the ocean. Marine biologists trying to estimate biodiversity from underwater recordings run into an almost identical problem: a hydrophone can only register an animal above the ambient noise floor at a certain range, and that range depends heavily on how loud the animal is, which correlates — imperfectly, but really — with how big it is. A 2020 review in the Royal Society's open-access journal states it without hedging: acoustic surveys are "biased towards more gregarious, larger, louder or easily detected species leading towards erroneous richness measurements." Swap "loud" for "large-bodied" and you have the same sentence Kays could have written about his forest. Two completely different instruments, one built to watch and one built to listen, both quietly favoring the animal that's easiest for the instrument itself to hold onto, and both producing a "diversity" number that is partly a description of the recording equipment.

I don't think this is a story about faulty gadgets. Every recording instrument has a shape — a set of things it's built to notice and a set of things that fall outside its aperture — and that shape doesn't announce itself. It just quietly becomes the record, and the record quietly becomes "what happened," and eventually nobody in the room remembers there was a step in between. The forest didn't get less foxy in the weeks those cameras ran. The fox was there the whole time, at whatever rate foxes actually pass through half a hectare of loblolly pine. What changed, camera to camera, frame to frame, was how much of that fox's presence survived the trip from the world into the data set — and the animals for whom the least survived were, structurally, the ones the surveys most need to count accurately, because they're the ones already closest to invisible.

A researcher walking that grid in July 2013 could have stood in the exact spot where a gray fox passed and left nothing behind but a blank frame or no frame at all — not because nothing happened there, but because the instrument's threshold for "this counts as having happened" was tuned, without anyone deciding it on purpose, to something closer to the size and speed of a deer. Trigger probability by body mass: 0.42, 0.61, 0.72, 0.91. Ranked, in order, exactly like a scale nobody built to be a scale, and read for twenty years as if it simply told you how many foxes were in the woods.
