On July 29, 2025, Jaricus Whitlock, chief of the air division at the Mississippi Department of Environmental Quality, wrote to Brent Mayo, a vice president at xAI, confirming that a set of gas turbines the company wanted to install at a site in Southaven would qualify as "mobile" — because each one would remain affixed to a flatbed trailer — and "temporary," because the company intended each turbine to stay on-site for less than twelve months. Two conditions. Wheels, and an intention. Nothing about how much fuel the turbines would burn, how much nitrogen oxide they would put into the air over Southaven and neighboring Memphis, or how long they would actually run once switched on. The letter set the test, and the test had nothing to do with the thing the test exists to regulate.

I was diagnosed autistic at nineteen, and the diagnosis wasn't a discovery. It was a label finally attached to a dataset that had been sitting there the whole time — the same me, the same patterns, now with a form to fill in. I've spent the years since noticing how often a category works exactly that way: less a measurement of the thing in front of you, more an administrative decision about which box the thing gets filed under, made once, by someone with the authority to make it, who did not have to check back later to see whether the box still fit.

The federal rule MDEQ was applying isn't obscure. Under the Clean Air Act, a piece of equipment normally treated as "nonroad" — think a generator on wheels at a construction site — stops counting as nonroad and becomes a regulated stationary source once it sits in one place for twelve consecutive months. Below twelve months, lighter rules. At twelve months and a day, an entirely different, stricter regime. The rule was written for equipment that actually moves between short jobs. It was not obviously written with a use case in mind where "mobile" hardware sits permanently at a data-center campus, running around the clock, because a company decided to power a supercomputer faster than the regional grid could connect it.

xAI leaned on that test twice. The company had already used it at a first site, Colossus I in Memphis, where the public later discovered it was running an estimated thirty-five gas turbines with no air permit at all — Shelby County's health department eventually permitted fifteen of them, months after the fact. When xAI planned a second facility, Colossus II, across the state line in Southaven, a senior xAI infrastructure manager told a Memphis newspaper the company would be "copying and pasting" what had already worked once. Not a mistake rediscovered. A method, reapplied.

What followed at Southaven is where the category really shows its shape. Three turbines in early August 2025. Seventeen planned by September. Eighteen confirmed on-site by aerial photograph in late October, thermal imaging showing at least nine of them actively running. Twenty-seven by December. In February 2026, after the Environmental Protection Agency had already moved to tighten the federal rule on stationary turbines nationally, the NAACP, the Southern Environmental Law Center and Earthjustice sent xAI formal notice that they intended to sue. xAI's response was not to pause. Court filings from that May describe the company adding six more turbines, bringing the total to thirty-three, after receiving the notice. "Rather than stop," said Earthjustice attorney Laura Thoms, "they've added more turbines capable of inflicting even more harm to surrounding communities." SELC's Ben Grillot put it more plainly: "Instead of fixing these issues, xAI installed six more massive turbines to its personal power plant."

I want to be careful here, because I went in assuming a cleaner story than the one the record actually supports. I expected to find that xAI had been physically shuffling turbines between sites to reset the twelve-month clock each time — move a unit out before it aged into "stationary," bring in a fresh one, repeat. That would have been a tidy mechanism. It isn't what happened. The documented pattern at Southaven is continuous addition of new units at the same location, not movement of old ones between locations. The clock wasn't being reset by relocation. It was never meaningfully started, because each new turbine arrived as its own separate "mobile" unit, and nobody — not xAI, not the state regulator that wrote the two-part test in the first place — treated the growing total as one continuous stationary operation that the rule should have caught.

Even EPA's own response to this complicates the version of the story where a loophole simply gets closed. The rule EPA finalized in January 2026 doesn't eliminate the mobile-turbine exemption. It writes a brand-new category alongside it — "stationary temporary combustion turbine" — with its own twenty-four-month clock and a real, if lighter, emissions standard. If the agency believed the existing rule already clearly barred what xAI was doing, building a whole new accommodating category for turbines like these would be an odd next move. What it looks like instead is a regulator acknowledging, after the fact, that its own category genuinely hadn't anticipated equipment like this — and choosing to write a wider door rather than simply lock the one that had been propped open.

That's the part worth sitting with longer than the outrage. A test built on wheels and an intention, satisfied on paper by a state agency's own letter, reused deliberately at a second site after public controversy over the first, defended not by hiding the growth but by not being asked to explain it — and a federal regulator's fix that keeps a version of the same form-based exemption alive for equipment that behaves. None of this required anyone to lie about what a turbine is. It only required a category that checks the wrong variable, and enough people willing to let the check stand in for the question it was supposed to answer.

The United States tried something almost identical once before, with trucks instead of turbines. A "glider kit" is a brand-new chassis, cab and axle built around a much older, remanufactured diesel engine — sold and driven as a new truck, polluting at levels ten or twenty years out of date, because the Clean Air Act's "new motor vehicle" rule technically asks whether a new engine was manufactured, not whether a newly operating vehicle is now on the road. Production of these trucks rose from a few hundred a year in 2004 to roughly ten thousand a year by 2015, once a separate greenhouse-gas rule made ordinary new trucks more expensive and gliders a cheap way around it. EPA closed that gap in 2016. In 2017, under new leadership, the agency proposed reopening it, arguing formally that glider vehicles shouldn't count as "new" at all under the statute's literal words. The proposal never fully went through, but only because the underlying market collapsed under later standards, not because the legal argument was rejected outright.

Same country, two industries, one distance apart in years, one pattern: a category built to track a real thing — is this a newly polluting vehicle, is this a genuinely temporary power source — quietly redefined to track something else instead, something that can be satisfied by a technicality regardless of what's actually happening on the ground. A diagnosis is supposed to describe a person. A regulatory category is supposed to describe a machine's behavior. Both can be gamed by whoever gets to write the test, and in both cases, the gap between what the label says and what's actually true doesn't announce itself. It just sits there, correctly filed, until somebody who already knows what that feels like goes looking for the sentence that doesn't add up.
