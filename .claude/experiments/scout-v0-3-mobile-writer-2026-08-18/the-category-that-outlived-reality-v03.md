Mississippi's Department of Environmental Quality has one specific power over the gas turbines running outside Southaven: it decides what to call them. A turbine can be permanent, which brings the full weight of state air-permitting law down on it, or it can be mobile and temporary, which brings almost none. The agency makes that call, and only the agency can make it. But by the time the turbines at xAI's data center site had multiplied into the dozens, the same agency had told the company something else. It did not need to report each new one as it arrived.

That is an unusual thing for a regulator to say about equipment it is supposed to be watching. The office with the authority to decide what these machines were had also decided it did not need to keep being told what they were becoming.

To see how that happened, go back to July 29, 2025, when there were only three.

On that date, Jaricus Whitlock, who runs the air division at MDEQ, wrote to Brent Mayo, a vice president at xAI, confirming a determination the company had asked for. The turbines xAI planned to run at its site on Stanton Road South, Whitlock wrote, would be mobile, because each one sat on a flatbed trailer rather than a poured foundation. They would be temporary, because xAI intended each one to leave the site within twelve months. Both halves of the test concerned a single machine, considered on its own. Neither half asked how many machines there would eventually be.

The second half rested on nothing the state could check for itself. It was xAI's own stated intention, written down and accepted. Whitlock's letter added one more line, asking the company to operate "in a manner that minimizes emissions." It was a request, not a condition. Nothing in the letter required xAI to honor it, and nothing in the letter would have caught it if the company hadn't.

Then the turbines arrived faster than anyone outside the company appeared to be tracking. By early September, xAI told the state to expect seventeen. By the end of October, aerial photographs, backed by thermal imaging, counted eighteen on site, with at least nine of them already running. That December, xAI told MDEQ nine more were coming. The total reached twenty-seven.

On February 13, 2026, the NAACP, the Southern Environmental Law Center, and Earthjustice sent xAI formal notice that they intended to sue over the twenty-seven turbines then on record. xAI's answer to being placed on legal notice was to keep building. Within weeks, six more turbines went up, bringing the count to thirty-three.

By that May, with the total past forty-six, Mississippi's environmental agency said so plainly that it left little room for a different reading: xAI did not have to notify the agency each time it added another turbine under the temporary-mobile designation. No number was attached to that answer. Nothing in the record says how many turbines would have had to arrive before the agency wanted to hear about it. The rule written for three trailers in the summer of 2025 was still the rule, and it still applied one machine at a time.

By August, one count put the total at sixty-nine.

In July 2026, MDEQ issued something closer to an ending than a deadline. The turbines classified a year earlier as certain to leave within twelve months were given a retirement schedule instead. The original deadline had been December 31, 2026. Under the new schedule, thirteen of the turbines were granted permission to keep running past it. Three of those thirteen were granted more time again — enough, by the agency's own accounting, to keep them in place until roughly July 2027.

Sixty-nine turbines, each one still sitting on its own trailer, still temporary on paper, still answerable one at a time to the two words a state regulator wrote down for three machines two summers before.
