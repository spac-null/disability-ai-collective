Follow Your Nose

ARTICLE
You can’t walk into a gallery at the Edinburgh Art Festival without learning about some subcultural genius or long-lost creative you’ve never heard of. That’s the whole point of the thing, really — a city-wide celebration of the underground and the unheralded. Queer narratives, secret histories, undiscovered archives. By the time you’re done you’ll have uncovered so many hidden stories you’ll feel like Columbo.

But EAF is also a big, unwieldy, confusing thing. Curated displays, satellite exhibitions, performances, off-site projects — it’s impossible to figure out how any of it fits together. You’ve just got to follow your nose and hope you sniff out something you like.

The language keeps returning to those verbs of movement and chance. Walk. Uncover. Follow your nose. Sniff out. Figure out. They describe a visitor free to drift, to like or not like, to move along.

Then the pattern appears. Sandra George, a black photographer and photojournalist who documented life on the margins of inner-city Edinburgh, captured the impoverished Craigmillar area: protests run out of living rooms and town halls, people in hostels and housing schemes, skinheads, labourers, Hare Krishnas, Indians, Jamaicans, black and white. She appears throughout her own show in self-portraits. Like a lot of people in EAF, she never showed her work publicly in her lifetime, and was only discovered after her death in 2013.

That sentence does something to the cheerful verbs. The visitor who follows her nose and stumbles across George is stumbling across someone who never got to present herself. The pleasure of uncovering was only made available by the fact that the maker never got to be seen.

The festival keeps repeating the pattern. At Ingleby Gallery, a group show centres on Frank Walter (1926–2009), an Antiguan painter, writer, poet and polymath who died having never shown any of the thousands of paintings and drawings he amassed over his lifetime. His work is small, subtle, strange — rainbows and stars and smiling faces on ripped cardboard and fading paper. Hung beside giants of contemporary painting, it eclipses them.

Japanese illustrator Sadao Hasegawa (1945–1999) offers an eye-popping display fusing myth and hardcore eroticism — work that stayed unpublished and unseen in his lifetime. You guessed it. The festival has made the posthumous discovery into a house style, a rhythm you learn to anticipate. The source never tells us why any of these artists went unseen while alive — only that they did.

“Follow your nose” is a phrase for a browser with time and appetite, free to like or not like, to drift. That works when EAF trades in hard-edged contemporary art. Eva Rothschild’s rubber-hose sculptures at Fruitmarket: “I love it, but I’m not sure how.” Anne Hardy’s installation is mind-meltingly indecipherable, way too knowingly obtuse, yet it works well. You can shrug at other pieces and move along. These works can withstand a browser’s whim.

But George, Walter and Hasegawa cannot. They are relying entirely on how they’re presented. If contemporary artists and institutions want to tell hidden stories, they have a duty — to viewers and to their subjects — to tell them clearly.

That’s the tension the language of stumbling carries. The festival can just be filled with interesting, diverse art and loads of undiscovered narratives. The living visitor who follows a nose has that choice. The dead artists whose discovery made the narratives possible had no such choice, and the festival’s breezy verbs never quite reckon with the difference.

*Follow Your Nose*

Most of us like the idea of stumbling across something unexpected. We tell ourselves that the best discoveries happen when we are free to wander, to follow our curiosity without a map. The Edinburgh Art Festival leans hard into that feeling. You can’t walk into one of its galleries without learning about some subcultural genius or long-lost creative you’ve never heard of. Queer narratives, secret histories, undiscovered archives: by the time you’re done you’ll have uncovered so many hidden stories you’ll feel like Columbo.

The official invitation is simple. The festival is big, unwieldy and confusing — curated displays, satellite exhibitions, performances, off-site projects. It’s impossible to figure out how any of it fits together. You’ve just got to follow your nose and hope you sniff out something you like. That language of walking, sniffing, uncovering and stumbling is repeated so often it starts to sound like the whole point.

Then you meet the artists whose work makes the language possible.

At the City Art Gallery hangs the photography of Sandra George. A black photographer and photojournalist, she documented life on the margins of inner-city Edinburgh. From her position as a semi-outsider she recorded the impoverished Craigmillar area: protests organised in living rooms and town halls, people in hostels and housing schemes, skinheads, labourers, Hare Krishnas, Indians, Jamaicans, black and white. She appears throughout the show in self-portraits, often with her young children, frequently in desperately bleak places. Like many of the people featured in the festival, she never showed her work publicly in her lifetime. She was only discovered after her death in 2013.

The cheerful verbs suddenly feel different. The visitor who follows her nose and stumbles across George is stumbling across someone who never got to present herself. The pleasure of uncovering exists only because the maker remained unseen.

The same pattern returns at Ingleby Gallery. A group show centres on Frank Walter (1926–2009), the Antiguan painter, writer, poet and polymath who died having never exhibited any of the thousands of paintings and drawings he produced. His pieces are small, subtle and strange — rainbows, stars and smiling faces on ripped cardboard and fading paper. Placed beside major contemporary painters, they quietly outshine them. Nearby, the Japanese illustrator Sadao Hasegawa (1945–1999) fills a room with work that fuses myth and hardcore eroticism — work that stayed unpublished and unseen while he was alive. The festival has turned posthumous discovery into a house style, a rhythm you learn to expect. It never explains why these artists went unseen; it only records that they did.

This is why the wandering matters. “Follow your nose” describes a visitor with time and appetite, free to like or dislike, to drift onward. That freedom works with the living. Eva Rothschild’s rubber-hose sculptures can be loved without quite knowing why. Anne Hardy’s installation can be called mind-meltingly indecipherable and still be allowed to work. You can shrug at other pieces and move on. Those works can survive a browser’s whim.

George, Walter and Hasegawa cannot. Their presence depends entirely on how they are shown. If institutions want to tell hidden stories, they have a duty — to viewers and to the people whose work is being shown — to tell them clearly.

That is the quiet tension the festival never quite names. The living visitor who follows a nose still has a choice. The artists whose absence made the discoveries possible never did. The breezy verbs of wandering keep rolling along as if the difference does not exist.
