# Following Your Nose Through Edinburgh's Hidden Stories

The way Edinburgh Art Festival describes itself, you can't walk into a gallery without learning about some subcultural genius or long-lost creative you've never heard of. That is the promise and the method both. There is no clean route through it, no map that resolves the whole thing into a shape. There are curated displays, satellite exhibitions, performances, off-site projects, and it's impossible to figure out how any of it fits together. You've just got to follow your nose and hope you sniff out something you like.

Follow it far enough and you arrive at two artists worth setting side by side. Sandra George (1957–2013) was a black photographer and photojournalist who documented life on the margins of inner-city Edinburgh, working from her semi-outsider's perspective in the impoverished Craigmillar area. Like a lot of people in the festival, she never showed her work publicly in her lifetime, and was only discovered after her death in 2013. Frank Walter (1926–2009) was an Antiguan painter, writer, poet and polymath — the work at Ingleby Gallery is small, subtle, strange, painted on ripped cardboard and fading paper, rainbows and stars and trees bending at weird angles. He died having never shown any of the thousands of paintings and drawings he amassed over his lifetime.

Two lives, then, and two bodies of work that stayed out of view while the people who made them were alive. It is worth pausing on what happens to the festival's favourite word once these facts are in the room.

*Discovery* is doing a great deal of work at Edinburgh. It is the register the whole event chooses for itself — undiscovered archives, secret histories, the underground and the unheralded, one previously unknown name after another. And in the mouth of a visitor moving through the galleries, the word means something exact and something perfectly ordinary. To discover Sandra George is to stand in front of her self-portraits with her young son, her protests and her housing schemes, her kids playing and laughing in bleak places, and to encounter all of it for the first time. To discover Frank Walter is to find his weird little paintings eclipsing the Rose Wylie and the Lynette Yiadom-Boakye hung beside them. The discovery is yours, in the present tense, in the room. It is the meeting of a person and a picture that person had not seen before.

The festival keeps its faith in clarity here. If contemporary artists and institutions want to tell hidden stories, the reviewer thinks, they have a duty — to viewers and to their subjects — to tell them clearly. That is one person's conviction about how the telling ought to go, and it sits well against work as direct as George's.

But hold the two senses of the word together and a small seam opens. The discovery the review celebrates is the visitor's, now, encountering work previously unknown to them. And some of that celebrated work had remained unshown while its maker was alive. George never showed her work publicly in her lifetime. Walter died having never shown any of his thousands of paintings and drawings.

So the word turns on its axis without anyone announcing the turn. The visitor discovers, in a single afternoon, what a lifetime did not put on a wall. The encounter is new; the not-being-seen is old. Those are not the same event, and the festival's cheerful vocabulary of discovery lets them share a single word.
