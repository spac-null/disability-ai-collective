# Following Your Nose Through Edinburgh

You can't walk into a gallery at the Edinburgh art festival without learning about some subcultural genius or long-lost creative you've never heard of. That is the texture of the whole thing: curated displays, satellite exhibitions, performances, off-site projects, none of it fitting together in any way you could diagram. There is no map that makes sense of it. You've just got to follow your nose and hope you sniff out something you like.

Two of the people you might sniff out are Sandra George and Frank Walter.

George (1957–2013) was a black photographer and photojournalist who documented life on the margins of inner-city Edinburgh. Like a lot of people in the festival, she never showed her work publicly in her lifetime, and was only discovered after her death in 2013. From her semi-outsider's perspective she photographed the impoverished Craigmillar area — protests run out of living rooms and town halls, people in hostels and housing schemes, skinheads and labourers and Hare Krishnas, kids everywhere, playing and laughing in bleak places. She appears throughout her show in self-portraits, often with her young son. This is work about Edinburgh that deserves to be seen far outside the city's confines.

Walter (1926–2009) was an Antiguan painter, writer, poet and polymath who died having never shown any of the thousands of paintings and drawings he amassed over his lifetime. His work is small, subtle, strange: painted on ripped cardboard and fading paper, rainbows and stars and smiling faces, dancing women, trees that bend at weird angles. In a group show he hangs among giants of contemporary painting — a big Rose Wylie, a Lynette Yiadom-Boakye — and eclipses them all with his weird little paintings.

So here is the word again, the one the festival keeps offering you at every threshold: discovery. Undiscovered archives, undiscovered narratives, previously undiscovered marginalised artists. It is the festival's whole way of talking about itself, and it is not a bad MO for a city-wide event — a celebration of the underground and the unheralded, hidden stories surfacing in gallery after gallery until you feel like Columbo.

But watch what the word is actually doing when it lands on George and on Walter. She was discovered after her death. He died having shown nothing. The discovery is real, but it is happening now, on this side of the gallery wall, in the present tense of a visitor who walks in and meets the work for the first time. That is the encounter the festival is naming: yours. The photographs and paintings are new to you. They were not, in their makers' lifetimes, shown.

Those are two different things sitting inside the same word. Discovery, as the festival uses it, belongs to the viewer's present — the moment of first sight, the pleasure of the previously unknown. But some of the work being celebrated in exactly those terms had remained unshown while its maker was alive. The word points forward, toward the person just now finding it. The condition it describes points backward, to a life in which the finding never happened.

If contemporary artists and institutions want to tell hidden stories, they have a duty — to viewers and to their subjects — to tell them clearly. That is the reviewer's own conviction, not the festival's line about itself, and it is worth holding onto here, because clarity is precisely what the two senses of the word tend to blur. To be discovered, in the festival's mouth, is to be seen at last. But being seen at last is not the same as having been seen. The visitor's first encounter and the maker's lifetime of unshown work are not one event told twice. They are two events, and only one of them is happening in the gallery.
