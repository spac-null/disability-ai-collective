# The Unindexed

The review makes a promise in the shape of a body. "You can't walk into a gallery without learning about some subcultural genius or long-lost creative you've never heard of." Read once, this is praise. Read twice, it is a set of instructions: walk in, learn, walk on. The festival's celebration of the underground arrives through a verb that presumes a spontaneous, mobile viewer — someone who can "follow your nose and hope you sniff out something you like" across curated displays, satellite exhibitions, performances, off-site projects scattered from City Art Gallery to the top of Calton Hill to "the horses and goats of Jupiter Artland."

So the working suspicion is easy to state: the festival's discovery premise is coded as an ambulatory act. The "unheralded" it celebrates is pre-filtered through an access architecture that quietly selects who gets to do the uncovering. If you cannot follow your nose — if the nose does not move freely across a dispersed city — the whole MO stops working on you. The delight is contingent on a body the prose never thinks to name.

That much holds. But it starts to narrow the moment you ask what, exactly, is being discovered.

Sandra George never showed her work publicly in her lifetime; she was "only discovered after her death in 2013." Frank Walter "died having never shown any of the thousands of paintings and drawings he amassed." Sadao Hasegawa's work "stayed unpublished and unseen in his lifetime." This is not incidental. It is the festival's entire subject: people who were never legibly catalogued, never shown, never entered into any clean index while alive. The unheralded are unheralded *because* they resisted, or were shut out of, the legible systems that would have made them findable.

This is where the neat version of the complaint collides with itself. If the demand is for a navigable interface — a tidy map, a sequenced route, everything fitting together — then that demand argues against the very archive-diving that surfaces this work at all. You cannot ask the festival to be a clean catalogue of things that survived precisely by escaping catalogues. The messiness the review resents ("impossible to figure out how any of it fits together") might be structurally faithful to its subjects rather than merely careless. There is no orderly path to the person who left no orderly trail.

So the barrier splits in two, and the split matters.

There is a spatial barrier — the physical scatter, the assumption of a body that can move between venues. That one is real and worth naming plainly. But there is a deeper one that a perfectly ramped building would not solve: a *cognitive* and *temporal* interface. "Follow your nose" is not just a claim about legs. It is a discovery model that demands spontaneous, unsequenced, self-directed navigation of meaning — no scaffolding, no order of operations, no way to know what you have and haven't already made sense of. Even total physical access leaves that interface standing. The festival that celebrates things which resisted indexing has built a way of encountering them that also resists being indexed by the visitor. The sprawl is not incidental to the content; it rhymes with it.

And here the review does something that looks, briefly, like it agrees. "If contemporary artists and institutions want to tell hidden stories, I think they have a duty — to viewers and to their subjects — to tell them clearly." This is the working reading arriving from the aesthetic direction: clarity framed as an obligation owed in two directions at once, to the person looking and to the person looked at. It is the closest the source comes to naming an access ethic.

But it will not fold cleanly into the complaint, and it should not be forced to. The reviewer means clarity *within a show* — Jamie Fitzpatrick's grinning, collapsing plinth-men, legible in their satire — not navigability *across the festival*. And the shows he most loves refuse exactly the clarity he calls a duty. Anne Hardy's ritual is "mind-meltingly indecipherable," "way too knowingly obtuse," and it "works well." Eva Rothschild's rubber hoses and rotting Memphis aesthetics: "I love it, but I'm not sure how." The taste runs toward the gloopy, the unresolved, the thing you cannot get figured out. The duty to be clear coexists, unbothered, with an appetite for the obtuse.

That is not hypocrisy to be caught out. It is the actual condition of the encounter, and it is where the piece has to sit rather than resolve. Clarity of *meaning* and clarity of *access* are being treated as one word doing two jobs, and the review never notices the seam. It asks its subjects be told clearly while savouring the ones it cannot decode — because for the reviewer, the body that walks in and the mind that gives up on figuring it out are both simply *his*, available, mobile, free to let a show wash over him without needing a route through it. The "vibe" is enough. The failure to make sense is a pleasure he can afford.

The live question was whether the sprawl is an access failure that pre-sorts who discovers, or the one honest thing — a structure that at least doesn't pretend the underground can be tidily shelved. The material won't let it be one or the other. It is both, and the both is the point. The refusal to be legible is faithful to Sandra George and Frank Walter, who were never legible on the terms that would have found them in time. And that same refusal is an interface, and interfaces sort people. A festival can be structurally honest to its subjects and still quietly decide, in the shape of a sentence, who gets to be Columbo.

The review ends "we can all go home happy. I did." The word carrying the weight is *all* — offered as though everyone who could arrive at the door arrived through the same unheralded, self-directed drift, and left the same way. The unindexed dead resisted the systems that would have found them. The living visitor is simply handed a city and told to sniff it out. Only one of those is a freedom.
