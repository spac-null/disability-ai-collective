# Follow Your Nose: The Uneasy Pleasures of Edinburgh Art Festival

You can't walk into a gallery at the Edinburgh art festival without learning about some subcultural genius or long-lost creative you've never heard of. That's the whole point of the thing, really — a city-wide celebration of the underground and the unheralded. Queer narratives, secret histories, undiscovered archives. By the time you're done you'll have uncovered so many hidden stories you'll feel like Columbo.

But EAF is also a big, unwieldy, confusing thing. Curated displays, satellite exhibitions, performances, off-site projects — it's impossible to figure out how any of it fits together. You've just got to follow your nose and hope you sniff out something you like. And you'd better like photography, because there's a lot of it here.

The whole thing starts with US photographer Del LaGrace Volcano, whose images of queer nightlife in 1980s London are intimate, confrontational, erotic, totally defiant — as are the shots of lesbian goths draping themselves over tombstones in Edinburgh graveyards. Not all the photos are good, but this feels like an artist who should be gathering far more attention.

And then, at City Art Gallery, you find Sandra George. Here the festival's language of discovery — sniffing out, uncovering, stumbling upon — stops being a game. George (1957-2013) was a black photographer and photojournalist who documented life on the margins of inner-city Edinburgh. From her perspective as a semi-outsider, she captured the impoverished Craigmillar area: protests run out of living rooms and town halls, people in hostels and housing schemes, skinheads, labourers, Hare Krishnas, Indians, Jamaicans, black and white. She appears throughout her own show in self-portraits, often with her young son. Kids play and laugh everywhere, frequently in desperately bleak places.

Like a lot of people in EAF, she never showed her work publicly in her lifetime. She was only discovered after her death in 2013.

That sentence does something to the festival's cheerful verbs. The visitor who follows her nose and stumbles across George is stumbling across someone who never got to present herself. The pleasure of uncovering was only made available by the fact that the maker never got to. This work deserves to be seen far outside the city's confines — but its arrival here is inseparable from an absence.

The festival keeps repeating the pattern. At Ingleby Gallery, a group show centres on Frank Walter (1926-2009), an Antiguan painter, writer, poet and polymath who died having never shown any of the thousands of paintings and drawings he amassed over his lifetime. His work is small, subtle, strange — rainbows and stars and smiling faces on ripped cardboard and fading paper. Hung beside giants of contemporary painting, a big Rose Wylie, a gorgeous Lynette Yiadom-Boakye, it eclipses all of them. At Custom Lane, the Japanese illustrator Sadao Hasegawa (1945-1999) offers an eye-popping display fusing myth and hardcore eroticism — work that, you guessed it, stayed unpublished and unseen in his lifetime.

You guessed it. The reviewer's own shrug is telling. The festival has made the posthumous discovery into a house style, a rhythm you learn to anticipate. And the source never tells us why any of these artists went unseen while alive — only that they did. The unknowing is part of what's on offer.

Which raises the real question about the wandering. "Follow your nose" is a phrase for a browser with time and appetite, free to like or not like, to drift on. It works beautifully when EAF trades in hard-edged contemporary art: Eva Rothschild's rubber-hose sculptures at Fruitmarket, like Memphis Group aesthetics gone goth and left to rot; Anne Hardy's dystopian ritual at Talbot Rice, mind-meltingly indecipherable but immersive. You can shrug at Jill Edelstein's polished portraits or squint at Emii Alrai's shonky monument and move along. These works can withstand a browser's whim.

But George, Walter and Hasegawa cannot advocate for themselves, and never could. They are relying entirely on how they're presented. And here the festival's charm curdles slightly into something worthy and lecture-y — as if you're being told off in every gallery. *You've never heard of this marginalised artist? You bastard.* The best answer to that is Jamie Fitzpatrick at Edinburgh Printmakers, whose gloopy, satirical clay statues loom over you with total clarity. If contemporary artists and institutions want to tell hidden stories, they have a duty — to viewers and to their subjects — to tell them clearly.

That's the tension the language of stumbling can't quite hold. "I love it," the reviewer says of Rothschild's upstairs room, "but I'm not sure how the art downstairs fails to work so well." The same uncertainty haunts the whole festival. Maybe not everything has to make sense; maybe a city can just be filled with interesting, diverse art and loads of undiscovered narratives, and we can all go home happy.

He did. But the dead artists whose discovery made that possible had no such choice, and the festival's breezy verbs never quite reckon with the difference.
