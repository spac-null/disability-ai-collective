# Follow Your Nose

The instruction the festival gives you is the instruction of a bloodhound. You follow your nose. You wander into a gallery and hope you sniff out something you like. This is not stated as a limitation. It is stated as the fun of it, the whole premise, the reason Edinburgh Art Festival scatters itself across the city in curated displays and satellite exhibitions and off-site projects that cannot be made to add up. By the time you're done you'll have uncovered so many hidden stories you'll feel like Columbo — and that feeling, the detective's feeling, the feeling of having personally cracked a case nobody else knew was open, is the thing being sold. You are the one who does the uncovering. Your feet are the method.

So consider what your feet are asked to do. You start, perhaps, at the City Art Gallery, standing in front of the self-portraits of Sandra George (1957-2013), a black photographer who documented the impoverished Craigmillar area of Edinburgh from what the wall text calls her position as a semi-outsider. She never showed the work in her lifetime. She was discovered only after her death. In her images the communities she moved through come forward: protests run out of living rooms and town halls, people in hostels and housing schemes, skinheads, labourers, Hare Krishnas, Indians, Jamaicans, white and black, and children everywhere, playing and laughing in desperately bleak places. She appears throughout, often with her young son. It is genuinely moving work, and it deserves to be seen far beyond the city. Note the shape of what makes it good: a person who could move through the margins, camera in hand, and see.

Then you go up Calton Hill. You have to, because that's where Richard Maguire is showing at Collective Gallery, at the top, and the top of a hill is the top of a hill. Then across to Fruitmarket for Eva Rothschild's rubber hoses coiled like giant liquorice. Then into the vaulted Georgian rooms of Talbot Rice, where Anne Hardy has laid breeze blocks across the floor and made it rain indoors. Then to Edinburgh Printmakers, where a figure in a Georgian wig grins menacingly at you as you enter, a red plasticine smile plastered across his face, on the verge of collapsing into a messy pile — Jamie Fitzpatrick's brilliantly gloopy revenge on the statues of war criminals and slavers who loom over British cities from their plinths. Then, eventually, out among the horses and goats of Jupiter Artland, a sculpture park that is not in Edinburgh at all but a drive beyond it, where a young artist has covered shields and drums and birds in sequins and pink fur.

You are, by now, tired. This is the honest thing the review keeps admitting between its own lines. It's all a bit worthy and lecture-y, it says, like you're getting told off in every gallery you enter. *You've never heard of this previously undiscovered marginalised artist? You bastard!* Anyone who has been dragged from venue to venue at a festival like this one knows the exact texture of that exhaustion: the low-grade guilt of your own ignorance, administered gallery by gallery, plinth by plinth, until the pleasure and the fatigue become the same sensation. The show wants more ambition, some bigger names, and at the same time it is built entirely to celebrate the people no name ever attached itself to. That's the live contradiction. The whole enterprise scolds you for not knowing the very artists whose entire condition was going unknown.

But there is a quieter contradiction folded inside that loud one, and it lives in the word *walk*. You can't walk into a gallery without learning about some subcultural genius you've never heard of. The sentence is a compliment to the festival's generosity. It is also, if you slow it down, a description of a very particular body — one that walks into galleries, plural, freely, up the hill and back down, across the city and out past it to the goats. The discovery premise is not neutral. It is ambulatory. It presumes a visitor who can follow their nose, which is to say a visitor who can go where the nose leads without first checking whether the door, the stairs, the hill, the distance, the drive will let them.

This is worth sitting with precisely because the review never sits with it, and neither, probably, does the festival. The whole thing is designed as an act of spontaneous physical wandering, and spontaneous physical wandering is the least distributed capacity in the room. The festival celebrates the marginalised — the queer nightlife photographed by Del LaGrace Volcano, the erotic illustrations of Sadao Hasegawa (1945-1999) that stayed unpublished and unseen in his lifetime, the thousands of small strange paintings Frank Walter (1926-2009) made and never showed, the entire underground and unheralded canon of people who were not seen. And it hands the seeing of them to whoever can most easily move through a dispersed, unmapped, off-site sprawl. The uncovering it prizes is filtered, before anyone even arrives, through the question of who gets to do the uncovering.

Think again about Sandra George, then, because she is the exact figure the festival most admires and she is also its unspoken model of the ideal visitor. Her gift was mobility of a specific kind: she could move through Craigmillar as a semi-outsider and document what she found. The festival asks the same of you. Move through, semi-outsider, document, discover, go home feeling like Columbo. The review ends with the writer going home happy, and there is no reason to doubt it — the happiness is real, the art is often good, the mess is part of the charm. Maybe not everything has to make sense, it concludes; maybe a city can just be filled with fun, diverse, hidden-story art and we can all go home.

*We.* The pronoun does a lot of quiet work. It assumes a body that could get to the stories in the first place — up the hill, across town, out to the horses — and could therefore be tired by them, guilted by them, delighted by them, and carried back home. The stories the festival rescues from obscurity are stories about who gets seen and who doesn't. The way it stages that rescue quietly decides who gets to do the seeing. Follow your nose, it says, warmly. It's a lovely method. It just isn't a method available to every nose in the city.
