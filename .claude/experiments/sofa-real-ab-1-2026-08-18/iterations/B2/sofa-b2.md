# Follow Your Nose: On Who Gets to Uncover the Unheralded

The review of the Edinburgh art festival opens with a promise dressed as a boast: "By the time you're done with Edinburgh art festival you'll have uncovered so many hidden stories you'll feel like Columbo." The verb is *uncovered*. The festival, as described, is not a thing you look at so much as a thing you dig through, sniff out, stumble into. "You can't walk into a gallery," the reviewer writes, "without learning about some subcultural genius or longlost creative you've never heard of." Walk into. The discovery is coded, from the first paragraph, as an act of the legs.

And the reviewer is honest that this is the only method on offer. There is no map that works. "There are curated displays, satellite exhibitions, performances, off-site projects, and it's impossible to figure out how any of it fits together. You've just got to follow your nose and hope you sniff out something you like." That sentence describes a festival, but it also describes a body: one that can follow, that can range across the city from City Art Gallery to Fruitmarket to Talbot Rice to the top of Calton Hill to the horses and goats of Jupiter Artland, gathering the unheralded as it goes. The reward structure of the whole event is built for a spontaneous, mobile spectator. The person who can't range freely across venues doesn't just miss shows; they miss the festival's entire premise, which is that the good stuff is scattered and you have to move to find it. The unheralded artists get their due only through a spectator who can do the uncovering — and that spectator has already been quietly pre-selected by an architecture of walking.

It would be easy to turn this into a simple complaint: make it navigable, publish a proper route, and the access problem dissolves. The reviewer half-wants this. He calls the festival "a big, unwieldy, confusing thing," says it's "too big and complex, with too many moving parts." The instinct is for a cleaner interface.

But the material resists that tidy fix, and it resists it from inside the festival's own logic. Consider Sandra George (1957–2013), the Black photographer whose show at City Art Gallery the reviewer rates as one of the strongest things here — work about Craigmillar that "deserves to be seen far outside the city's confines." The line that matters is quiet: "Like a lot of people in EAF, she never showed her work publicly in her lifetime, and was only discovered after her death in 2013." Frank Walter (1926–2009) is the same — an Antiguan polymath who "died having never shown any of the thousands of paintings and drawings he amassed." So is Sadao Hasegawa, whose work "stayed unpublished and unseen in his lifetime."

This is the festival's whole thesis, stated as biography: the best work is precisely the work that was never legibly catalogued, never shown, never made findable in life. These artists are unheralded because they resisted, or were shut out of, the systems that make an artist easy to find. Which means the festival's refusal to fit together — its unwieldiness, its lack of a clean map — may be structurally faithful to its subjects rather than merely careless. A festival whose material is the never-catalogued cannot itself become a clean catalogue without betraying what it holds. The demand for a navigable interface starts to look like a demand that argues against the very archive-diving that surfaced Sandra George at all.

So the access reading gets more precise, not larger. The problem was never that the festival is messy. The mess is the point; the mess is where the unheralded live. The problem is who the mess assumes when it says *follow your nose* — because following your nose is not a neutral instruction. It presumes the legs.

The reviewer, without knowing it, hands over the exact principle the situation needs. "If contemporary artists and institutions want to tell hidden stories," he writes, "I think they have a duty — to viewers and to their subjects — to tell them clearly." That is an access argument arriving from the aesthetic side: clarity owed not only to the audience but to the dead, the people whose lives were made illegible once already. It is worth noticing what he does *not* mean by it, though. He means clarity of meaning *inside a single show* — Jamie Fitzpatrick's grinning war criminals in Georgian wigs, telling their story plainly. He does not mean navigability across the city. And in the same breath he confesses a taste for the obtuse: he "loves" Eva Rothschild's rubber-hose goth Memphis upstairs, admires Anne Hardy's "mind-meltingly indecipherable" dystopian ritual.

That is the tension worth sitting inside rather than resolving. A duty to be clear can coexist with a love of the indecipherable, because the two clarities are different clarities. One is about what a work means. The other is about who can reach the work at all. The festival, for all its celebration of the overlooked, has only worked out the first. The unheralded are still waiting on the second — and so is whoever can't follow their nose across a whole city.
