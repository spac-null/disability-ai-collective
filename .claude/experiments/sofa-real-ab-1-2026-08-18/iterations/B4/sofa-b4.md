# Follow Your Nose

The Edinburgh Art Festival tells you, more or less, how to use it. Follow your nose. Sniff out something you like. There is no map that will help — curated displays, satellite exhibitions, performances, off-site projects, and "it's impossible to figure out how any of it fits together." So the instruction is really about the visitor's own equipment: your capacity to wander, to notice, to trust that something worthwhile will announce itself before you've located the whole.

That's a lot to ask of a person's attention. It means holding no expectation of sequence, letting each gallery arrive as its own surprise, and carrying the accumulated weight of everything you've already seen from venue to venue. The festival's promise is that you'll "uncover so many hidden stories you'll feel like Columbo." The detective flatters the visitor — but a detective works cases in order, follows one thread to the next. Here there is no order. You are the detective and the maze both.

What actually rewards that wandering attention is worth naming. Del LaGrace Volcano's photographs of queer nightlife in 1980s London — intimate, confrontational, defiant, lesbian goths draped over tombstones in Edinburgh graveyards — do the thing the festival keeps promising: they open a door onto genuinely underground life. Not all the pictures are good. But you sniff out the ones that are.

And then the account starts to complicate itself. The festival's whole method is discovery, and the more you look, the more you notice how much of what's being discovered was never meant to be found. Sandra George (1957–2013), a black photographer who documented Edinburgh's impoverished Craigmillar — protests run out of living rooms, kids playing in bleak places, hostels and housing schemes — "never showed her work publicly in her lifetime, and was only discovered after her death in 2013." Frank Walter (1926–2009), the Antiguan polymath, "died having never shown any of the thousands of paintings and drawings he amassed over his lifetime." Sadao Hasegawa (1945–1999), whose erotic illustrations fuse Japanese mythology and hardcore porn, "stayed unpublished and unseen in his lifetime."

The source does not say why. It says only that it happened — three times, across three continents and half a century. The festival's job, then, is not merely to arrange objects for a wandering eye. It's to speak for people who did not speak for themselves in public. And the visitor's sniffing-out becomes something heavier than taste: you are being handed responsibility for stories their makers never released.

This is where "follow your nose" starts to strain. Because a good deal of what the festival puts in front of you is designed to resist being followed. Anne Hardy's dystopian ritual at Talbot Rice — breeze blocks, flickering lights, the sound of rain, hooded bronze figures — comes with a handout about "vibrations, energy, AI, weather systems in Texas." The verdict: "mind-meltingly indecipherable." Richard Maguire's strong ideas about race and homosexuality across south Asian and British histories arrive as "wispy, barely contextualised lumps and scrawls." Eva Rothschild's Fruitmarket takeover is better upstairs than down, and even the reviewer can't say why the downstairs fails: "I love it, but I'm not sure how the art downstairs fails to work so well."

Notice what that last admission does to the festival's own instructions. If following your nose sometimes lands you somewhere you can't explain even after you've responded to it, then the method has a limit. You can sense; you cannot always account for what you sensed. And when the subject is a hidden story — someone's undisclosed life — sensing isn't enough. Here the piece states the condition outright: "If contemporary artists and institutions want to tell hidden stories, I think they have a duty — to viewers and to their subjects — to tell them clearly."

That is the answer the festival's own account keeps circling. It asks the visitor to wander, to trust instinct, to accept incoherence as the price of abundance. And most of the time, for pleasure, that's fine. But it has stacked its program with the unseen dead, and the unseen dead cannot be served by vibes. Jamie Fitzpatrick's grinning Georgian statues at Edinburgh Printmakers — war criminals and slavers rendered as gloopy, collapsing Muppets in clay — work because they tell you exactly what they mean. Clarity is not the enemy of strangeness; Frank Walter's small weird paintings on ripped cardboard eclipse the giants hung beside them precisely because you can see them.

So what does the festival require of someone who takes it at its word? A willingness to get lost, yes. But underneath that, a harder demand it doesn't quite admit it's making: to become the keeper of stories whose owners are gone, using only a nose the festival itself has told you to trust — and then, half the time, given nothing clear to smell.

The festival is too big to make sense. Maybe not everything has to. But some things do.
