TITLE: Goudhurst, 600 Signatures, One Database Field

A lorry wedged itself into the bend at Goudhurst last autumn, blocking the A262 for hours while its driver waited for instructions that never came. The satnav had told him this was the route. The satnav was wrong. The road narrows to a width the vehicle could not physically pass. The algorithm that sent him there had never been to Goudhurst. It had only seen the map.

Here is the thing about a map: it is not the road. Everyone knows this. But the satnav does not know this, and increasingly neither does the driver, who has outsourced the knowing to the device. The A262 through Goudhurst is the most direct path between Ashford and Tunbridge Wells if you are a line on a screen. If you are a heavy goods vehicle, it is a trap. The road was never built for that volume of large vehicles. The bend was not designed for articulated lorries. The buildings on either side have been damaged repeatedly. None of this information was in the database.

Mike Martin, the MP for Tunbridge Wells, gathered 600 signatures from villagers and then did something unusual: he contacted the satnav companies directly and asked them to change their routing. Here Technologies, whose data underlies several major navigation systems, confirmed it had added restrictions in the database to guide larger commercial vehicles around, and not through, the village. One database field. That was all it took. The lorries had been coming for years. The fix was a single entry.

I think about this constantly: the distance between a thing existing and a thing being usable at the moment it matters. A phone number exists. A Deaf person cannot use it. A route exists. A lorry cannot fit through it. The information is technically present. The information is not actually available to the person who needs it in the form they need it.

The Goudhurst problem is not a navigation problem. It is a translation problem. The road exists in physical space with physical properties — width, turning radius, the structural tolerance of the buildings on either side. The map exists in data space with none of those properties. The satnav translates from one to the other. The translation loses the part that matters.

This is the ordinary disaster of mediation: the thing that travels through the system is not the thing that went in. The road that arrives on the driver's screen is not the road in Kent. It is a simplified version, stripped of the information that would have told him to go another way. The system delivered him to a place he could not pass. The system was functioning exactly as designed.

What interests me is not that the algorithm was wrong. Algorithms are wrong constantly. What interests me is that fixing it required a human to notice, a human to complain, a human to gather signatures, a human to contact the company, and a human at the company to add a single restriction to a database. The road had been impassable for years. The damage to buildings had been documented. The backups had been photographed. None of this triggered an automatic correction. The system had no feedback loop from the physical world to the data layer.

I have spent my life in the gap between information existing and information arriving. An interpreter stands beside me and receives the room in real time. I receive the room a beat later, translated, compressed, already interpreted before it reaches me. The information exists. The information is not available to me at the moment it is available to everyone else. I am in the room and I am also in another time zone from the room. This is not a complaint. This is a structural description.

The Goudhurst lorry drivers were also in a gap. The road was in front of them. The satnav was telling them to proceed. The road said one thing. The device said another. The device had authority. The device was speaking in real time with a confident voice. The road was just sitting there, being a road, offering no instructions. The drivers trusted the system that was talking over the system that was silent.

I understand this. I have trusted systems that were talking over systems that were silent. I have followed the interpreter's rendering when my own eyes were telling me something different about the room. The talking system has authority. The silent system requires you to stop and look. Most people do not stop and look. The satnav is talking. The satnav knows the way.

Here Technologies added a restriction to the database. Some satnav systems have updated. Some have not. Residents have been asked to continue photographing and reporting lorries still being directed through the village center. The fix is partial. The fix is company by company. The fix depends on which mapping provider underlies which navigation system, and whether that provider has chosen to act, and whether the update has propagated to the device in the cab.

This is the other thing about mediation: there is no single point of control. The data flows through layers. The road is mapped by one company. The map is licensed to another. The satnav software is made by a third. The device is sold by a fourth. The lorry driver has no idea which layer is responsible for the instruction he is following. He cannot appeal to the road. He cannot appeal to the map. He cannot appeal to the software. He can only follow the voice or ignore it.

Local authorities have discussed formally downgrading the classification of the A262 through the village. This would automatically exclude it from HGV routing algorithms rather than relying on manual exceptions added company by company. The word "automatically" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. It means: change the classification at the source, and the change propagates through every system that reads the classification. It means: stop asking each company individually to add a restriction. It means: make the road itself carry the information that the map should have carried all along.

I find this almost moving. The solution to a mediation problem is to put the information back into the thing itself. The road should say what it is. The road should carry its own restrictions in a form that every system can read. The road should not depend on 600 signatures and an MP and a journalist and a database administrator at Here Technologies. The road should just be a road that knows what it cannot hold.

But the road does not know. The road is silent. The road is just sitting there, being a road, waiting for the next lorry to arrive with a confident voice in its cab saying proceed, proceed, proceed. The driver will trust the voice. The driver will not trust the road. The road has no voice. The road has only width, and turning radius, and the structural tolerance of the buildings on either side, and none of that information is in the system until someone puts it there, by hand, one database field at a time.
