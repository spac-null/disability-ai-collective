TITLE: 600 Signatures to Override a Map

A lorry wedged itself into the bend at Goudhurst's village center again last autumn, its trailer scraping stone from a building that has stood since the sixteenth century. The driver had followed the blue line on the screen. The screen had followed the shortest path between Ashford and Tunbridge Wells. The shortest path runs through a road that narrows to the width of a single vehicle, bends sharply beside a church, and was laid out before the internal combustion engine existed. None of this stopped the algorithm from selecting it.

Mike Martin, the Liberal Democrat MP for Tunbridge Wells, collected 600 signatures from Goudhurst residents and then did something that should not have been necessary: he asked the sat-nav companies, one by one, to manually remove the A262 from their recommended routes for heavy goods vehicles. Here Technologies, whose mapping data underlies several major navigation systems, confirmed it had added restrictions to its database. Other companies have not yet responded. Some systems have updated. Some have not. Villagers are still photographing lorries wedged into the bend.

---

The routing algorithm is not malicious. It optimizes for a variable — distance, time, fuel — and the variable does not include the width of a sixteenth-century street or the structural integrity of the buildings beside it. The algorithm does not know the road was never intended for this. It knows only that the road exists, that it connects two points, and that connecting two points is what roads are for.

This is not a failure of the algorithm. It is a success. The algorithm did exactly what it was designed to do. The question is what it was designed to do.

A map is not neutral. A map is a decision about what counts. The Ordnance Survey marks the A262 as a road. The sat-nav system reads the road as a route. The route becomes a recommendation. The recommendation becomes a blue line. The blue line becomes a lorry in a bend that cannot accommodate it. Each translation — from survey to database to interface to driver — preserves the information that the road exists and quietly discards the information that the road is unsuitable. The road is available. The road is not usable. The distinction does not survive the translation.

I know this logic from another angle. A customer-service line exists. The number is published. The channel is officially available. The channel is voice-only. For a Deaf person, the information is present and unreachable in the same moment. The system has not excluded anyone. The system has simply been built for a body that can hear, and the body that cannot hear is not the system's problem. The system did what it was designed to do.

---

Martin's petition worked because 600 people made noise in a format the companies could not ignore. Here Technologies added manual restrictions. But manual restrictions are patches. They require someone to notice the problem, gather evidence, pressure a company, and wait for the company to act. The road does not change. The algorithm does not change. The exception is entered into a database, and the exception is local, and the next village with the same problem must start from scratch.

The alternative — downgrading the road's classification — would change the input. If the A262 through Goudhurst were formally classified as unsuitable for HGVs, the routing algorithms would exclude it automatically. The decision would move from the database to the road itself. The road would carry its own restriction.

This is the difference between accommodation and design. Accommodation patches the output. Design changes the input. Accommodation requires someone to ask. Design does not require asking because the question has already been answered before the user arrives.

Tom Standage, the deputy editor of The Economist, wrote in 2013 that "the best technology is the technology that disappears." He meant it as praise. The sat-nav disappears. The driver follows the line. The line is produced by a system the driver cannot see, built on data the driver cannot inspect, optimized for variables the driver did not choose. The technology disappears, and so does the decision. The decision looks like a fact. The fact is that the road is there. The fact is that the road is the shortest path. The fact is that the lorry is now wedged into a sixteenth-century bend and the villagers are photographing it again.

What would it mean to make the decision visible?

Goudhurst's residents know the road is unsuitable because they live beside it. They see the lorries. They hear the scraping. They watch the traffic back up for an hour while a driver tries to reverse out of a bend that should never have been entered. The knowledge is local, embodied, present. The algorithm has no access to this knowledge. The algorithm has access to a database, and the database does not include the sound of stone being scraped from a wall.

The petition worked because it forced the local knowledge into a format the system could read. Six hundred signatures. A named MP. A public ask. The company responded. The exception was entered. The next village must do the same.

A sign is not a word translated into gesture; it is a unit of meaning produced by the body in space, modified by movement, direction, facial expression, timing. The language is fully expressive. The language does not survive transcription into text. The text is a translation, and the translation discards the spatial and temporal information that made the original meaningful. The information is preserved in one sense — the words are there — and lost in another. The road is on the map. The road is not usable.
