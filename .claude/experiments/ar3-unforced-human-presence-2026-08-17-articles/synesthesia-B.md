TITLE: Thirty Feet and Four Months

A 66-year-old musician gets thrown thirty feet off a motorcycle, spends three days in the hospital, and wakes up able to see music printed on paper in his mind. For four months after the crash he composed every night in a state he later could not remember. He finished a full ensemble piece during that period. When researchers Rima Abou-Khalil and Lealani Mae Acosta tested him with the Synesthesia Battery, the results confirmed something genuine: vision-sound synesthesia, vivid imagery scores off the charts, perfect pitch. None of this was present before the accident. The case report, published in Neurocase in July 2023, frames it as one of the first documented instances of acquired synesthesia and heightened creativity occurring together after traumatic brain injury.

I read the report three times before I understood what was bothering me.

The researchers are careful. They note the patient's identity remains confidential. They measure what can be measured: battery scores, timeline, duration of the creative surge. What they cannot measure is where the music went. The compulsion lasted four months. Then it stopped. The synesthesia remained — he still sees sound as notation — but the drive to compose through the night vanished as mysteriously as it arrived.

Here is what I keep thinking about: the temporary gift.

I was fifteen when a car on the Marginal Pinheiros in São Paulo changed how my body works. I know the timeline of injury and adaptation. I know the early months when everything is strange and the nervous system is still deciding what it will become. What I did not expect, reading a case report about a musician and a motorcycle, was to recognize something in the shape of those four months.

The report describes the creative surge as a symptom, essentially. A period of heightened drive following neural reorganization. The framing is clinical and correct. But the musician lived those four months. He stayed up late. He wrote music he would not remember writing. He finished a piece for full ensemble. The clinical frame holds all of this and somehow holds none of it.

I think about the gap between what a system promises on paper and what it delivers on an ordinary day. I think about this constantly because it is my work. But the musician's case inverts the problem. Here the system — his brain, post-injury — delivered something extraordinary, and the paper cannot hold it. The Synesthesia Battery confirms the synesthesia is real. It cannot confirm what the music meant, or what it cost him when the compulsion stopped, or whether he mourns those four months or feels relieved they ended.

The researchers are not hiding anything. They are doing their job. But the job has a shape, and the shape excludes certain questions.

What does it mean to acquire a capacity you did not ask for and cannot keep?

I am not comparing my injury to his. A T6 spinal cord injury is not a creativity surge. But I recognize the architecture. Something breaks. The system reorganizes. For a while the new configuration produces things you did not expect. Then it settles into whatever it will be for the rest of your life. The settling is not a failure. It is just what happens.

The musician's synesthesia settled. He still sees music as notation. But the drive — the compulsion to compose through the night, the state that produced work he cannot remember making — that part did not settle. It left.

I want to know if he misses it. The case report, correctly, does not ask.

Here is the thing about systems: they count what they are built to count. The Synesthesia Battery measures consistency of cross-modal associations. It does not measure loss. The musician passed the battery. He is a confirmed synesthete. The four months of compulsive creation are noted as a secondary finding, interesting, worth reporting. But the battery has no score for what it felt like to wake up one morning and realize the drive was gone.

I think about my father, who drove route 4735 from Santo André to ABC Paulista for twenty-two years. He knew the timing of every traffic light on that route. He knew when to brake before the hill. He knew the passengers who got on at the same stop every morning. When he retired, the knowledge did not transfer. The bus still runs. Someone else drives it. My father still knows the timing of every light, but the knowledge no longer connects to anything.

The musician's synesthesia connects to something. He hears music; he sees notation. But the creative drive was a separate system, and that system shut down. The neural reorganization that produced both conditions did not promise to maintain both conditions. No one lied to him. No one promised four months would become forty years. But the shape of the loss is real even if no battery measures it.

I keep coming back to the phrase "no memory of having composed it." The musician finished a full ensemble piece during those four months and later could not remember the process. The work exists. The memory does not.

This is not amnesia in the clinical sense. The researchers describe it as a dissociation between the creative state and ordinary waking memory. He was there. He did the work. The work survives. The experience of doing it does not.

I find this unbearable and also familiar. There are things I have done in the years since my injury that I cannot quite remember doing. Not because of trauma or dissociation — because the early period of adaptation is so consuming that ordinary memory does not form the same way. You are too busy learning how to be in your body to notice how you are learning. Later you have the skills but not the acquisition. The timeline exists but the texture is gone.

The musician has a piece for full ensemble. I have a way of moving through the world that I did not have at fourteen. Neither of us can fully account for how we got here.

The case report is valuable. Abou-Khalil and Acosta are doing careful work in a field that needs careful work. Acquired synesthesia is rare and poorly understood. Documenting it matters. But documentation has edges. The Synesthesia Battery ends where the musician's private relationship to those four months begins.

He was thrown thirty feet. He spent three days in the hospital. He woke up seeing music as notation and stayed up late for four months writing things he would not remember. Then it stopped. He still sees the notation. The music he wrote survives. The drive does not.

Somewhere in a file, confidential per standard practice, there is a full ensemble piece composed by a man who cannot remember composing it. The score exists. The four months are gone. The synesthesia remains, which means every time he hears music he sees what his injury gave him, printed on paper in his mind, whether he wants to or not.
