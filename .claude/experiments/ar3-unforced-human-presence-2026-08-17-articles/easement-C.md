TITLE: Ten Feet Wide, Permanently Blocked

A fence does not move. This seems obvious enough that writing it down feels absurd. But the Maine Supreme Judicial Court needed to say it on April 21, 2026, in a ruling against a couple who had built a fence inside a ten-foot easement their neighbors use to reach the Webhannet River. The lower court had found that only parked cars — not the fence itself — obstructed the path. The higher court disagreed. A fixed structure can block passage whether or not someone is actively standing behind it at the moment you try to walk through.

I have been thinking about this ruling for a week. Not because I care about kayaks or Maine real estate disputes or the Lytles' access to their river. Because the lower court's logic is a pattern I recognize from other systems entirely.

The distinction matters. A car parked in an easement is a temporary act. Someone chose to park there; someone can choose to move it. The obstruction has a face, a decision-maker, a moment of occurrence. A fence is different. A fence is a rule made physical. Once it exists, no one has to choose anything. The blocking happens continuously, automatically, without a human present to feel bad about it.

---

In November 2024, a York County judge looked at the Linds' fence and concluded it did not interfere with access. The cars parked in the driveway did. This is a strange finding if you think about it for more than a few seconds. The easement is ten feet wide. The fence and driveway exist within those ten feet. The neighbors need to carry kayaks and paddleboards down to the water. The path is narrowed. The passage is harder. But the court saw only the cars — the active, temporary, removable obstruction — as the problem.

I suspect I know why. A parked car is legible as interference because it involves a choice someone made today. You can point to the moment. You can ask the person to move it. The fence is harder to see as interference because it has always been there, at least since the Lytles started complaining. It is part of the landscape. It does not feel like an act; it feels like a condition.

This is the pattern I keep seeing. The thing that blocks you once, visibly, with a face attached, gets named as the problem. The thing that blocks you continuously, invisibly, as infrastructure, gets called normal.

---

I spent three years working on the Crossrail consultation in London. One of my jobs was to review how station designs handled pedestrian flow under various capacity scenarios. The models were elegant. They assumed a standard pedestrian: 1.5 feet of shoulder width, a walking speed of 4.5 feet per second, a turning radius that fit neatly into the calculations. The standard pedestrian did not use a wheelchair, a cane, a walker, or a guide dog. The standard pedestrian did not travel with a child in a pushchair or pull a suitcase or carry a folding bicycle. The standard pedestrian was an abstraction that matched roughly 70% of actual users and excluded the rest by design.

No one chose to exclude wheelchair users from the pedestrian flow model on any particular day. The exclusion was built into the parameters. It ran automatically, generating station designs that worked beautifully for the standard pedestrian and created pinch points, dead ends, and impossible routes for everyone else. When I flagged this, the response was polite and procedural. The parameters were industry standard. Changing them would require recalculating every scenario. The timeline did not allow it.

The parameters were the fence. The timeline was the car. Everyone could see the timeline as a constraint. No one could see the parameters as an obstruction.

---

The Maine ruling does something unusual. It says a fixed structure can constitute interference independent of any active use. The fence itself — not the fence plus a parked car, not the fence plus someone standing there refusing to let you pass — violates the easement. The obstruction is the thing, not the behavior around the thing.

This is harder than it sounds. Our legal systems, our administrative systems, our design systems are built to identify acts. Someone did something. Someone failed to do something. The verb has a subject. But a fence has no subject after the moment of installation. A parameter has no subject after the moment of adoption. A policy has no subject after the moment of approval. The obstruction continues, but the act is over.

I have watched this logic play out in eligibility systems, in building codes, in transport planning, in benefits assessments. The rule was written once. It runs forever. It excludes the same people year after year, and no one is making a decision to exclude them. The exclusion is automated. The fence is permanent. The cars come and go, and everyone argues about the cars.

---

What the Maine Supreme Judicial Court did was refuse the distinction. The Linds argued that the fence was compatible with the easement because the neighbors could still technically get through. The court said no. A recorded right of passage is not satisfied by the theoretical possibility of squeezing past an obstruction. The easement guarantees a ten-foot width. The fence narrows it. The narrowing is the interference, whether or not anyone is actively making it worse at any given moment.

I find this almost moving. Not because it changes anything about how most systems work. One easement ruling in Maine does not rewrite the logic of infrastructure design or benefits administration or urban planning. But because it names the thing I have been trying to name for years. The obstruction that has no face is still an obstruction. The interference that has no moment is still interference. The rule that runs without a human present is still a choice someone made.

The Lytles sued in October 2023. The lower court ruled in November 2024. The appeal was decided in April 2026. Two and a half years to establish that a fence blocks a path. Two and a half years to get a court to see the fixed structure as the problem, not just the cars parked in front of it.

---

I keep thinking about what it would mean to apply this logic elsewhere. To look at a building code and ask: does this parameter obstruct passage, independent of any particular act? To look at an assessment form and ask: does this eligibility criterion block access, independent of any particular decision? To look at a standard and ask: does this definition exclude, independent of any particular application?

The fence is still standing in Wells, Maine. The ruling says it must be removed or modified. The Linds will comply or appeal further or do nothing and face contempt. The neighbors will carry their kayaks down to the river or they will not. The ten-foot easement will be ten feet wide or it will not.

A fence does not move. That is the point.
