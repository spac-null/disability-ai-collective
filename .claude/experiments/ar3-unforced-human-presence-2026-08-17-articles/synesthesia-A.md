TITLE: Thirty Feet and Four Months

A man gets thrown thirty feet off a motorcycle and wakes up able to see music printed on paper inside his head. The crash happened sometime before July 2023, when Rima Abou-Khalil and Lealani Mae Acosta published their case report in *Neurocase*. The patient was sixty-six, a musician, hospitalized for three days. When he listened to music afterward, the sound arrived with an involuntary visual score—notes he had never learned to see, appearing without his permission.

I have read dozens of stories like this one. Brain injury, strange gift, heightened creativity, the implication that something was unlocked. The researchers confirmed the synesthesia was genuine using the online Synesthesia Battery. They measured his visual imagery vividness and found it exceptionally high. They confirmed perfect pitch. For four months after the accident, he composed music late into the night, every night, driven by something he could not name. He wrote a full ensemble piece during that period and later had no memory of having written it.

Here is what stopped me: the four months ended.

---

The heightened creativity, the compulsion, the nightly composition—it faded. The case report does not say when or how. It notes only that the period was "sustained" and then, implicitly, that it was not sustained anymore. The synesthesia appears to have persisted; the drive did not. The man got a gift, and then the gift changed shape, became something quieter or smaller or just different. The researchers are careful to note this is one of the first published cases where acquired synesthesia and acquired heightened creativity occur together in the same patient following traumatic brain injury. What they do not say—what nobody ever says in these reports—is what it costs to have a capacity arrive uninvited and then watch part of it leave.

I know something about capacities that arrive without warning. Mine did not come with visions. It came with the sudden discovery of what my body could not do anymore, which is a different kind of involuntary knowledge. I was fifteen, on the Marginal Pinheiros in São Paulo—a highway that kills forty people a year. The city has been redesigning it for four decades. I was not driving. I do not remember the impact.

What I remember is the months afterward, when I learned that my T6 spinal cord injury was incomplete, which meant some sensation remained, which meant the neurologists could not tell me with certainty what I would or would not recover. For a while, everything was possibility. I might walk again. I might not. The uncertainty had a texture. It was like living inside a sentence that would not finish itself.

The musician in the case report composed an ensemble piece he could not remember writing. I spent a year relearning how to dress myself. These are not the same experience, but they share a structure: the body does something the conscious mind did not plan, and afterward you have to figure out who you are now.

---

The case report calls the four-month period "heightened creativity." The word "heightened" does a lot of work there. It implies that creativity is a volume knob, that more is better, that the injury turned something up. But the man composed music he could not remember composing. He worked late into the night, every night, under a compulsion. That is not a gift. That is a seizure dressed as productivity.

I have a friend—a painter in Brooklyn, wheelchair user since a fall in 2011—who told me once that the worst part of her injury was the six months when everyone kept asking if she had discovered any new talents. "Like the fall was supposed to give me something," she said. "Like I owed the universe a compensating superpower." She said this in her studio on Atlantic Avenue, surrounded by canvases that looked exactly like the canvases she had painted before the fall. She had not gained synesthesia. She had gained chronic pain and a new relationship with doorframes.

The researchers note that the patient's identity and further biographical detail remain confidential per standard case-report practice. This is appropriate. The man did not ask to become a case study. But the absence of detail means the report cannot tell us what he thinks about what happened. Does he miss the four months of compulsion? Does he want them back? Is the synesthesia a gift he would choose to keep, or just something that happened to him, like the crash itself?

I have no way to ask him. But I notice that the report treats the synesthesia and the creativity as findings, not as events with emotional weight. The Synesthesia Battery confirmed the vision-sound connection. The imagery vividness score was high. The period of heightened creativity was four months. These are measurements. They do not tell you whether the man is happy or bewildered or mourning something he cannot name.

---

Here is what I actually want to say: the injury-as-gift narrative is a trap, and the case report walks right into it without noticing. The researchers are careful and precise. They confirm their measurements. They note that this is one of the first published cases of its kind. But the frame they are working inside—brain injury produces unusual capacity, unusual capacity is noteworthy, noteworthy capacity gets published—is a frame that cannot see the injury as anything other than a mechanism for producing interesting outcomes.

The man was thrown thirty feet. He was hospitalized for three days. He now sees music in a way he did not choose and cannot turn off. He composed something he cannot remember composing. The creativity faded; the synesthesia did not. What remains is a perceptual experience that arrived without consent and will not leave.

I am not saying the synesthesia is bad. I have no idea whether it is bad. Maybe the man loves it. Maybe he finds it useful. Maybe it makes his music richer or his life stranger in ways that feel like gain. But the case report cannot ask those questions, because the case report is not interested in what the experience means to the person having it. The case report is interested in what the experience tells us about brain plasticity and the neural correlates of creativity.

The gap between those two interests is the gap I keep coming back to. It is the same gap I find in every system that processes bodies: the distance between what the system measures and what the person inside the measurement actually lives.

A man gets thrown thirty feet and wakes up seeing music. The researchers confirm the synesthesia is genuine. The four months end. Somewhere, the man is still listening to songs and watching notes appear unbidden, a score he never learned to read printing itself across the inside of his skull.
