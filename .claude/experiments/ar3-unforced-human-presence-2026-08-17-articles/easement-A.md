TITLE: Ten Feet Wide and Nowhere to Stand

The Lytles carry kayaks. That is the fact at the center of the case — not abstract passage, not hypothetical access, but the specific act of carrying a kayak down a ten-foot-wide easement to the Webhannet River in Wells, Maine. The Linds installed a fence within the boundaries of that easement. Then they poured a driveway. On April 21, 2026, the Maine Supreme Judicial Court ruled that the fence alone constituted illegal interference, regardless of whether a car happened to be parked there at any given moment.

The lower court had said otherwise. In November 2024, a judge found that the fence did not block access — only the parked cars did. The reasoning ran something like this: you can still walk through, so the right is preserved. The higher court disagreed. A recorded easement guarantees a use, not merely the theoretical possibility of passing through if nothing else gets in the way.

I have been reading property-law decisions for years, not because I practice law but because they are the clearest record of how systems decide what counts as obstruction. The pattern is consistent. Courts tend to rule that an interference exists only when someone is actively being blocked at the moment of complaint. A barrier that does not touch your body is not a barrier. A gate you could theoretically open is not closed. A fence you could theoretically climb is not an obstacle.

---

The Lytles did not climb. They argued that the fence made it difficult or impossible to carry kayaks, paddleboards, and other equipment down to the water. That phrase — "difficult or impossible" — is doing a lot of work. It names a category of obstruction that does not wait for total blockage. It says: the degradation of access is the harm, not only its elimination.

Gregory Bateson wrote in the 1970s that information is "a difference which makes a difference." I think about that when I read these rulings. The lower court saw no difference: the Lytles could still reach the river, so the easement was intact. The higher court saw a difference that made a difference: the quality of passage had changed, and the change was not incidental.

The Linds, presumably, did not wake up one morning and decide to obstruct their neighbors. They installed a fence. They poured a driveway. Both are normal things to do on your own property. But the easement was recorded. The ten-foot-wide strip was not theirs to fill, even if the deed said they owned the land underneath it. The right of way was an obligation that predated their ownership and would outlast it.

This is where the case gets interesting. The Linds' lawyer argued that the fence itself did not interfere — only the cars. The logic is familiar: if the obstacle can be removed, it is not structural. If the structural thing can be walked around, it is not an obstacle. The court rejected this. A fixed structure within the boundaries of an easement is an interference whether or not someone is also actively using it to block passage at any given moment.

---

I have spent years trying to explain why this distinction matters, and I keep failing in a specific way. The failure looks like this: someone nods, agrees that the principle is correct, and then asks why I care so much about a fence in Maine. The answer is that the fence is not the point. The point is the structure of the reasoning.

Systems that define obstruction as active blockage — someone standing in your way, a car parked in your path, a door locked at the moment you try to open it — systematically undercount obstruction. They count only the incidents. They do not count the fixed thing that makes the incidents possible.

The lower court's ruling would have required the Lytles to prove, each time, that a car was parked in the easement when they wanted to use it. The higher court's ruling says: the fence is the problem. The fence is always there. The fence changes the nature of the passage even when no car is present.

This is not a metaphor. It is a measurement problem. If you count only the moments of active blockage, you will conclude that obstruction is rare and episodic. If you count the fixed conditions that make active blockage possible, you will conclude that obstruction is structural and continuous. Both counts are technically accurate. Only one of them captures what it is like to carry a kayak down a ten-foot-wide path that now has a fence in it.

Justice Andrew Horton wrote the majority opinion for the Maine Supreme Judicial Court. The ruling is brief and technical, but it contains a sentence I keep returning to: "The grant of an easement includes the right to have it unobstructed by structures that impede its use." Not unobstructed by active interference. Unobstructed by structures.

I grew up in Vällingby, a suburb of Stockholm built in the 1950s as one of the first transit-oriented developments. The T-bana station was placed before the houses were built. The whole neighborhood was designed around the assumption that people would need to move through it, not just live in it. My father worked for the Swedish railways. He used to say that a timetable is a promise, and a broken promise is worse than no promise at all.

The Lytles had a promise. A recorded easement is a promise written into the land itself. The Linds' fence did not erase the promise; it made the promise harder to keep. The lower court said: the promise is still there, you can still reach the river. The higher court said: the promise was for passage, and passage has been degraded.

I find myself thinking about this when I try to explain why certain kinds of friction matter. The friction is not the fence. The friction is having to argue, each time, that the fence is a problem. The friction is the assumption that if you can still get through, nothing is wrong. The friction is the labor of proving, over and over, that a fixed structure is not neutral just because it does not actively block you at this exact moment.

The Lytles sued in October 2023. The lower court ruled in November 2024. The higher court ruled in April 2026. Two and a half years to establish that a fence inside an easement is an obstruction. The legal system worked, eventually. But the fence was there the whole time.
