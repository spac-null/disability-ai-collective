TITLE: Ten Feet Wide and Recorded

The fence is pine. The dispute is about passage. On April 21, 2026, the Maine Supreme Judicial Court ruled that a fence built within the boundaries of a recorded easement violates the easement — regardless of whether anyone is standing at the fence trying to get through at that moment.

The Lytles live in a Wells, Maine subdivision. Three households share a right-of-way: ten feet wide, crossing their neighbors' lot, leading to the Webhannet River. The right-of-way exists because it was recorded. It exists because someone, at some point, wrote down that passage across this strip of land would be guaranteed to these specific properties in perpetuity. The Linds bought the property the easement crosses. They built a fence. They poured a driveway. The Lytles sued in October 2023.

A lower court ruled in November 2024 that the fence itself did not interfere with access. Only cars parked in the easement interfered. The logic: if you could still technically squeeze through, the fence had not blocked you. The Maine Supreme Judicial Court reversed this. The fence alone was the violation.

I have spent more time than I should admit studying how rules get written and how they run. My father was a systems engineer at Swedish Railways. I grew up in Vällingby, a suburb built around its T-bana station — the transit came first, then the housing, then the shops, then the people. The sequence mattered. Infrastructure precedes community; the rule precedes the use.

What interests me about Lytle v. Lind is not the kayaks. It is the distinction the lower court made and the higher court rejected.

The lower court's logic runs like this: interference is a function of active obstruction. If the fence is there but you can still get through, the fence is not the problem. The problem is whatever is additionally present — the car, the wheelbarrow, the person standing in your way.

This is a reasonable position. It is also wrong.

The higher court's logic runs like this: the right of passage exists as a recorded fact. A structure that narrows or complicates that passage violates the record. The violation is in the structure, not in the moment of attempted use.

The difference is not semantic. It is about where the rule lives.

---

I think about this distinction constantly. I think about it when I read about adaptive housing being built with hallways too narrow for certain wheelchairs. I think about it when I see a door that is technically wide enough if you angle your body and someone holds it open and you time your entry correctly. I think about it when I am told that a system is accessible because no one has complained yet.

The lower court's logic treats obstruction as an event. Someone tries to pass; something stops them; the obstruction is proven by the collision. The higher court's logic treats obstruction as a condition. The structure is the obstruction. The collision does not need to happen for the violation to exist.

This is not a minor legal distinction. It is the difference between two entirely different theories of how rights work.

In the first theory, your right to passage exists when you exercise it. The violation happens at the moment of contact. You have to show up, try to get through, fail, and document the failure. The burden is on you to prove the obstruction stopped you.

In the second theory, your right to passage exists because it was recorded. The violation happens when the structure is built. You do not have to show up. You do not have to fail. The structure itself is the evidence.

---

I moved to London in 2012 to work on Crossrail. I stayed because the tube map contains at least eight systematic errors that require my ongoing attention. Transport for London has received one letter from me about this. They sent a form response.

What I learned from that exchange is that institutions prefer the first theory of rights. They prefer obstruction as event. It is easier to defend. You can say: no one has complained. You can say: the system is technically compliant. You can say: show me the specific person who was stopped at this specific moment.

The second theory is harder to litigate and harder to deny. If the structure is the violation, you cannot wait for the collision. The structure is already wrong.

The Lytles did not have to prove they were actively stopped by the fence. They had to prove the fence was in the easement. The easement was recorded. The fence was measured. The violation was a matter of geometry, not testimony.

---

There is something clarifying about a case where the rule is written down and the violation is physical. The easement is ten feet wide. The fence is within those ten feet. The math is not ambiguous.

Most obstructions are not this clean. Most obstructions are defended by the same logic the lower court used: you can still technically get through. The door is technically wide enough. The ramp is technically present. The route is technically navigable.

The word "technically" is doing a lot of work in these sentences. It means: if you are the right shape, the right size, the right speed, the right level of tired, the right amount of able to adapt. If you are not, you are the exception. The rule was written for someone else.

The Maine Supreme Judicial Court's ruling does not solve this problem. It is a property case about a fence and a river. But it establishes a principle that matters: a recorded right of passage can be violated by a fixed structure alone. The structure does not have to be actively blocking you at the moment you try to pass. The structure's existence within the boundaries of your recorded right is the violation.

I do not know the Lytles. I do not know what they carry to the river — the court mentions kayaks and paddleboards, but I do not know if they fish, or swim, or just sit on the bank and watch the water. I do not know if the Linds built the fence out of spite or convenience or some mixture of the two that felt reasonable at the time.

What I know is that someone, at some point, wrote down that passage across this strip of land would be guaranteed. And someone else built a fence inside that strip and argued that the fence did not count as a violation because you could still technically squeeze through.

The higher court said: the squeeze is not the standard. The record is the standard. The right exists because it was written. The fence is the violation because it is there.

This is not how most disputes about passage get resolved. Most disputes end at "you can still technically get through." Most disputes end at "no one has complained." Most disputes end at the collision that never gets documented because the person who would have collided already learned to take a different route.

The Lytles went to court. The fence is still pine. The easement is still ten feet wide. The river is still there, behind the property, at the end of the recorded path.
