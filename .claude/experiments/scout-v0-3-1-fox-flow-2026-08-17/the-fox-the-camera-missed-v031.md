---
title: "The Fox the Camera Missed"
byline: Pixel Nova

In June 2013, four ecologists set fifty-six cameras across half a hectare of pine forest in North Carolina. They spaced them ten metres apart, in a tight grid, and left them running for a month. Roland Kays, Allison Hody, David Jachowski and Arielle Parsons wanted to know something that sounds almost too basic to be a research question: when an animal walks past a camera trap, does the camera actually see it?

A deer walking through the grid got caught, clearly, 86 percent of the time. A gray fox: 29 percent.

At first, it seems simple: a camera either catches an animal or it doesn't. You might expect the misses to be random — a bad angle, a gust of wind, bad luck. But look at which animals got missed, and the pattern stops looking like luck.

The camera doesn't watch the forest continuously. It waits. A heat sensor in the lens decides when to wake the shutter up, triggered by a change in warmth passing in front of it. A big, slow animal throws a big, lingering wall of heat. A small, fast one flickers past and is gone before the shutter catches up.

Line the four animals up by weight, lightest to heaviest, and they land in the exact order the camera trusted them: gray fox, raccoon, coyote, deer. The camera fired for the fox 42 percent of the time it should have. For the raccoon, 61. The coyote, 72. The deer, 91. The misses weren't random. The camera missed some animals far more often than others, and the animals it missed most were the small, fast ones.

Sign-language interpretation makes this same difference easy to see. An interpreter first has to understand what was said, then translate it — which means a Deaf audience member gets the information a half-beat late. A joke can be translated perfectly and still arrive after the room has already started laughing. Nothing was missing. It just arrived late. That's the distinction the fox needs, too: not recorded is not the same thing as not there.

The paper's authors say something close to this themselves, in a line easy to skim past: "This problem would be worse with rare species, which are often the target of faunal surveys." Camera traps are one of the standard tools ecologists use to figure out how common or rare an animal actually is in a given patch of forest. If the camera's ability to catch an animal depends on that animal's size and speed, then a "rarity" score built from camera counts isn't purely a measurement of the animal. Part of it is a measurement of the camera.

Ecologists have a name for this general worry — "imperfect detection," coined back in 2002 — and a whole statistical toolkit for handling it. But that toolkit doesn't automatically know to check for this specific pattern. A 2023 follow-up study had to build an entirely new set of experiments just to measure, camera by camera and species by species, how much a moving animal's size affects whether it gets caught at all. Twenty years of vocabulary for "we might be missing things," and researchers are still going out into the field to find out exactly what, and how much.

Underwater, the camera disappears. There's no lens, no shutter, no frame to miss. Marine biologists trying to count species from sound alone use hydrophones instead — underwater microphones that can only pick up an animal once its call is loud enough to rise above the noise of the ocean itself. That's a hard physical cutoff, and it turns out to sort animals by almost the same quality that sorted the fox from the deer: how big, and how loud.

A 2020 review in the Royal Society's open-access journal doesn't hedge about what this does to the data. Acoustic surveys, it says, are "biased towards more gregarious, larger, louder or easily detected species leading towards erroneous richness measurements." A shy, quiet animal barely shows up in the recordings, not because it isn't there, but because it never got loud enough to count.

Two instruments, then: one built to watch, one built to listen. Both quietly prefer the animal that's easiest for them, specifically, to hold onto. Both hand back a "diversity" number that is, in part, a description of the equipment.

None of this means the forest changed. A gray fox moving through that half-hectare of loblolly pine at three in the morning left the same mark on the world whether or not a camera happened to catch it. The fox doesn't need the camera's permission to have been there. What varies, frame to frame, is something smaller and stranger: whether an animal's own body was big enough, and slow enough, to leave a heat signature the shutter could catch before it had already moved on.

Somewhere on that grid, in the summer of 2013, a fox almost certainly crossed within ten metres of a camera and left nothing behind. No photo, or a blank one, filed the same as if nothing had happened there at all. The fox crossed the forest. The camera can leave a record that looks exactly like it didn't.
