---
title: "The Category That Outlived Reality"
byline: Zen Circuit
draft_status: scout-v0.2.4-shadow-only
---

On July 29, 2025, a Mississippi environmental official confirmed two labels for a set of gas turbines: mobile, and temporary. The turbines were mounted on flatbed trailers, and xAI, the company running them to power a data center in Southaven, said each one would be gone within a year. On that basis, Jaricus Whitlock, who runs the air division at the state's Department of Environmental Quality, accepted the classification and confirmed it in writing to an xAI vice president named Brent Mayo.

The letter captured one moment. What happened afterward was harder to fit inside those two words.

A few days after Whitlock signed it, three turbines sat on the property. By the end of October, aerial photographs showed eighteen, with thermal imaging confirming that at least nine of them were already running. By December, the count was twenty-seven. As the number grew, Mississippi's environmental agency told xAI it didn't need to notify them each time more turbines showed up. So the total could keep changing without ever forcing anyone to revisit the classification it was supposed to fit inside.

Then, on February 13, 2026, the NAACP, the Southern Environmental Law Center, and Earthjustice sent xAI formal notice that they intended to sue over the Southaven turbines. The growth did not stop. By that spring there were thirty-three. By July, according to later reporting, sixty-nine.

The letter dealt with the turbines one by one. It did not ask what dozens of them, still arriving, might become.

By then, the argument in Southaven was no longer just a local one. It had reached the EPA. In January 2026, the federal agency finalized a new rule for gas turbines, and coverage at the time described it as closing the loophole that let temporary turbines run indefinitely. What the rule actually did was leave the old mobile-turbine exemption in place and build a new category next to it: a "stationary temporary combustion turbine," permitted to run for up to twenty-four months under its own, lighter set of emissions rules.

And in Southaven, temporary kept getting longer. The turbines classified in the summer of 2025 were originally supposed to be gone by December 31, 2026. In July 2026, the state's environmental agency issued a retirement schedule instead of an ending. Thirteen of the turbines were granted permission to keep running past that deadline. Three of those thirteen were granted more time again, on top of that — enough, by the agency's own accounting, to keep them running until roughly July 2027.

The turbines remained on their trailers. In that narrow sense, "mobile" still fit. "Temporary" had become harder to read so simply. What began with a promise of less than a year now included deadlines reaching into 2027.
