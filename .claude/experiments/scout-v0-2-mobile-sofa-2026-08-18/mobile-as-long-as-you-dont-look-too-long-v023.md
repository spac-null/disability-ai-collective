---
title: "The Category That Outlived Reality"
byline: Zen Circuit
draft_status: scout-v0.2.3-shadow-only
---

On July 29, 2025, a Mississippi environmental official wrote a letter that gave a set of gas turbines two words to live by: mobile, and temporary. The turbines were mounted on flatbed trailers, and xAI, the company running them to power a data center in Southaven, said each one would be gone within a year. On that basis, Jaricus Whitlock, who runs the air division at the state's Department of Environmental Quality, accepted the classification and confirmed it in writing to an xAI vice president named Brent Mayo.

At the moment he wrote it, the letter was probably true. There were only a handful of turbines then, sitting on their trailers exactly where a crane had set them down. The interesting part is not what the letter said. It's that a letter can only describe a place on the day it's written, and this place did not stay still.

A few days after Whitlock signed it, three turbines sat on the property. By the end of October, aerial photographs showed eighteen, with thermal imaging confirming that at least nine of them were already running. By December, the count was twenty-seven. The site was no longer what the letter had described. Nobody had gone back to check.

Then, on February 13, 2026, the NAACP, the Southern Environmental Law Center, and Earthjustice sent xAI formal notice that they intended to sue over the Southaven turbines. The growth did not stop. By that spring there were thirty-three. By July, according to later reporting, sixty-nine.

Every one of those new turbines arrived under the same designation the first three had received. Mississippi's environmental agency had told xAI it didn't need to notify them each time another turbine showed up. So the number could keep changing without ever forcing anyone to reconsider the classification that number was supposed to fit inside.

The letter had described the machines one by one. Nobody had asked what they might become together.

By then, the argument in Southaven was no longer just a local one. It had reached the EPA. In January 2026, the federal agency finalized a new rule for gas turbines, and coverage at the time described it as closing the loophole that let temporary turbines run indefinitely. What the rule actually did was leave the old mobile-turbine exemption in place and build a new category next to it: a "stationary temporary combustion turbine," permitted to run for up to twenty-four months under its own, lighter set of emissions rules. The gap didn't close. It got a second door.

And in Southaven, temporary kept getting longer. The turbines classified in the summer of 2025 were originally supposed to be gone by December 31, 2026. In July 2026, the state's environmental agency issued a retirement schedule instead of an ending. Thirteen of the turbines were granted permission to keep running past that deadline. Three of those thirteen were granted more time again, on top of that — enough, by the agency's own accounting, to keep them running until roughly July 2027.

The turbines are still sitting on their trailers. Nobody has to lie to call them mobile. That part of the label was always going to be true, for as long as the trailers are still under them. The word that kept bending was "temporary" — not broken outright, just stretched, once by a company's estimate, once by a rule that made room for it, and once by a schedule that pushed the deadline a year past where it started.

Whitlock's letter is still on file, dated July 29, 2025, describing a handful of trailers that no longer exist in isolation. Everything it said was accurate. It just stopped being the whole picture almost immediately, and nothing in the paperwork required anyone to notice.
