---
title: "The Category That Outlived Reality"
byline: Zen Circuit
draft_status: scout-v0.2.2-shadow-only
---

Some labels describe what something looks like right now. Others depend on what is supposed to happen later. A machine can be called mobile because of how it's built — wheels, a trailer underneath it. It can be called temporary because of something someone said about the future — that it won't be here long.

In Southaven, Mississippi, just south of Memphis, a set of gas turbines received both labels. They were "mobile" because each one sat on a flatbed trailer. They were "temporary" because xAI, the company that installed them to power a data center, said each one would stay for less than a year. The strange question comes later: who checks whether those words are still true, once the place itself keeps changing?

On July 29, 2025, Jaricus Whitlock, chief of the air division at the Mississippi Department of Environmental Quality, wrote to Brent Mayo, a vice president at xAI. His letter confirmed the classification. Trailer underneath it: mobile. Less than twelve months, by the company's own stated intention: temporary. Two conditions, both satisfied on paper, and nothing in the letter asked how many turbines there would eventually be, or how long the site as a whole might actually run.

In early August 2025, three turbines sat on the property. By the end of October, aerial photographs showed eighteen, with thermal imaging confirming at least nine of them already running. By December, the count was twenty-seven. On February 13, 2026, the NAACP, the Southern Environmental Law Center, and Earthjustice sent xAI formal notice that they intended to sue over the Southaven turbines. The number kept climbing anyway: to thirty-three by that spring, and, according to later reporting, sixty-nine by July.

Each new turbine arrived under the same mobile-and-temporary designation the first ones had. According to one legal filing, Mississippi's environmental agency told xAI it didn't even need to notify them each time more turbines showed up. Nobody was required to look at the total and ask whether the words written for a handful of trailers in August still described whatever was standing on that land by the following summer.

The federal government eventually changed the rules, but not by simply ending the exemption. In January 2026, the Environmental Protection Agency finalized a new rule for stationary gas turbines. Reports at the time described it as closing the loophole. What the rule actually does is keep the mobile-turbine exemption in place, and add a new category next to it — a "stationary temporary combustion turbine," allowed to run for up to twenty-four months under a separate, lighter set of emissions rules. The old exemption didn't disappear. A new one was built alongside it.

Something similar happened once with trucks. A "glider kit" is a brand-new truck built around a much older, remanufactured engine. Because the engine itself wasn't newly made, some manufacturers argued the truck wasn't "new" either, under a law that defined newness by the engine rather than by the vehicle rolling off the line. Production of these trucks rose from a few hundred a year in 2004 to roughly ten thousand a year by 2015, until the EPA closed the gap in 2016. A different administration tried to reopen it the following year. The attempt never fully succeeded, but only because the market for these trucks collapsed under later rules — not because anyone decided the original argument was wrong.

Back in Southaven, the turbines that were classified as temporary in the summer of 2025 were originally supposed to be gone by December 31, 2026. In July 2026, Mississippi's environmental agency issued a retirement schedule instead. Thirteen of the turbines were granted permission to keep running past that date. Three of those were granted still more time on top of that — enough, by the agency's own accounting, to keep them running to roughly July 2027.

The turbines are still sitting on their trailers. That much of the label has never stopped being true. The harder word was always "temporary." Months passed. More turbines arrived. The deadline moved. The paperwork had a name for each machine, checked once, on the day it showed up. The place they were building kept changing underneath it.
