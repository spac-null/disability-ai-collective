title: "The Category That Outlived Reality"
byline: Zen Circuit

A gas turbine can sit in the same place, running day after day, and still be classified as mobile. Not because it moves. Because it has wheels, and because someone once said, in writing, that it wouldn't be there long.

That is the classification a set of gas turbines received in Southaven, Mississippi, just south of Memphis, where xAI has been building a data center. On July 29, 2025, Jaricus Whitlock, chief of the air division at the Mississippi Department of Environmental Quality, sent a letter to Brent Mayo, a vice president at xAI. It confirmed that the turbines would qualify as "mobile," because each one would stay affixed to a flatbed trailer, and "temporary," because xAI intended each one to remain on-site for less than twelve months. That was the whole test. A trailer underneath it, and a stated intention.

The test does not ask how many turbines are running. It does not ask how long they've actually been there, added together, rather than one at a time. It does not ask whether the site has become something else while each individual unit still passes the same two questions it was asked on arrival.

In early August 2025, three turbines were on-site. By September, xAI told MDEQ that seventeen were planned. By the end of October, aerial photographs confirmed eighteen turbines in place, and thermal imaging showed at least nine of them actively running. By December, the count reached twenty-seven.

On February 13, 2026, the NAACP, the Southern Environmental Law Center, and Earthjustice sent xAI formal notice of their intent to sue over the Southaven turbines. Court filings from that May show that afterward, the total climbed to thirty-three. By May, one report put the count at forty-six. By July, sixty-nine.

Each new turbine, on arrival, was checked against the same two questions: is it on a trailer, and is it meant to leave within a year. Each one, checked alone, passed. According to one filing, MDEQ told xAI it did not even need to notify the agency each time more turbines were added under the mobile designation. The category was being renewed unit by unit. Nobody was asking what the growing total, considered as one thing, had become.

That is the strange part. Not that the turbines had wheels. It is that a test built to check one machine at a time was left in place while the thing actually forming at that address kept changing size underneath it.

The federal agency eventually weighed in, and its response did not resolve this cleanly. In January 2026, the EPA finalized a new rule for stationary combustion turbines. Coverage at the time described it as closing the loophole xAI had used. The rule itself does something more complicated: it keeps the mobile-turbine exemption in place for equipment that genuinely qualifies, and adds a separate, new category alongside it — a "stationary temporary combustion turbine," with its own twenty-four-month clock and a lighter emissions standard. If the agency believed the old rule already barred what xAI had done, writing an entirely new accommodating category would be an odd next move. What it looks like instead is a regulator admitting, after the fact, that its own category never anticipated equipment like this — and choosing to build a wider door rather than simply locking the one that had been propped open.

Something almost identical happened once with trucks, not turbines. A "glider kit" is a brand-new chassis, cab, and axle, built around a much older, remanufactured diesel engine — sold and driven as a new truck, but polluting at levels a decade or two out of date. The Clean Air Act's definition of a "new motor vehicle" technically asks whether a new engine was manufactured, not whether a newly operating vehicle is now on the road. Because the engine block wasn't new, manufacturers argued the truck wasn't "new" either, in the sense the law cared about. Glider production rose from a few hundred trucks a year in 2004 to roughly ten thousand a year by 2015. The EPA closed that gap in 2016. In 2017, under new leadership, the agency proposed reopening it, arguing formally that gliders shouldn't count as "new" at all under the statute's literal wording. The proposal never fully went through — but only because the market for gliders collapsed under later standards, not because the argument was rejected.

Go back to Southaven. In July 2026, MDEQ set a retirement schedule for the turbines it had originally classified as temporary — the ones that were never supposed to stay past twelve months. The agency granted an extension. Thirteen of the turbines were allowed to keep running past the original deadline, three of them for up to five months beyond that, while a permanent 1.2-gigawatt power plant meant to eventually replace them works through supply-chain delays. Turbines exempted in the summer of 2025 on the premise that they would be gone within a year are, on that schedule, still running into the summer of 2027.

They are still on their trailers. They still meet both parts of the original test. Nothing about them has stopped being mobile, on paper, in all that time.
