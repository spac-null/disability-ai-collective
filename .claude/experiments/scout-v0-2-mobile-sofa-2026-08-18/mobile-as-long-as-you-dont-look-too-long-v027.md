title: "The Category That Outlived Reality"
byline: Zen Circuit

Three gas turbines on trailers are easy to call temporary. Sixty-nine are harder.

In August 2025, xAI had three gas turbines running at its data center site in Southaven, Mississippi, just south of Memphis. By the following July, there were sixty-nine. Mississippi's environmental agency had a test for deciding whether a turbine counted as mobile and temporary, and by every account it kept applying that same test as the machines arrived. What the test never asked was what sixty-nine of them, sitting together on the same stretch of land, actually were.

The test came from a letter. On July 29, 2025, Jaricus Whitlock, who runs the air division at Mississippi's Department of Environmental Quality, wrote to an xAI vice president named Brent Mayo, confirming that the turbines qualified as mobile because each one sat on a flatbed trailer, and temporary because xAI said each would be gone within twelve months. Both conditions were about a single machine. Neither one asked how many machines there would eventually be.

A few days after Whitlock signed the letter, three turbines sat on the property. By the end of October, aerial photographs showed eighteen, with thermal imaging confirming that at least nine of them were already running. By December, the count was twenty-seven. As the number grew, Mississippi's environmental agency told xAI it didn't need to notify them each time more turbines showed up. The classification had been built for a handful of trailers, and nothing about it required the state to look again once that handful became dozens.

Then, on February 13, 2026, the NAACP, the Southern Environmental Law Center, and Earthjustice sent xAI formal notice that they intended to sue over the Southaven turbines. The growth did not stop. By that spring there were thirty-three. By July, sixty-nine.

The letter had dealt with the turbines one by one. It did not ask what dozens of them, still arriving, might become.

The same gap had reached federal regulators. In January 2026, the EPA finalized a new rule for gas turbines nationwide, and coverage at the time described it as closing the loophole. It didn't. The rule left the mobile-turbine exemption in place and added a new category beside it — a "stationary temporary combustion turbine," allowed to run for up to twenty-four months. The exemption that let individual machines pass as mobile and temporary was still there. It was the accumulation the rules never addressed.

In Southaven, the turbines classified in the summer of 2025 were originally supposed to be gone by December 31, 2026. In July 2026, the state issued a retirement schedule instead of an ending. Thirteen turbines were granted permission to keep running past that deadline. Three of those thirteen were granted more time again — enough, by the agency's own accounting, to keep them running until roughly July 2027.

Sixty-nine turbines, each one still sitting on its own trailer, each one still answerable, one at a time, to the same two words written for three machines in the summer of 2025.
