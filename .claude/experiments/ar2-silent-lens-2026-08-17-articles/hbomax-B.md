TITLE: What the Algorithm Refuses to Say

Warner Bros. Discovery announced on February 26, 2026, that HBO Max's password-sharing crackdown will expand to international markets this year. JB Perrette, the company's streaming chief, declined to say which countries, when, or how the system decides who belongs to a household. The mechanism is secret by design.

I have spent the last week trying to map what that secrecy actually looks like from the inside. Not because I care about HBO Max specifically — I don't subscribe — but because the structure is familiar. A rule runs. It sorts people. The criteria are hidden. The sorted have no way to know why they were sorted. I know this shape. I have been on the wrong side of it before.

---

Netflix pioneered the model in 2023. The company gained roughly nine million subscribers in the initial wave after enforcement began, according to industry reports. Then the surge plateaued. The crackdown produces one spike in new sign-ups, not sustained growth. Warner Bros. Discovery also announced it will stop reporting HBO Max subscriber numbers going forward, mirroring Netflix's earlier decision. This means the actual effect of the crackdown on subscriptions will be difficult for outside analysts to verify.

So the rule runs, the numbers disappear, and the only people who know what happened are the ones who designed the rule.

I assumed, when I started looking, that the enforcement mechanism would be technical — IP addresses, device fingerprints, location pings. It is. But the technical signals are not the interesting part. The interesting part is what the company has declined to define: what counts as a "household."

Under the current US system, HBO Max identifies which accounts are "legitimate" members of a household after months of internal testing. A subscriber can add an out-of-household account for an extra $7.99 a month. The company has not published the specific technical signals it uses to distinguish a shared household from a shared password.

I called a friend in Los Angeles who works in disability policy. She has a client — I'll call him Marcus — who lives in a group home with three other adults. They share a common room, a kitchen, a single internet connection. They are, by any ordinary definition, a household. But Marcus's name is not on the lease. His address is technically the address of a nonprofit organization. His device fingerprint shows him logging in from the same IP as three other people who are not related to him and not named on his account.

"He got locked out in September," she told me. "The system flagged him. He couldn't figure out how to appeal. The form asked for proof of address, but his mail goes to the nonprofit."

Marcus paid the $7.99 extra-member fee. He did not know what else to do.

---

The word "household" appears in eligibility rules across welfare systems, housing policy, and now streaming subscriptions. It is never defined the same way twice. In British council housing allocation, a household is the people who will live together. In US food stamp eligibility, a household is the people who purchase and prepare food together. In HBO Max's enforcement system, a household is whatever the algorithm decides it is, and the algorithm does not explain.

I thought I was looking at a streaming policy. I was looking at a diagnostic category.

Gregory Bateson wrote that mind is located in the pattern of relationships, not the individual. I found that sentence at twenty, in a library in Stockholm, and it was the first thing I had read that explained my own experience of knowing. The pattern is the unit of analysis, not the node. But diagnostic systems — medical, administrative, algorithmic — work the other way. They isolate the node. They ask: does this person belong to the category? They do not ask: what relationships constitute the category in the first place?

HBO Max's system isolates the account. It asks: does this login belong to the household? It does not ask: what is a household, and who decided?

The answer, of course, is that someone decided. JB Perrette decided, or someone who reports to him. The criteria were chosen. The thresholds were set. But the setting happened once, in a room I was not in, and now the rule runs without a human present to feel bad about the edge cases it produces.

---

I want to be fair. Password-sharing costs the company money. The crackdown is a business decision, not a conspiracy. If I were running a streaming service and half my users were sharing passwords with people who could afford to pay, I would probably do something about it too.

But the fairness stops where the secrecy begins.

Netflix's extra-member slot costs $7.99 with ads, $9.99 without. HBO Max charges $7.99. These are not ruinous sums. The harm is not the fee. The harm is the opacity. The harm is that Marcus, in his group home in Los Angeles, has no way to know why the system flagged him, no way to prove that his living arrangement is legitimate, no way to appeal to a person who can look at his situation and make a judgment.

The rule runs. The rule does not blink.

I have been flagged by systems before. Not streaming systems — assessment systems, benefits systems, forms that ask for a single correct answer when the truth is more complicated. The experience is always the same. You know something is wrong. You do not know which input caused the error. You cannot trace the logic backward because the logic is hidden. You sit with the outcome and try to reverse-engineer the machine.

In my case, the algorithm did not converge. I could not find the moment the room changed. I could not find the input that made the system decide I did not belong.

---

Warner Bros. Discovery will expand the crackdown to international markets sometime this year. JB Perrette did not specify which markets, or when, or how the enforcement mechanism will adapt to different housing arrangements, different definitions of family, different patterns of who lives with whom.

In Sweden, where I grew up, multigenerational households are less common than in Southern Europe or South Asia. In the UK, where I live now, the average household size is 2.4 people. In India, it is 4.4. The algorithm will sort all of these households using criteria designed in Burbank, California, by people who did not think to ask what a household looks like in Lagos or Manila or a group home in Los Angeles.

The rule will run. The numbers will disappear. The people who are sorted will not know why.

This is not a streaming policy. This is a diagnostic category, automated and exported. The machine decides who belongs. The machine does not say how.
