TITLE: 1,700 Stores and One Second Behind

Kroger's cameras watch you hesitate over a plum. The system from Everseen, now installed in more than 1,700 of the chain's roughly 2,700 stores, uses overhead computer vision to detect when a shopper picks up an item without scanning it. When the algorithm flags you, a short replay of the footage appears on your checkout screen. You are meant to watch yourself, recognize your error, and correct it before a store associate is alerted. The company calls this "self-correction." Tom Arigi, Kroger's director of asset protection, says the video-replay step resolves roughly 80 percent of flagged incidents without staff intervention.

I read that figure three times. Eighty percent of people, shown footage of themselves, fix their own behavior before anyone has to speak to them.

---

The system assumes you can see the replay. It assumes you can recognize yourself in the footage, parse what the algorithm is accusing you of, and respond in real time. Alex Siskos, Everseen's vice president of strategy and growth initiatives, told Grocery Dive that "the goal is to give the customer the chance to self-correct first." The architecture of that chance is built for a particular body. A body that perceives visual footage without assistance. A body that processes the accusation and formulates a response in the same temporal window the machine allots. A body that does not need an interpreter.

I know what it is to be one beat behind a room. An interpreter cannot translate simultaneously. They must first understand a stretch of what is said, then choose a rendering. By the time the translation reaches me, the conversation has moved. I have laughed at jokes a beat late my whole life — not because I didn't understand, but because the understanding arrived through someone else's processing first. You learn to live inside that small displacement. You learn that information arriving is not the same as information arriving on time.

The self-checkout camera does not offer that beat. The replay appears, the clock runs, and if you do not respond within whatever threshold the system has set, a staff member is summoned. The machine has already decided what counts as a reasonable response window. That window was not designed with a Deaf shopper in mind. It was not designed with a blind shopper in mind. It was not designed with anyone who might need a few extra seconds to parse what they are being shown, or who might not be able to parse it at all.

---

A separate system, from the Tel Aviv startup KanduAI, uses deep-learning image recognition to identify produce at self-checkout scales. Instead of requiring you to search a manual code list for your fruit, the system shows you a short list of likely matches. The company says it has reduced produce-related checkout errors from 18 percent to under 4 percent in French and Israeli pilot stores. Ariel Shemesh, KanduAI's founder, says the system reaches roughly 92 percent accuracy on the first attempt.

Ninety-two percent. That means 8 percent of the time, the system is wrong on the first try. What happens to the shopper standing in front of a wrong list? The system has already made a judgment. The shopper must now argue with the machine's confidence.

A friend told me once about watching her Deaf mother try to use an automated phone tree. The number was printed on a form as the only way to resolve a billing error. The system existed. The information was technically available. Her mother could not reach it. The form did not list an alternative. The phone tree did not know it was excluding anyone. It was simply built for the body it was built for, and that body was not hers.

The Everseen system works the same way. It is not designed to exclude. It is designed for a shopper who can see, hear, respond in real time, and perform innocence on command. If you cannot do those things, the system does not refuse you. It simply does not know you exist.

---

Some retailers are now testing facial-recognition age verification at self-checkout. The system compares your face at the camera against an ID image already on file with the retailer's app. Lukas Kinigadner, CEO of the Austria-based vendor Anyline, calls this "a natural extension of computer-vision checkout generally." The face becomes the credential. The camera becomes the gatekeeper. The body is read before the person has said a word.

I think about the temporal structure of this. The camera captures your face. The algorithm compares it to a stored image. The system makes a judgment. All of this happens in fractions of a second. You are verified or you are flagged. The decision is made before you know it has been made. You are already categorized by the time you notice the screen.

This is not new. Categorization by algorithm happens constantly, invisibly, in systems we do not see. What is new is that the categorization is now happening in a physical space you are standing in, in real time, with a camera pointed at your face. The judgment is instantaneous. The appeal is not.

---

The industry justification for all of this is shrink. Retailers estimate annual US losses to theft at more than sixty billion dollars. A recent survey found that 67 percent of shoppers reported experiencing some kind of self-checkout system failure. More than a quarter admitted they had used a self-checkout error to avoid paying for an item at least once.

So the cameras are watching for theft. But the cameras are also watching for confusion. For hesitation. For the pause that happens when you are not sure which button to press, or when the machine has made an accusation you do not understand, or when the produce list is wrong and you do not know how to tell the system it is wrong.

The system cannot distinguish between theft and confusion. It can only detect deviation from the expected behavior of the expected body.

I made a work once called *De Gebarentaaltolk en Ik* — "The Sign Language Interpreter and I." A poem came to me in a dream, in NGT. I had an interpreter translate it into spoken Dutch. Then a second interpreter translated that spoken Dutch back into NGT. I showed all three versions simultaneously, as three synchronized videos. For the first time, I could watch my own language without the time-lag. The synchronization made visible what sequential viewing hides: the drift, the loss, the small shifts in meaning that happen every time something passes through another system.

The self-checkout camera shows you a replay of yourself. But the replay is not you. It is the version of you the algorithm captured, compressed, interpreted, and judged. The system has already decided what your hesitation means. You are watching the machine's reading of your body, not your body itself.

The replay is a translation. And like all translations, it arrives a beat late, carrying the interpreter's choices inside it.
