TITLE: Shuttle Buses Running Every Four Minutes

Somewhere between Terminal B and the P4 parking lot, a $3.5 billion transit project is being built on the assumption that the problem is the train.

The Port Authority announced in January that weekday rail service on Newark's AirTrain would be suspended from 5 a.m. to 3 p.m. to allow construction of a new guideway. Shuttle buses now run every four to five minutes during those hours. The replacement system, due around 2030, will add three stations and increase passenger capacity. The press materials mention modernized amenities. They do not mention what happens when you cannot board the shuttle bus.

I read the construction notice three times looking for the word "accessible." It appears nowhere. The phrase "budget extra time" appears twice.

---

My father drove route 4735 in Santo André for twenty-two years. I grew up knowing that a bus schedule is a promise the city makes about whose time matters. A bus that runs every four minutes sounds generous until you learn that the first bus was full, the second had a broken lift, and the third driver did not know how to deploy it. Then four minutes becomes twelve, becomes twenty, becomes the flight you missed.

The Port Authority's construction notice treats the shuttle buses as a simple substitution: rail out, bus in, same route, same function. This is the math of people who have never waited for a lift that might or might not work.

I have watched shuttle bus operations at three airports in the past two years. At JFK in October 2024, I counted seven buses pass before one stopped with a functioning ramp. The driver who finally lowered it told me the previous buses "weren't set up for that." At Heathrow in March 2025, the accessible shuttle ran on a separate schedule—every fifteen minutes instead of every three—and the signage directing passengers to it had been placed behind a construction barrier. At Newark itself, in 2019, before the current project began, I missed a connection because the AirTrain elevator was out of service and the alternative route added forty minutes.

The new system will use automated people-movers. No drivers. This is presented as progress. But automated systems fail in predictable ways, and the question is always: what happens when they fail? A stuck elevator with a human attendant is different from a stuck elevator with a help button connected to a call center in another state.

---

The Port Authority says it will pause the most disruptive construction work during peak summer travel, from Memorial Day through Labor Day. This is a concession to volume, not to access. The disruption they are pausing is the disruption that affects the largest number of travelers. The disruption that affects the smallest number of travelers—the wheelchair user who cannot board the shuttle, the blind passenger whose wayfinding cues have been rerouted, the person with a cardiac condition who cannot walk the temporary path in July heat—that disruption continues.

Kevin Irvine, who runs the transit advocacy group Riders Alliance, said in 2023 that construction mitigation plans "almost never include specific provisions for passengers with disabilities." He was talking about the Second Avenue Subway extension, but the pattern holds. Mitigation is designed for the median traveler, and the median traveler can walk.

Here is what I want to know about Newark's shuttle buses: How many have functioning lifts? How many drivers have been trained to operate them? What is the average deployment time? What happens when a lift fails mid-boarding? Is there a backup vehicle, and how long does it take to arrive? What is the protocol when a passenger misses a flight because the accessible option did not function?

None of these questions appear in the construction notice. None of them appear in the press coverage. The $3.5 billion figure appears everywhere.

---

I have been writing about transit access for fifteen years, and the pattern is always the same. The blueprint shows a ramp. The ribbon-cutting shows a ramp. The Tuesday after the ribbon-cutting, someone parks a luggage cart in front of the ramp, and there is no protocol for moving it, because the protocol assumed the ramp would remain clear.

The gap between the ramp on the blueprint and the ramp blocked on a Wednesday is the gap my entire field refuses to measure. Newark is spending $3.5 billion to close a gap that does not exist on paper—the aging infrastructure, the capacity constraints, the outdated technology—while leaving unmeasured the gap that exists in practice.

Aimi Hamraie, who studies accessible design at Vanderbilt, has written about how "universal design" often becomes "design for the universal user," which is to say, design for a body that can walk, hear, see, and process information at standard speed. The Newark replacement system promises greater capacity. Greater capacity for whom?

I called the Port Authority's press office in February to ask about accessibility provisions in the shuttle bus operation. The spokesperson told me the buses were "ADA compliant." I asked what that meant in practice—how many accessible buses per hour, what the average wait time was, what the protocol was for equipment failure. She said she would get back to me. She did not.

---

The construction timeline runs through 2030. Five years of disruption, five years of "budget extra time," five years of shuttle buses running every four minutes. For most travelers, this is an inconvenience. For some of us, it is a calculation: Is this trip possible? Is this trip worth the risk?

I have become very good at these calculations. I can estimate, within ten minutes, how long it will take me to navigate an unfamiliar airport during construction. I can guess, with reasonable accuracy, whether the accessible route will actually be accessible. I have learned to arrive two hours early when the standard advice is one, because the standard advice assumes my body.

What I cannot calculate is the cost of all this calculation. The flights I did not book because the connection was too tight. The trips I did not take because the airport was under construction and I could not trust the mitigation plan. The meetings I attended by video because the in-person option required a transfer I could not guarantee.

Newark's AirTrain replacement will cost $3.5 billion. The cost of the disruption—the real cost, counted in missed flights and missed opportunities and the slow accumulation of trips not taken—does not appear in any budget line. It is borne by the people who bear it, invisibly, and the system that created it never learns what it cost.

The shuttle buses run every four minutes. The question is whether you can board them.
