TITLE: 80%

The camera above the self-checkout sees you pick up the avocado. You lift it toward the scanner. You do not scan it. The system flags this. A video replays on your screen: you, from above, holding the avocado, not scanning it. The system gives you the chance to self-correct.

This is how Everseen's computer-vision system works at more than 1,700 Kroger stores. Tom Arigi, Kroger's director of asset protection, says the video-replay step resolves roughly 80% of flagged incidents without staff intervention. The number matters. It means the system is built to reach resolution before a human employee enters the frame. It means the loop — detection, display, correction — can close without anyone speaking to anyone.

I stood at a self-checkout in Rotterdam last month. The machine made a sound. I did not hear it. The screen changed, but the change was small — a flashing border, a message in text I had already stopped reading because I was bagging. A staff member appeared beside me. She had been watching a screen I could not see. She pointed at my basket. I had double-scanned something. The machine had been asking me to remove it. I had not known.

The 80% figure assumes a particular body. It assumes a shopper who hears the alert tone, who reads the text prompt in real time, who can follow the replay and connect it to the action they just performed. It assumes that the moment of correction arrives and is usable in the same instant. For me, the alert arrived, but it arrived in a channel I do not have. The correction window passed. The human appeared. I was flagged.

---

Alex Siskos, Everseen's vice president of strategy and growth, describes the goal: "give the customer the chance to self-correct first." The word is *chance*. A chance is an opening in time. It is not a guarantee. The system offers the opening, and the opening has a shape. The shape is: overhead camera, replay, text prompt, countdown, correction. If your body fits that shape, you get the chance. If it does not, you get the staff member.

KanduAI's produce-recognition system works differently but lands in the same place. The deep-learning image recognition identifies your fruit, shows you a short list of likely matches, and you select. Ariel Shemesh, the founder, says the system reaches roughly 92% accuracy on the first attempt. The old method — searching a manual code list for "avocado" — required you to read, scroll, type. The new method shows you pictures. This is better for many people. It is faster. It reduces errors from 18% to under 4% in pilot stores. But the interface still assumes you can see the screen, that you can distinguish the images, that you can tap the correct one in the time the system gives you.

I am not blind. I can see the screen. But I have stood next to Deaf friends who are also blind, who navigate by touch and by timing, who have memorized the produce codes because no system ever showed them anything. The 92% accuracy is real. The 8% failure is also real. Where does the 8% land? On whom?

The facial-recognition age-verification systems being tested at some self-checkouts compare your face to an ID image already on file with the retailer's app. Lukas Kinigadner, co-founder and CEO of Anyline, calls this "a natural extension of computer-vision checkout generally." The phrase is revealing. *Natural extension* suggests a smooth continuation — the camera already watches, so why not let it verify? But the extension is not smooth. It requires you to have registered an ID image. It requires your face to match. It requires the lighting, the angle, the skin tone, the expression to fall within the system's tolerance.

I do not know what happens when the system cannot verify. Does it flag you? Does it call staff? Does it lock the register? The article does not say. The gap is instructive. The system is built to succeed. Its failure mode is someone else's problem.

---

A recent survey found 67% of shoppers reported experiencing some kind of self-checkout system failure. More than a quarter admitted they had used a self-checkout error to avoid paying for an item at least once. The industry estimates annual US retail losses to theft at more than $60 billion. These numbers drive the push toward camera-based detection. The logic is straightforward: if people are stealing, watch them more closely.

But the watching is not neutral. The watching arrives through a medium. The medium has a shape. The shape decides who is legible and who is not.

In 2019, I tried to return a jacket at a department store. The receipt was on my phone. The staff member asked me to explain the return. I gestured to the receipt. She asked me to speak. I signed that I am Deaf. She called a manager. The manager called another manager. The jacket sat on the counter for eleven minutes. I had the receipt. I had the jacket. I had the tags. The return was valid. But the system — the human system, not the machine — could not process me without speech. I was flagged by being illegible.

The AI camera systems are faster than that manager. They do not hesitate. They flag and replay in seconds. But speed does not fix the shape problem. It compresses it. The window for correction is smaller. The loop closes faster. If you fit, you self-correct. If you do not, you are handed to staff. The 80% resolution rate is a success metric for the system. It is not a success metric for the 20%.

Tom Arigi's statement is precise: the video-replay step resolves roughly 80% of flagged incidents without staff intervention. The remaining 20% requires a human. What I want to know is: who is in that 20%? How many of them were not stealing? How many of them simply could not hear the tone, or see the replay in time, or read the prompt fast enough, or respond in the channel the system expected?

The system does not track this. The system tracks resolution. The system tracks shrink. The system does not track who was legible and who was not.

---

At a Deaf Village gathering years ago, I bought coffee from a stall. The vendor was Deaf. I signed my order. She signed back. The transaction took eight seconds. No screen. No replay. No correction window. No chance to self-correct, because there was nothing to correct. The channel matched. The loop closed. No one was flagged.

That stall does not exist anymore. The village was temporary. I sometimes want to move back to a place that no longer exists. The question is: where would I even go?

Kroger's 1,700 stores are not going anywhere. The cameras are overhead. The replay is ready. The 80% will self-correct. The rest of us will wait for the staff member, who may or may not know how to speak to us, who may or may not have been trained, who may or may not believe we were not stealing.

The avocado is still in my hand.
