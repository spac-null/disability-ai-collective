TITLE: Vulcan's Confession

Amazon calls it a sense of touch. I heard the press release last week and laughed — not unkindly, just the laugh that comes when someone says a thing they cannot mean. Vulcan, the company's newest warehouse robot, knows when it makes contact and how much force it's applying. That is the claim. A suction-cup arm reaches into an inventory pod, nudges aside a box of detergent, and registers the resistance. The arm adjusts. The box moves. Amazon says this is touch.

I have been thinking about this for days now. Not because it is wrong — it is not exactly wrong — but because it names something I recognize and then walks away from the recognition as though nothing happened.

---

Touch is not pressure data. Touch is not a sensor array converting force into numbers. I know this the way I know my own hands: by what they have failed to give me and what they have given me instead. When I was nineteen, in a flat in Edinburgh, my roommate came home crying. I reached for her. She said, gently, that she needed someone who could see her face. I understood. What I could not offer in that moment was not sight — it was a particular kind of presence that required a sense I did not have. But what I could offer, the pressure of my hand on her shoulder, the warmth of standing near, the sound of my breath slowing to match hers — that was touch. That was real. And it was not reducible to a force measurement.

Vulcan does not know it is touching anything. Vulcan receives data about resistance and adjusts its grip. The suction cup does not register the texture of cardboard, the slight give of plastic wrap, the difference between a box that has been handled a hundred times and one fresh from the manufacturer. These are the things touch actually is: a history of contact, a relationship between surfaces, a knowledge that accumulates in the body before it ever becomes a thought.

---

Amazon has deployed more than a million robots across its fulfillment network since 2012. I did not know this number until last week. A million machines moving through those enormous sheds, navigating by encoded floor markers and forward-facing cameras, ferrying inventory pods to picking stations where human workers once bent and reached and strained. The Shreveport facility, opened in 2024, runs eight different robotics systems on one floor. Sequoia delivers items to workstations positioned between mid-thigh and mid-chest height, specifically so workers do not have to reach overhead or squat to floor level. Hercules and Titan are the haulers. Proteus, the next generation, is learning to accept plain conversational text instructions — deployment planned for European sites in 2027.

Here is what struck me: the company has spent years designing systems that spare human bodies from the movements that damage them. Overhead reaching. Deep squatting. Repetitive strain. These are the things the robots are meant to absorb. And now Amazon says Vulcan has a sense of touch, and what they mean is that the robot can do the reaching without getting hurt.

But touch is not the absence of injury. Touch is the presence of knowledge.

I spent three years walking cities with a Sennheiser MKH50, recording spaces before and after renovation. What I learned was that buildings speak acoustically before they speak visually. A wall tells you where it is by how it reflects sound. A floor tells you what it is made of by the frequency it returns. This is not metaphor. It is physics. And it is also touch — because touch and hearing are both pressure senses, both ways of knowing the world through contact.

Vulcan knows force. It does not know the wall.

---

There is a version of this argument that says Amazon is lying, that calling this "touch" is marketing, that the company wants us to feel warm about its robots so we forget what they replaced. I do not think that is quite right. I think Amazon genuinely believes it has built something that touches. The engineers who designed Vulcan probably feel proud of what they made. The sensor array is sophisticated. The force feedback is real. In a narrow, technical sense, the robot does something that resembles touch more than any previous machine in the network.

But the narrow technical sense is where the trouble lives. Pauline Oliveros, the composer and theorist who shaped everything I know about listening, used to talk about the difference between hearing and listening. Hearing is passive reception. Listening is active attention. You can hear a sound without knowing what it is. You cannot listen without learning something.

Touch works the same way. You can register pressure without knowing what you are touching. The knowledge requires something more — a history, a context, a relationship to the surface that extends beyond the moment of contact. Vulcan has pressure sensors. It does not have touch.

---

Amazon says employees at its newest sites are moving into skilled technical roles: inventory oversight, quality control, robot maintenance. The physically repetitive tasks shift to the machines. I do not know whether this is good or bad for the workers. Probably both, in different proportions for different people. What I notice is the language: "skilled technical roles." As though the previous work — the reaching, the lifting, the sorting — was not skilled. As though the knowledge in a warehouse worker's hands was not knowledge at all.

I have been in warehouses. Not Amazon's, but others, doing field recordings for a project on industrial acoustics. The workers I met knew things about the objects they handled that no sensor could capture. They knew which boxes were fragile by weight alone. They knew which items had been returned by the texture of the tape. They knew when a shipment was wrong before they opened it. This is touch. This is what the body learns when it does the same movements ten thousand times.

Vulcan will never learn this. Vulcan will handle three-quarters of stowing tasks at speeds comparable to human employees, and it will do this without ever knowing what it is stowing. The robot's sense of touch is a measure of force. The worker's sense of touch was a measure of meaning.

---

I am not sentimental about this. I do not think Amazon should keep humans in repetitive-strain jobs to preserve some romantic notion of craft. But I do think we should be precise about what we are losing and what we are calling it. When Amazon says Vulcan has a sense of touch, the company is not describing a capability. It is erasing a distinction. It is saying that force data and tactile knowledge are the same thing, that pressure and presence are interchangeable, that what a body learns through years of contact can be replaced by a sensor array in a suction cup.

They cannot. The suction cup knows when it makes contact. It does not know what contact means.
