TITLE: Vulcan Knows When It Touches Something

The robot has a sense of touch, and I cannot stop thinking about what it doesn't hear.

Amazon's Vulcan arm, deployed this year across the company's fulfillment network, uses suction cups fitted with cameras to pick and stow inventory. The press release says it "knows when it makes contact and how much force it's applying." This is the language of sensation borrowed for machines. Contact. Force. Knowing. I read the announcement in my flat in Amsterdam at four in the morning, the canal outside doing its usual thing — water lapping brick, a bicycle bell somewhere distant, a barge engine idling two bridges down. Vulcan works in a building designed to be silent to human ears. I wondered what it would hear if it could.

---

Here is what I mean. I spent three years recording warehouses with a Sennheiser MKH50 before and after renovation. The old ones — the ones built in the 1970s for manual labor — were loud in ways that told you things. Concrete floors echoed footsteps back. Metal shelving rang when you brushed it. The air moved audibly through vents you could locate by sound alone. In 2016 I stood in a distribution center outside Rotterdam that had just been retrofitted for automation. The new floor was coated with something rubberized, dense, absorptive. The shelving had been replaced with polymer pods that swallowed sound. I tapped my cane against a support column and got almost nothing back. The space had been made quiet for the robots, not for me.

This is not a complaint about accessibility. This is an observation about who a building speaks to.

Vulcan's camera sees the object. Its suction cup measures resistance. It calculates how much force to apply. Amazon says it can now handle roughly three-quarters of stowing tasks at speeds comparable to human employees. Those human employees, meanwhile, work at stations positioned between mid-thigh and mid-chest height — a design choice the company says reduces repetitive-strain injury from overhead reaching and squatting. Sequoia, Hercules, Titan, Proteus: the robots ferry inventory pods using forward-facing 3D cameras and encoded floor markers to navigate around people and other machines.

Encoded floor markers. That phrase sat with me for days. The building communicates to the robot through a visual code pressed into the floor. The robot reads the building. The building does not speak to the robot acoustically because the robot does not need it to. The robot does not echolocate. The robot does not hear its own movement returned to it by the walls. The robot navigates by sight alone, and the building has been redesigned to serve that.

---

Georgina Kleege, the writer and scholar who is blind, has argued that blind people often know more about visual representation than sighted people because we've had to think about it. We've had to decode a world that wasn't built for us. We've had to reverse-engineer the logic of spaces designed for eyes. This gives us a kind of literacy sighted people don't develop — not because we're special, but because we had no choice.

I think about that argument when I read about Vulcan's touch. The robot has been given one channel of sensory feedback: contact and force. It has not been given sound. It has not been given the ability to hear the pod rattle when it's unbalanced, or the shelf creak when it's overloaded, or the floor change pitch when something is blocking the path. Those were cues human workers used to have. Those were cues I use every time I enter a room.

A friend of mine — sighted, worked at a fulfillment center in Germany for eleven months in 2019 — told me once that the old warehouse floors used to warn you. You could hear when a forklift was coming around a corner. You could hear when a shelf was about to tip. The new floors don't do that. The new floors are engineered for silence because silence is what the robots prefer. She said it felt like working in a room that had stopped talking to her.

I don't think Amazon set out to build a space hostile to human perception. I think they built a space optimized for machine perception and assumed humans would adapt. This is what happens when you design for one channel and forget the others exist.

---

Murray Schafer, the composer who invented the term "soundscape," spent his career arguing that we live in acoustic environments as surely as we live in visual ones. He was right about that and wrong about almost everything else — his nostalgia for pre-industrial silence was its own kind of romantic blindness. But the core insight holds. A space has a sound. That sound tells you something. When you strip the sound away, you strip away information.

Vulcan knows when it touches something. This is remarkable. It is also partial. The robot has been given the sense that serves the task: contact, force, positioning. It has not been given the senses that would serve understanding. It does not know what the building is doing around it. It does not know what the air is telling it. It works in a space that has been flattened into a visual grid with tactile feedback, and that is enough for the work, and that is all the work requires.

I am not saying the robot should hear. I am saying the building used to speak, and now it doesn't, and the people who work there have lost something they may not have words for.

A next-generation version of Proteus is being tested that can accept plain, conversational text instructions rather than pre-programmed routes. Deployment is planned for European sites in 2027. The robot will understand language. It will still not understand the room.

Last month I stood in the doorway of a community pool at 5:45 in the morning. I do this sometimes — not to swim, just to listen to the water hear itself back. The pool is old. The tiles are cracked. The ceiling is high and domed and returns every splash with a half-second delay that tells me exactly how big the space is. If they ever renovate it, they will probably install sound-absorbing panels. They will probably make it quieter. They will probably call it an improvement.

Vulcan touches. Vulcan does not listen. The building has been rebuilt to match.
