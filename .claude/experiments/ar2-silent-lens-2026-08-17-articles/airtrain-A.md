TITLE: Shuttle Bus Interval

The Port Authority suspended rail service between Newark Airport's rail station and P4 from 5 a.m. to 3 p.m. on January 15, 2026. Shuttle buses now run every four to five minutes through the construction zone. The press release calls this "replacement service." I call it something else.

A bus that comes every four to five minutes sounds reliable until you are waiting for the second one. The first bus, the one that arrived on time, had no lift. Or the lift worked but the driver had not been trained to deploy it. Or the lift deployed but the securement straps were missing. Or the straps existed but someone's luggage was blocking the wheelchair space. I have lived all four scenarios. I have watched the second bus pull away while still explaining the problem with the first.

---

My father drove the 4735 in Santo André for twenty-two years. I grew up knowing that a published schedule is a promise a transit authority makes to itself, not to its riders. The gap between the schedule and the arrival is where working-class life happens. You learn to read it. You learn what four to five minutes actually means when you cannot simply step onto the next vehicle.

The Port Authority's construction timeline runs through 2030. Five years of suspended rail service during peak daytime hours. Five years of shuttle buses as the only ground-level connection between the regional rail station and the terminals. The new system will have three additional stations, greater passenger capacity, an automated people-mover design. The renderings show wide platforms and smooth curves. I have seen a lot of renderings.

Here is what the renderings do not show: the moment of transfer. The place where the system assumes you can move from one mode to another without assistance, without delay, without the accumulated friction of a body that does not match the baseline. A shuttle bus is not a train. The geometry is different. The boarding height is different. The number of people who must move out of your way is different. The social negotiation required to occupy space you are legally entitled to occupy is different.

I spent three hours last month reading the Port Authority's environmental impact statement for the new AirTrain. Forty-seven pages on noise mitigation. Twelve pages on stormwater management. Six pages on "accessibility features" that describe the stations as "fully compliant." Compliant with what, the document does not specify. Compliant is a word that sounds like a promise but functions as a ceiling.

---

In 2019, I interviewed a woman named Dolores who had worked as a gate agent at Newark for thirty-one years. She told me about a passenger who missed his flight because the elevator between the AirTrain platform and the terminal was out of service. "He was early," she said. "He did everything right. He just couldn't get there." The airline rebooked him. No compensation. The system had worked as designed.

I asked her how often the elevator was out of service. She laughed. "You want the real number or the reported number?"

The real number and the reported number are never the same. This is the gap my entire field refuses to measure. A ramp exists on the blueprint. A ramp exists in the compliance report. A ramp exists in the press release announcing $3.5 billion in modernized amenities. Whether the ramp exists at 6:47 a.m. on a Tuesday when your flight leaves at 8:15 is a different question. Whether the ramp exists when the shuttle bus driver does not know how to deploy the lift is a different question. Whether the ramp exists when the construction crew has staged equipment in the accessible loading zone is a different question.

The Port Authority has promised to pause the most disruptive construction work during peak summer travel, from Memorial Day through Labor Day. I read this sentence three times. Disruptive to whom? A construction delay that adds eight minutes to a walking passenger's journey is an inconvenience. The same delay that adds forty minutes to a wheelchair user's journey because the alternative route does not exist is something else. The Port Authority does not appear to have a word for it.

I do. The word is exclusion. But exclusion sounds abstract, like a policy failure, like something that happens to a category rather than a person. So let me be more precise. The word is waiting. The word is watching. The word is the second bus pulling away while you are still explaining.

My father used to say that a bus driver learns more about a city than any planner. He knew which stops had shelter from the rain and which did not. He knew which corners flooded in summer storms. He knew which passengers needed extra time to board and which drivers gave them that time and which did not. The schedule said four minutes. The reality said something else.

The new Newark AirTrain will cost $3.5 billion. The old one opened in 1996 and has run for nearly thirty years. Three decades of service, and the replacement still uses the same basic design: an automated people-mover connecting terminals to parking facilities to the regional rail station. The stations will be new. The platforms will be wider. The capacity will be greater. The fundamental assumption—that passengers can move between modes without assistance, without delay, without friction—will remain unchanged.

I am not arguing that the project should not happen. I am arguing that the project's definition of success is too narrow. A system that moves more passengers per hour is not automatically better. A system that moves passengers who were already able to move is not progress. Progress would be measuring something else: the number of passengers who can now travel independently who could not before. The reduction in transfer time for passengers who require assistance. The percentage of elevator outages resolved within one hour rather than one day.

These measurements exist. Transit agencies in Tokyo, in Zurich, in Singapore track them. The Port Authority does not. Or if it does, the numbers do not appear in the environmental impact statement, in the press releases, in the renderings of wide platforms and smooth curves.

There is a hill on Prospect Park West where the grade is perfect. I can hit a speed that makes my eyes water. The feeling is not compensation for anything. It is not overcoming. It is just the thing itself: velocity, momentum, the particular pleasure of a body moving through space without obstruction.

The Newark AirTrain will never give anyone that feeling. Transit infrastructure is not supposed to. But it could give something adjacent: the absence of friction. The experience of moving through a system without having to explain yourself, without having to wait for the second bus, without having to calculate whether you have enough time to miss one elevator and still make your flight.

That absence has a cost. The $3.5 billion does not include it. The 2030 completion date does not account for it. The compliance reports will certify that the stations meet the minimum requirements, and the minimum requirements will remain the ceiling, and somewhere in Terminal B a passenger will miss a flight because the shuttle bus driver did not know how to deploy the lift, and the system will have worked as designed.
