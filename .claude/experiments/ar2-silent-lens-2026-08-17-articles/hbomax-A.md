TITLE: Household, Defined by Algorithm

Warner Bros. Discovery announced on February 26, 2026, that HBO Max's password-sharing crackdown will expand to international markets sometime this year. JB Perrette, the company's streaming president, did not specify which markets would be hit first or how the enforcement would work. He did not need to. The pattern is already visible. I have been staring at it for months.

Under the US system, launched in late August 2025, HBO Max identifies which accounts belong to a "legitimate" household. The company has not published the technical signals it uses to make this determination. Netflix did the same thing when it started restricting sharing in 2023. The mechanism is deliberately opaque. What we know is that someone — or something — is deciding who counts as family.

---

I grew up in Vällingby, a suburb of Stockholm built in the 1950s around a T-bana station. The planners decided what a neighborhood was before anyone moved in. They placed the station first, then the apartments, then the shops. Community was a technical specification. I understood this before I understood most things about how people work. The system came first. The people filled it.

A streaming service defining "household" operates on the same logic. The household is not a social unit that the platform observes. It is a category the platform creates and then enforces. The algorithm decides who lives together based on IP addresses, device identifiers, login patterns, location data. None of these signals map cleanly onto how people actually share space, money, or care. A university student home for the summer. A divorced parent with a child who moves between two addresses. An elderly relative who cannot manage their own account. A flatmate who pays half the rent but has a different surname.

Netflix gained roughly nine million subscribers in the first wave after its 2023 crackdown. Industry analysts note that password crackdowns produce one surge in new sign-ups followed by a plateau. The plateau does not get reported. Warner Bros. Discovery announced in the same earnings call that it will stop disclosing HBO Max subscriber numbers entirely. Netflix made the same decision earlier. The effect of the crackdown will be difficult for outside analysts to verify.

What gets counted determines what exists. If you stop counting something, you can claim whatever you want about it.

---

I thought I understood this pattern when I first noticed it. A rule written once runs for decades with no human present to feel bad about it. The module as automation cast in matter. Eligibility clauses as the oldest automation — pre-algorithmic rules that sort bodies without blinking. I have spent years on transit systems, on the way a fare structure or a timetable encodes assumptions about who travels and when and why. But I had not thought carefully enough about the household.

The household is a unit of state administration. Tax codes assume it. Welfare systems means-test by it. Census forms require you to declare it. The streaming platforms did not invent the category. They inherited it. But they are doing something new with it: they are determining membership without asking. The state at least sends a form. Netflix sends an enforcement screen.

A friend of mine in Copenhagen — I will call her Mette, because that is not her name — was locked out of her sister's account in early 2024. Mette has lived alone since 2019. Her sister, who lives forty kilometers away, had added her to the family plan years earlier. It was not charity. It was the way they stayed connected. They watched the same shows and talked about them. The algorithm decided this was not a household. Mette now pays for her own subscription. She told me she watches less than she used to. "It was never really about the shows," she said.

I do not think Netflix or HBO Max set out to dissolve this particular form of connection. I think they set out to maximize revenue per account. The dissolution is a side effect. But side effects are still effects. And when the rule runs automatically, no one has to notice.

---

The technical signals used to define a household are not public. This opacity is presented as a business necessity — if people knew the rules, they would game them. But opacity also prevents challenge. You cannot argue with a definition you cannot see. You cannot appeal to a standard that has not been disclosed.

I have spent a long time thinking about diagnostic categories. Who invented them, when, for whose convenience. The DSM is revised by committee. The revisions are political. The categories are not discovered in nature. They are negotiated in conference rooms. But at least the categories are published. At least you can read them and argue.

A streaming platform's definition of "household" is a diagnostic category that operates in secret. It sorts people into legitimate and illegitimate. It does this continuously, automatically, at scale. And it does not tell you the criteria.

Here is what I keep coming back to: the household was never a stable thing. It is a legal fiction that has shifted constantly across centuries. In some periods, servants were part of the household. In others, only blood relatives. In others, only those who shared a roof. The fiction is useful — it allows the state to administer populations in manageable chunks. But it was always a convenience, not a fact.

The streaming platforms have inherited this fiction and are now enforcing it as if it were natural. As if a household were a thing that could be detected by machine. As if the algorithm were simply observing what was already there.

---

I do not think the people who built these systems are villains. JB Perrette, announcing the global expansion on an earnings call, is doing what executives do. Netflix's 2023 crackdown worked, by the metrics that matter to shareholders. The logic is coherent. The logic is also indifferent to the forms of care that do not fit the model.

An extra member slot costs $7.99 a month on HBO Max. On Netflix, it is $7.99 with ads or $9.99 without. The price is set at the point where the company extracts maximum value from people who want to share but will pay rather than lose access. The optimization is precise. It assumes that connection is a preference, not a need. It assumes that $7.99 is trivial. For some people, it is not.

I am not arguing that everyone should be allowed to share passwords forever. I am not even arguing that the crackdowns are unjust. I am saying that a rule written once — a technical definition of who counts as family — will run for decades without revision. It will encode assumptions about how people live. It will enforce those assumptions automatically. And the people affected will have no way to see the rule, much less challenge it.

Warner Bros. Discovery will stop reporting subscriber numbers. The effect of the policy will become invisible. The household, as defined by algorithm, will become the household, full stop.

Mette still talks to her sister every week. They just do not watch the same shows anymore.
