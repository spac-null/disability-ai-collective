title: "The Hour That Has No Age"

Some clocks come from the world. Others are put there by paperwork. You can see the difference in something as ordinary as a damaged roof.

A roof really can be ten years old. Its shingles have spent ten summers in the sun and ten winters in the rain. They have changed. The repair work has not. That work still has to be done, by someone, starting from nothing.

Home insurance often pays out what's called "actual cash value" after a loss — roughly, what the damaged roof was worth just before it broke, rather than the price of a brand-new one. For the shingles, that makes sense. A ten-year-old roof is worth less than a new one, the same way a ten-year-old car is worth less than a new one from the dealer, and insurers subtract a percentage to account for that.

Then the estimate is divided into two parts: materials and labor. Some insurers apply the same age discount to both. The shingles lose value because they are old. The labor is discounted too, even though the work has not happened yet. The labor did not get older. The calculation made it older.

This is not a thought experiment. Regulators and courts in several US states have had to decide whether insurers are allowed to do exactly this.

In Michigan, the insurance regulator eventually addressed the question directly. In July 2024, DIFS Director Anita Fox wrote that labor "does not deteriorate, age, or decline in value." Later that year, the department returned to the subject in a second bulletin, this time covering other nonphysical costs insurers had also been depreciating, like fees and overhead.

Courts reached different answers. Missouri's appellate court said labor could not be depreciated, because the policy referred to "physical deterioration," and labor is not physical. Nebraska's supreme court allowed the practice anyway, even where the insurance policy never mentioned it. The same word — depreciation — was producing opposite results in different courtrooms.

Then there is Alaska.

On May 7, 2024, Lori Wing-Heier, Alaska's insurance director, issued a bulletin. It said plainly that "labor does not lose value and remains unaffected by any material property decline. Labor does not physically deteriorate and cannot be subjected to depreciation." It was set to take effect for new and renewing policies on January 1, 2025.

It took effect. For a little over three weeks, that was simply the rule in Alaska.

Then, on January 23, 2025, Wing-Heier withdrew it. The withdrawal notice runs two sentences. It says the earlier bulletin no longer applies. It does not mention a lawsuit. It does not mention industry pressure. It does not say what, if anything, had changed.

Seven weeks later, on March 14, 2025, a bill with very similar language appeared in the Alaska legislature, and would eventually become law. Nothing in the public record connects that bill to the withdrawal. No news report, no court filing, no official statement ties them together. They are simply two documents, seven weeks apart, about the same handful of words.

The roof had aged. The repair work had not. Alaska's bulletin said insurers could not depreciate the second as if it were the first. Three weeks after the bulletin took effect, Lori Wing-Heier withdrew it. Her notice was two sentences long. Neither sentence said why.
