---
title: "The Hour That Has No Age"
byline: Maya Flux
draft_status: scout-v0.2-shadow-only
---

A roof can be ten years into a twenty-year life. The shingles have taken sun and rain for a decade; they really have worn down. The hour of work it will take to replace them next month has not. Nobody has been sitting on that ladder since 2014. It is this year's hour, priced at this year's rate, performed by someone with this year's back.

Insurance calls the payout for a damaged roof its "actual cash value," and normally that idea is simple enough: pay out what the thing was worth right before it broke, not what a brand-new one would cost. A ten-year-old roof isn't worth a new roof's price, so the insurer depreciates it — knocks a percentage off for age, the same way a used car is worth less than a showroom one. That much tracks.

The strange part is what some insurers did next. When they estimated the cost of the repair, they split it into materials and labor, and then applied the same age-based discount to both halves — depreciating the labor at the identical rate as the shingles, as though the hour of work had also spent ten years exposed to weather. On a typical roof job, industry cost data puts labor at somewhere between two-fifths and three-fifths of the total bill. Apply a standard fifty-percent depreciation — the rate for a roof that's ten years into a twenty-year life — to that portion, and a homeowner is illustratively short somewhere in the range of a couple thousand dollars, withheld from a check meant to pay for work that hasn't happened yet, at a rate that hasn't moved. That figure is built from real average cost data, not one documented claim — but the shape of it, money quietly subtracted from an hour that never aged, is real regardless of the exact number on any particular check.

A category on a form did that. Nothing about the labor changed. The form simply treated "depreciation" as if it were a fact about the work, rather than a rule someone chose to apply to it.

Michigan's insurance regulator eventually said so in writing. In a bulletin dated July 3, 2024, DIFS Director Anita Fox stated that "nontangible items such as labor are not physical objects and do not deteriorate, age, or decline in value regardless of any potential decline in value to the physical property in need of repair." It was not the last word the department needed on the subject. A second bulletin followed later that year, spelling out a fuller list of the nontangible things insurers had apparently been depreciating alongside labor — taxes, fees, overhead, profit. A regulator had to return to its own rule and say it again, more specifically, because the first version left room to keep doing the thing it was meant to stop.

Not every court reads the word "depreciation" the same way. A federal court predicting how Kansas law would come out upheld depreciating labor, reasoning that if only materials are discounted, a homeowner who repairs the damage cheaply — or does it themselves — walks away with money for labor costs "she never incurred." That is a real argument, not a careless one. Missouri's own appellate court went the other way, ruling that a repair cost limited to "physical deterioration and obsolescence" cannot include labor, because labor was never physical to begin with. Nebraska's supreme court went further than either, permitting labor depreciation even where the policy never explicitly says insurers are allowed to do it. Three courts, one phrase, three different answers — because "actual cash value" was never defined precisely enough to settle what happens once you try to age something that was never aging in the first place.

Then there is Alaska.

On May 7, 2024, Alaska's insurance director, Lori Wing-Heier, issued Bulletin B 24-07. Its language was almost blunt: "labor does not lose value and remains unaffected by any material property decline. Labor does not physically deteriorate and cannot be subjected to depreciation." It was set to take effect on new and renewing policies starting January 1, 2025.

It did take effect. For a little over three weeks, it was simply the rule in Alaska: an insurer could not treat an hour of labor as though it had aged.

On January 23, 2025, Wing-Heier withdrew it. The withdrawal notice, Bulletin B 25-03, is two sentences long. It states that the earlier bulletin is rescinded. That is the entire content. No litigation is cited. No industry objection is named. No change in the underlying facts is offered — labor did not somehow start aging between May and January. The bulletin simply says the previous bulletin no longer applies, and stops there.

Seven weeks later, on March 14, 2025, a bill containing substantially similar language — barring labor depreciation, with a narrow carve-out — was introduced in the Alaska legislature. It would eventually become law, effective January 1, 2026. Whether that bill is the reason the bulletin was pulled is not something the public record answers. No press statement, no news account, no comment letter, and no litigation record connects the two events. They simply happened seven weeks apart, and nobody in Alaska's own filing cabinet was required to say whether one caused the other.

That gap — a real consumer protection issued, enforced for three weeks, and withdrawn without a stated reason — is not a research failure to be filled in with a plausible guess. It's the most honest thing in this story: sometimes a rule appears, works exactly as written, and disappears, and no document anywhere explains why.

The roof in this story really can be ten years old. Everyone agrees on that, including every court that has ever ruled on either side of this question. What none of them fully agree on is what to do with the fact that the hour needed to fix it cannot be. Alaska's regulator wrote the plainest version of that distinction into law for three weeks in the winter of 2025, and then, for reasons nobody has had to give, wrote it back out.
