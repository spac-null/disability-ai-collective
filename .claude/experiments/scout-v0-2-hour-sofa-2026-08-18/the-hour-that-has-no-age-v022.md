---
title: "The Hour That Has No Age"
byline: Maya Flux
draft_status: scout-v0.2.2-shadow-only
---

A roof can be ten years old. The work to repair it next month hasn't happened yet. Some insurers have applied the roof's age to both.

That raises a strange question: how old is an hour of labor?

Home insurance often pays what's called "actual cash value" — roughly, what the damaged roof was worth just before it broke, not the price of a brand-new one. An old roof gets discounted for its age. That much makes sense. Shingles really do wear down after ten years in the sun.

Some insurers also apply that same age discount to the labor needed to fix the roof. The discount that applies to the old shingles gets applied to work that hasn't happened yet. The labor did not get older. The calculation made it older.

This is not hypothetical. Regulators and courts in several US states have had to decide whether insurers may do exactly this.

Michigan's insurance regulator addressed it directly. In a bulletin dated July 3, 2024, DIFS Director Anita Fox wrote that labor "does not deteriorate, age, or decline in value" the way physical property does. A second bulletin followed later that year, naming other nonphysical costs — taxes, fees, overhead — that insurers had also been depreciating.

Courts have split. Missouri's appellate court ruled that labor cannot be depreciated, because it was never physical and so cannot "physically deteriorate." Nebraska's supreme court ruled the opposite way, allowing labor depreciation even when the insurance policy never mentions it. Same question. Opposite answers.

Then there is Alaska.

On May 7, 2024, Alaska's insurance director, Lori Wing-Heier, issued a bulletin. It stated plainly that "labor does not lose value and remains unaffected by any material property decline. Labor does not physically deteriorate and cannot be subjected to depreciation." The bulletin was set to take effect for new and renewing policies on January 1, 2025.

It took effect. For about three weeks, insurers in Alaska could not depreciate labor.

On January 23, 2025, Wing-Heier withdrew the bulletin. The withdrawal notice is two sentences long. It says only that the earlier bulletin no longer applies. It does not mention a lawsuit. It does not mention industry objections. It gives no reason at all.

Seven weeks later, on March 14, 2025, a bill with very similar language was introduced in the Alaska legislature. It would eventually become law. Whether the bill and the withdrawal are connected is not something the public record answers. No news report, no court filing, and no official statement ties the two together. They simply happened seven weeks apart.

The roof was old. The repair work was still in the future. For about three weeks, Alaska's bulletin treated those as two different things. Then the bulletin was withdrawn, and nobody was required to say why.
