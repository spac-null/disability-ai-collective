---
title: "The Hour That Has No Age"
byline: Maya Flux

A roof can be ten years old. The shingles really have spent ten years in sun and rain. But the work needed to replace them next month is new. A roofer will do that work next month, not ten years ago. The hour itself has no age at all.

Insurance has a name for what it pays out when a roof is damaged: "actual cash value." The idea is simple. Pay out what the roof was worth right before it broke, not the price of a brand-new one. A ten-year-old roof is worth less than a new roof, so the insurer subtracts a percentage for age. That part makes sense. A used car is worth less than one straight from the factory, for the same reason.

Here is where it gets strange. When insurers estimate the cost of a repair, they often split the bill into two parts: materials and labor. Some insurers then apply the same age-based discount to both parts. The shingles get discounted for being ten years old. So does the labor — as if the hour of work had also spent ten years sitting in the sun.

Labor is often a large part of a roof-repair bill, sometimes close to half. Discounting it by the same percentage as the materials can cut a meaningful amount from the payout. Any specific dollar figure here would be an illustration built from average industry costs, not one documented family's bill — but the basic effect is real: money is subtracted from work that has not happened yet, at a rate that has not changed.

The labor did not get older. The calculation made it older.

Michigan's insurance regulator eventually put this in writing. In a bulletin dated July 3, 2024, DIFS Director Anita Fox stated that "nontangible items such as labor are not physical objects and do not deteriorate, age, or decline in value regardless of any potential decline in value to the physical property in need of repair." Later that year, the department issued a second bulletin. This one named more of the nonphysical costs insurers had apparently also been depreciating — taxes, fees, overhead, profit. The regulator had returned to its own subject and said more.

Courts have not agreed on what to do with any of this. A federal court predicting Kansas law allowed insurers to depreciate labor. Its reasoning: if only materials are discounted, a homeowner who repairs the damage cheaply, or does it themselves, ends up with extra money for labor they never actually paid for. Missouri's appellate court ruled the opposite way. Its reasoning: the policy language only covers "physical deterioration," and labor was never physical to begin with. Nebraska's supreme court went a step further than either. It allowed labor depreciation even when the insurance policy never mentioned it at all.

Same basic question. Three different courts. Three different answers.

Then there is Alaska.

On May 7, 2024, Alaska's insurance director, Lori Wing-Heier, issued a bulletin. It said plainly: "labor does not lose value and remains unaffected by any material property decline. Labor does not physically deteriorate and cannot be subjected to depreciation." The rule was set to take effect on January 1, 2025.

It did take effect. For about three weeks, this was simply the law in Alaska.

On January 23, 2025, Wing-Heier withdrew it. The withdrawal notice is two sentences long. It says the earlier bulletin no longer applies. That is all it says. It does not mention a lawsuit. It does not mention industry pushback. It does not explain why the rule was reversed.

Seven weeks later, on March 14, 2025, a bill with very similar language appeared in the Alaska legislature. It would eventually become law. Whether that bill is connected to the withdrawal is not something anyone has confirmed. No news report, no court record, and no official statement links the two events. They simply happened seven weeks apart.

The roof really was ten years old. The work needed to fix it was not. For about three weeks, Alaska's own rule treated those as two different things. Then the rule disappeared, and nobody was required to say why.
