---
title: "The Hour That Has No Age"
byline: Maya Flux

Some clocks come from the world. Others are put there by paperwork.

A roof really can be ten years old. Its shingles have spent ten summers in the sun and ten winters in the rain, and that weather has actually done something to them — worn them thinner, made them more brittle, brought them closer to failing. But the work needed to repair that roof next month has not been aging beside it. It hasn't happened yet. There is no ten-year-old version of that hour sitting somewhere, waiting its turn. And yet some insurance calculations let the roof's age and the labor's age grow old together, on the same line of the same estimate, as if they were the same kind of thing.

Home insurance often pays out what's called "actual cash value" after a loss — roughly, what the damaged roof was worth just before it broke, rather than the price of a brand-new one. For the shingles themselves, that's a reasonable idea. A ten-year-old roof is worth less than a new one, the same way a ten-year-old car is worth less than one straight off the lot, and insurers subtract a percentage to account for that. The strange part comes when insurers estimate the cost of the repair and split it into two pieces — materials and labor — and then apply that same age discount to both. The discount that belongs to the old shingles gets carried over onto work that hasn't happened yet. The labor did not get older. The calculation made it older.

This is not a hypothetical worry. It is a documented practice that regulators and courts in several states have had to sit down and decide whether to allow.

Michigan's insurance regulator eventually put the obvious distinction into writing. In a bulletin dated July 3, 2024, DIFS Director Anita Fox stated that labor "does not deteriorate, age, or decline in value" the way a physical object does — a sentence that exists specifically because insurers had been treating it as though it did. Later that year, a second bulletin followed, extending the same logic to other costs that don't age either, like fees and overhead. Someone in the state government had to keep coming back to the same idea and restating it, because the practice kept finding room to continue underneath it.

Not every court has agreed that this is even a problem. Missouri's appellate court looked at labor and concluded it cannot be depreciated, because the policy language covers "physical deterioration," and labor was never physical to begin with — there is nothing there to deteriorate. Nebraska's supreme court, confronting essentially the same question, ruled the opposite way, allowing labor to be depreciated even when the insurance policy never says insurers are permitted to do it. Two courts, one plain question, two opposite answers — proof that "actual cash value" was never quite precise enough to settle what happens once you try to age something that was never aging at all.

Then there is Alaska, where the same question got a rule, and then lost it.

On May 7, 2024, Alaska's insurance director, Lori Wing-Heier, issued a bulletin. It said, without hedging, that "labor does not lose value and remains unaffected by any material property decline. Labor does not physically deteriorate and cannot be subjected to depreciation." It was set to take effect for new and renewing policies on January 1, 2025.

It took effect. For a little over three weeks, that was simply the rule in Alaska — an insurer could not treat an hour of future labor as though it had already aged.

Then, on January 23, 2025, Wing-Heier withdrew it. The withdrawal notice runs two sentences. It states that the earlier bulletin no longer applies. That is the entire explanation. It does not mention a lawsuit. It does not mention industry pressure. It does not mention anything at all having changed about labor, or roofs, or depreciation, in the eight months between the two documents.

Seven weeks after that, on March 14, 2025, a bill containing very similar language showed up in the Alaska legislature, and would eventually become law. Nothing in the public record ties that bill to the withdrawal. No news report, no court filing, no official statement connects them. They are simply two documents that happened seven weeks apart, in the same small office, about the same handful of words.

The roof really was old. Rain and sun had actually changed it. The repair work was still ahead of it, waiting to be done, priced at whatever it would cost when someone finally climbed up there. For about three weeks, a bulletin from the state of Alaska kept those two facts separate, the way they actually are. Then the same director who wrote that bulletin took it back, in two sentences, and neither sentence said why.
