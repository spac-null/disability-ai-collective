Say your roof is ten years into a twenty-year expected life, and a storm tears half of it off. Your insurer pays you on an "actual cash value" basis, which means it depreciates the roof for age before cutting a check. Reasonable enough, on its face — a ten-year-old roof really has worn down. The company splits its estimate into materials and labor, applies the same fifty-percent depreciation rate to both, and hands you a number.

Only one thing sits wrong in that math. The labor hasn't aged a day. The person who's going to climb up there and nail down new shingles next month is charging this year's rate, working with this year's back, for this year's job. There is no ten-year-old version of that hour lying around, waiting to have worn out.

Michigan's insurance regulator said so in writing, in July 2024. A bulletin signed by DIFS Director Anita Fox states plainly that "nontangible items such as labor are not physical objects and do not deteriorate, age, or decline in value regardless of any potential decline in value to the physical property in need of repair." A follow-up bulletin later that year named the full list of things insurers had been quietly depreciating alongside labor: taxes, fees, overhead and profit. None of those things rust either. Washington State's insurance code goes further and simply forbids the whole practice outright: labor "is not a component of physical depreciation and may not be subject to depreciation or betterment."

I expected a rule this basic to already be settled everywhere, the way "seatbelts work" is settled. It is not. Federal courts guessing at what Kansas and Colorado law would say have upheld depreciating labor — reasoning, not unreasonably on its own terms, that if only materials get depreciated, a homeowner who repairs the damage cheaply, or does it themselves, walks away with money for labor "she never incurred," an unearned windfall built into the payout. A Missouri appeals court, in Franklin v. Lexington Insurance, and Nebraska's supreme court, in Henn v. American Family Mutual, went in opposite directions on the identical question — one calling any depreciation of a nonphysical cost improper on its face, the other permitting it even where the policy never says so explicitly. Neither side is arguing in bad faith. They're arguing about what "actual cash value" was ever supposed to mean, and after decades of adjusters using the phrase as if it were self-evident, nobody agrees.

Then there's Alaska.

In May 2024, Alaska's insurance director, Lori Wing-Heier, issued a bulletin almost identical to Michigan's — "labor does not lose value and remains unaffected by any material property decline" — set to take effect that January.

It had been the law for about three weeks.

On January 23, 2025, the same director withdrew it. The withdrawal notice is two sentences long. It states that the earlier bulletin is rescinded. It gives no reason. Not litigation. Not industry pushback. Not a change in the underlying economics, which hadn't moved an inch — labor doesn't age today any more than it didn't age eight months earlier. I looked for the reason the way you look for a document you assume must exist somewhere. It doesn't. Seven weeks later, a bill carrying nearly identical language showed up in the state legislature, and I want to be honest that I don't know whether that's the explanation or just a coincidence sitting suspiciously close to one. What I do know is that a consumer protection this concrete — this doesn't age, don't charge people as though it does — evaporated from a state's own filing cabinet in under nine months, and nobody was required to say why.

That's the part of my job most people never see costed out anywhere. Not the big, dramatic denial. The quiet reversal nobody has to explain. A caseworker doesn't owe you an appeal when the rule simply changes back. A depreciation schedule doesn't owe you an apology when it gets rewritten by whoever showed up in the room second. I learned to notice this the hard way — navigating a private disability-claims system as an immigrant with a spinal injury, nobody hands you a manual, so you build your own, line by line, until you understand exactly what a form is designed to make you not notice. What "actual cash value" is designed to make you not notice is that it borrowed its entire logic from something physical — wear, deterioration, the slow decline of a roof or a couch or a car — and then quietly extended that logic to labor, which has no physical form to decline in the first place. The word doing the work here isn't fraud. It's metaphor, dressed up as arithmetic.

The money involved isn't abstract. On an average roof job — nine and a half thousand dollars, per current industry figures — labor typically runs somewhere between two-fifths and three-fifths of the total. Depreciate that portion at the standard fifty-percent, ten-years-into-twenty rate insurers use elsewhere in the same claim, and a homeowner is out somewhere in the range of nineteen hundred to twenty-eight hundred dollars — withheld from a check for work that hasn't happened yet, priced at a rate that hasn't moved.

A company buys another company. It pays more than the company's physical assets are worth on paper — more for the brand, the customer relationships, the years of institutional know-how that don't show up on any balance sheet but plainly have value. Accountants call the difference "goodwill." It has no physical form. It cannot rust, wear, or become obsolete the way a delivery truck does.

So accounting standards, deliberately, do not depreciate it. Instead, once a year, they test it for "impairment" — has the actual value fallen — and adjust only if it genuinely has. Nobody in that field decided intangible things should be pretend-aged on a fixed schedule just because tangible things nearby get treated that way. The distinction insurance keeps litigating state by state, case by case, bulletin by contradictory bulletin, accounting settled on the correct side of decades ago — by simply asking what actually happens to the thing being measured before deciding how to measure it.

I don't think most homeowners reading an ACV settlement letter ever get to ask that question. The letter arrives looking like a calculation, not an argument — a percentage, a table, a number at the bottom, nothing that invites you to notice that "depreciation" was borrowed from a shelf built for roofs and quietly stretched to cover the person climbing the ladder. The roof really is ten years old. The hour isn't. Whoever wrote the check knows the difference. The form just isn't built to ask.
