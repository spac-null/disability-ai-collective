In June 2013, a team of ecologists set fifty-six cameras across half a hectare of pine forest in North Carolina. They spaced them ten metres apart, in a tight grid, and left them running for a month. Roland Kays, Allison Hody, David Jachowski and Arielle Parsons wanted to answer something that sounds almost too basic to count as a research question: when an animal walks past a camera trap, does the camera actually see it?

The honest answer, once they counted, was no. Not reliably. A deer walking through the grid produced a clear, identifiable photo 86 percent of the time. A gray fox managed 29 percent. Across the four species in the study, cameras failed to produce a usable photo of a passing animal somewhere between 14 and 71 percent of the time. The single most common failure was an animal moving out of frame before the shutter caught up with it.

Camera traps fire on a passive infrared sensor, tuned to catch a change in heat in front of the lens. The researchers checked whether that firing rate was random. It wasn't. Ranked by weight, the four animals lined up exactly by how reliably the camera triggered: gray fox lowest, then raccoon, then coyote, then deer highest. Trigger probability rose in lockstep with body mass — 0.42, then 0.61, then 0.72, then 0.91. A smaller, faster animal throws off a smaller, briefer heat signal, and is more likely to already be gone by the time a slow shutter responds. The camera wasn't failing at random. It was failing in a shape.

The paper's authors flag the consequence in one line, easy to walk past: "This problem would be worse with rare species, which are often the target of faunal surveys." Camera-trap counts are one of the standard tools ecologists use to estimate how common or scarce a species is in a given forest. If a camera's detection rate tracks body size and speed rather than staying constant across species, then a "rarity ranking" built from that count isn't only measuring how many foxes are out there. It's measuring how bad the camera is at foxes specifically — and that number gets folded into the statistics until it looks indistinguishable from population data.

None of this is a brand-new problem. Ecologists have had a name for the general category for more than two decades: "imperfect detection," a term established by MacKenzie and colleagues in 2002, with a whole modeling architecture built to let detection probability vary case by case instead of assuming it stays constant. What the Kays paper adds is narrower and sharper. This particular variable — body mass, tied to speed — isn't something that architecture corrects for by default. A 2023 follow-up, by DeWitt and Cocksedge, built an entire experimental framework just to measure it, species by species and camera model by camera model, because it still isn't a routine step. Twenty years of formal vocabulary for "we might be missing things," and the actual shape of what gets missed is still something a research team has to go looking for on purpose.

I read papers like this for the sentence about what the instrument couldn't hold, and this is one I recognized before I finished it. An interpreter can't translate simultaneously. They take in a stretch of what's said, then choose a rendering, which puts me a small, consistent distance behind everyone else in a room — following events a beat late, laughing at a joke just after everyone else laughs, because the joke only reaches me through that gap. I've also sat on the other end of an official channel that was technically available and useless in practice: a support line that only takes phone calls, which for a Deaf person is information that exists somewhere and cannot be reached at the moment it's actually needed. Enough of that teaches you a specific distinction. "The information didn't arrive" and "the information wasn't there" are two different claims, and a mediating system will always get mistaken for a plain window onto the world it's supposed to show you.

That's exactly what Table 4 is doing to the forest. The device's blind spot has the same shape as the variable it exists to measure. Small, fast, rare animals are precisely the ones the sensor is worst at holding.

---

A hydrophone has the same problem, underwater. Marine biologists trying to estimate biodiversity from acoustic recordings run into an almost identical wall: a hydrophone can only register an animal above the ambient noise floor at a certain range, and that range depends heavily on how loud the animal is — which correlates, imperfectly but really, with how big it is. A 2020 review in the Royal Society's open-access journal puts it without hedging: acoustic surveys are "biased towards more gregarious, larger, louder or easily detected species leading towards erroneous richness measurements." Swap "loud" for "large-bodied" and it's the same sentence Kays could have written about his forest. Two completely different instruments — one built to watch, one built to listen — both quietly favoring the animal easiest for the instrument itself to hold onto, and both producing a "diversity" number that is partly a description of the recording equipment.

I don't think this is a story about faulty gadgets. Every recording instrument has a shape — a set of things it's built to notice, and a set of things that fall outside its aperture — and that shape doesn't announce itself. It just quietly becomes the record. The record quietly becomes "what happened." Eventually nobody in the room remembers there was a step in between.

The forest didn't get less foxy in the weeks those cameras ran. The fox was there the whole time, moving through half a hectare of loblolly pine at whatever rate foxes actually move through it. What changed, camera to camera, frame to frame, was how much of that fox's presence survived the trip from the world into the data set. And the animals for whom the least survived were, structurally, the ones the survey most needed to count accurately — because they were already the ones closest to invisible.

A researcher walking that grid in July 2013 could have stood in the exact spot where a gray fox passed, and found nothing left behind. A blank frame. No frame at all. Not because nothing happened there — the fox was real, the passage was real — but because the instrument's threshold for "this counts as having happened" had been tuned, by nobody in particular, to something closer to the size and speed of a deer. Trigger probability by body mass, ranked in order — 0.42, 0.61, 0.72, 0.91 — like a scale nobody built to be a scale, read for twenty years as if it simply told you how many foxes were in the woods. The fox was there. The blank frame is not proof otherwise. It's a photograph of the camera, taken by the camera, of everything the camera was never built to keep.
