On July 29, 2025, Jaricus Whitlock, chief of the air division at the Mississippi Department of Environmental Quality, wrote a letter to Brent Mayo, a vice president at xAI. The letter confirmed that a set of gas turbines the company wanted to install at a site in Southaven would qualify as "mobile" — because each one would stay affixed to a flatbed trailer — and "temporary," because xAI intended each turbine to remain on-site for less than twelve months.

That's the whole test. Two conditions. Wheels, and a stated intention. Nothing about how much fuel the turbines would burn. Nothing about how much nitrogen oxide they would put into the air over Southaven and neighboring Memphis. Nothing about how long they would actually run once switched on. The letter set the test, and the test had nothing to do with the thing the test exists to regulate.

The federal rule behind that letter isn't obscure. Under the Clean Air Act, a piece of equipment normally treated as "nonroad" — a generator on wheels at a construction site, say — stops counting as nonroad and becomes a regulated stationary source once it sits in one place for twelve consecutive months. Below twelve months: lighter rules. At twelve months and a day: an entirely different, stricter regime. The rule was written with equipment that actually moves between short jobs in mind. It was not obviously written for a case where "mobile" hardware sits permanently at a data-center campus, running around the clock, because a company decided to power a supercomputer faster than the regional grid could connect it.

xAI had already leaned on this test once, at a first site, Colossus I in Memphis, where the public later discovered the company was running an estimated thirty-five gas turbines with no air permit at all. Shelby County's health department eventually permitted fifteen of them, months after the fact. When xAI planned a second facility, Colossus II, across the state line in Southaven, a senior infrastructure manager told a Memphis newspaper the company would be "copying and pasting" what had already worked once. Not a mistake, rediscovered. A method, reapplied.

Here is where the category starts to show its actual shape. Three turbines, early August 2025. Seventeen planned by September. Eighteen confirmed on-site by aerial photograph in late October — thermal imaging showed at least nine of them already running. Twenty-seven by December. In February 2026, after the EPA had already moved to tighten the federal rule on stationary turbines nationally, the NAACP, the Southern Environmental Law Center, and Earthjustice sent xAI formal notice of intent to sue. xAI's response was not to pause. Court filings from that May describe the company adding six more turbines, bringing the total to thirty-three, after receiving the notice. "Rather than stop," said Earthjustice attorney Laura Thoms, "they've added more turbines capable of inflicting even more harm to surrounding communities."

I want to be honest that I went looking for a tidier story than the one the record actually supports. I expected to find that xAI had been physically shuffling turbines between sites to reset the twelve-month clock — move a unit out before it aged into "stationary," bring in a fresh one, repeat. That's not what happened. The documented pattern at Southaven is continuous addition of new units at the same location, not movement of old ones between locations. The clock was never reset by relocation. It was never meaningfully started at all, because each new turbine arrived as its own separate "mobile" unit, and nobody — not xAI, not the state regulator who wrote the two-part test in the first place — treated the growing total as one continuous operation the rule should have caught.

Even EPA's own response complicates the version of this story where a loophole simply gets closed. The rule EPA finalized in January 2026 doesn't eliminate the mobile-turbine exemption. It writes a brand-new category alongside it — "stationary temporary combustion turbine" — with its own twenty-four-month clock and a real, if lighter, emissions standard. If the agency believed the existing rule already clearly barred what xAI was doing, building a new accommodating category for turbines like these would be an odd next move. What it looks like instead is a regulator acknowledging, after the fact, that its own category genuinely hadn't anticipated equipment like this, and choosing to write a wider door rather than simply lock the one that had been propped open.

A category can do this. It can be built to track one real thing — is this equipment genuinely temporary — and end up tracking something else instead, something a form can satisfy regardless of what's actually happening on the ground. That gap between what the label says and what's true doesn't announce itself. It just sits there, correctly filed, until somebody who already knows what a filed-but-wrong label feels like goes looking for the sentence that doesn't add up. I know that feeling from my own paperwork. A diagnosis, once it's written down, becomes an administrative fact about you, whether or not it still describes what's actually happening.

In 2004, something similar started happening to trucks. A "glider kit" is a brand-new chassis, cab and axle, built around a much older, remanufactured diesel engine — sold and driven as a new truck, but polluting at levels ten or twenty years out of date. The Clean Air Act's "new motor vehicle" rule technically asks whether a new engine was manufactured, not whether a newly operating vehicle is now on the road. Because the engine block itself wasn't new, some manufacturers argued the whole truck wasn't "new" either, in the sense the statute cared about — even though it rolled off the line yesterday and drove exactly like any other new truck.

Glider production rose from a few hundred a year in 2004 to roughly ten thousand a year by 2015, once a separate greenhouse-gas rule made ordinary new trucks more expensive and gliders a cheap way around it. EPA closed the gap in 2016. In 2017, under new leadership, the agency proposed reopening it — arguing formally that glider vehicles shouldn't count as "new" at all under the statute's literal words. The proposal never fully went through, but only because the underlying market later collapsed under stricter standards, not because the legal argument was rejected outright.

Same country. Two industries, a decade apart. One pattern: a category built to track a real thing — is this a newly polluting vehicle, is this a genuinely temporary power source — quietly redefined to track something else. Something a technicality can satisfy regardless of what's actually happening on the ground. Both times, the gap got closed only partway, and only after the industry that benefited from it had already gotten years of use out of it.

Go back to Whitlock's letter. Wheels, and a stated intention. A trailer under a turbine that hasn't moved in a year. A twelve-month clock that resets itself every time a new unit shows up, because "new" was never defined carefully enough to notice that the total kept growing. None of this required anyone to lie about what a turbine is. It only required a category that checks the wrong variable, and enough people willing to let the check stand in for the question it was supposed to answer. The turbines are still there, on their trailers, exactly where they've always been. That's the part "mobile" was never built to see.
