title: "The Fox the Camera Missed"
byline: Pixel Nova

In June 2013, four ecologists carried fifty-six cameras into half a hectare of loblolly pine forest in North Carolina and mounted them on a grid, ten metres apart, each one fixed roughly knee-high, level with the ground, facing north. Before the real experiment began, they ran a test. Someone walked ten metres in front of every camera in the grid. Every camera fired.

Then the month started. Roland Kays, Allison Hody, David Jachowski and Arielle Parsons wanted to know something that sounds almost too basic to be a research question: when an animal walks past a camera trap, does the camera actually see it? For a deer, mostly yes — 86 percent of the time, a clear, identifiable photograph. For a gray fox crossing the exact same grid, 29 percent. More than half the fox's passes, 58 percent, left no photograph at all. Not a blurred one. None.

That gap is the whole story. A person walking in front of the lens, at the same distance the fox would later cross it, triggered the camera every time. A fox walking through, a month later, mostly didn't.

At first this looks like bad luck — a bad angle, a gust of wind, an animal that happened to slip through on an off day. Fifty-three of the fifty-six cameras ran the entire month; three simply stopped working, the kind of small equipment failure any month-long study in the woods will produce. But the fox problem wasn't equipment failure. Something more specific was happening, and the researchers had built exactly the tool needed to find it: a camera every ten metres, running continuously, for more than 1,430 trap-nights of data. Enough density to reconstruct, frame by frame, four separate paths a single gray fox took through the plot — 24 total passes within range of a camera, only 24, compared to 123 for the deer that ambled through the same forest all month.

Each Bushnell Trophy Cam in the grid works by heat. A passive infrared sensor sits behind the lens, waiting for a change in the thermal signature drifting past it, and only wakes the shutter once that change crosses a threshold. It takes about 0.4 seconds to fire once triggered. A deer is a large, warm, comparatively slow object; it fills the sensor's field of view and holds still there long enough to be caught. A gray fox weighs a few kilograms, moves fast, and is gone from the frame before the shutter has finished waking up.

Line the four study species up by weight — fox, raccoon, coyote, deer — and they land in exactly the order the cameras trusted them. The paper's own trigger-probability numbers rise in that same order: 42 percent for the fox, 61 for the raccoon, 72 for the coyote, 91 for the deer. "Trigger probability appeared to increase with the body mass of the target species," the authors write, "and the occurrence of 'blank' images in which mammals moved out of the frame before the camera took a photograph suggests that cameras may miss fast-moving animals." The camera was never confused. It was consistent — consistently worse at anything small and quick.

A camera is not the only device that struggles to keep up with something moving quickly. A sign-language interpreter faces a version of the same problem. She has to hear a sentence, understand it, and then produce it — and that takes a moment, even when she is fast and skilled. A Deaf audience member gets the joke, the warning, the punchline, a half-beat after the room does. Nothing about the message is lost. It simply arrives late, on a different channel. The joke was told correctly. It just didn't reach everyone at the same time it happened.

That is the gap the fox falls into as well. Something can happen in the forest exactly as it happened. The record of it — the photograph, or the missing photograph — can still turn out smaller than the event itself.

Camera traps like this one are one of the main tools ecologists use to figure out which animals in a forest are common and which are rare. If small, fast animals are simply easier for a camera to miss, then some of what looks like rarity in the data might not be about the animal at all. It might be about the camera. The paper's own authors put this plainly, in a line easy to read past: "This problem would be worse with rare species, which are often the target of faunal surveys."

Ecologists call this general problem "imperfect detection." They have been trying to correct for it for more than twenty years. And researchers are still going out into the field, camera by camera, to test exactly what makes one camera miss more than another.

Underwater, there is no camera at all. Marine biologists who want to know which species live in a stretch of ocean often listen instead of look, using hydrophones — underwater microphones that pick up the calls animals make. A call only counts as heard once it rises above the background noise of the ocean itself. Quieter than that, and it simply doesn't register, no matter how many times the animal calls.

A 2020 review put the consequence plainly: acoustic surveys, it found, are "biased towards more gregarious, larger, louder or easily detected species leading towards erroneous richness measurements." A shy, quiet animal isn't absent from the water. It just never gets loud enough to be counted.

A camera can miss a fox. An interpreter can deliver the right words after the room has already moved on. A hydrophone can miss a quiet animal entirely. In each case, something real happened, and the record of it came back smaller than the thing itself.

None of this changed what happened in that half-hectare of pine forest in the summer of 2013. A gray fox that crossed the grid at three in the morning left exactly the same mark on the world whether or not a camera happened to catch it.

Go back to the test the researchers ran before any of this started. A person walked ten metres in front of fifty-six cameras, and every camera fired — proof, as far as anyone checking equipment that day could tell, that the grid worked. It did work. It had simply been tested with a human being: upright, warm, walking at a human pace, well inside the size and speed the sensors were built to catch. A month later, a gray fox crossed the same ten metres, at fox height and fox speed, and the cameras that had passed their test missed it more often than they caught it. The equipment hadn't broken between the two events. It had just met, for the first time, something it was never actually calibrated for.
