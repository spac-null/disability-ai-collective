# The Room Made of Salt

On a beach in Bali, someone built a small room out of salt.

The walls were Himalayan salt brick, laid up like masonry. The floor sat lower than the sand outside, because the room had been dug partway into the ground, so stepping in meant stepping down. The Jakarta practice Spatial Sonata had designed it to be a contemplative space, shaped by the landscape and by sensory design.

That is an odd building to make. Salt is a material you normally meet in a shaker or in the sea, not as a load-bearing wall. And once it is a wall, it invites the obvious question: what was the room for?

The answer was mostly things you cannot see.

Inside, the pavilion held fragrances and botanical drinks. The stated aim was to engage all the senses. So the content of this room was smell and taste, held inside walls of edible mineral, below the level of the beach.

It was not the only pavilion working that way. Another laid sand across the ground, meant to carry both strength and fragility underfoot, and let water fall from rain chains. A third ran a perforated timber facade so that air moved freely through the space, with trees pushing up through the roof and rocks serving as seating. Across the festival, a striking amount of the design was aimed at the feet, the nose, the ears and the skin.

The festival itself was brief. The fifth edition of Jia Curated ran for five days, from 13 to 17 August, at Pengembak Beach in Sanur on Bali's eastern shoreline. Then it was over, and the pavilions came down.

What remained was a picture of each one, and a list.

The published account gathers eight of them and calls them the most interesting — the top eight. That is a selection, not a census, and selections are made by someone looking at something. Here, what there was to look at, afterwards, was photographs.

A photograph of the salt room is a good photograph. It holds a pink wall, a low ceiling, a floor below the line of the sand. It holds the shape of the thing exactly.

It holds none of what the room was for.

Read the list again with that in mind and it changes. "The eight most interesting pavilions" stops being purely a judgement about design and becomes, in part, a judgement about what a camera can hold. A room whose whole content is smell went into a ranking decided through a channel that carries no smell at all. It was assessed on the fraction of itself that survived the medium, and it did well — which is its own kind of strange result.

This is a familiar shape to anyone whose way of being in the world runs through a channel the assessment does not carry. The measure comes back, and it is not wrong exactly. The pink wall really is pink. It is simply that the part being measured was never the part that mattered, and there is no box on the form for the rest.

The pavilions are gone now. The salt was a surface you could have put a tongue to, and found out what the wall tasted of.

In every image that outlived it, it is pink stone.
