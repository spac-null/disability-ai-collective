# The Room Made of Salt

On a beach in Bali, someone built a small room out of salt.

The walls were Himalayan salt brick. The room was set partway into the ground, so it belonged to the beach rather than standing on it. The Jakarta practice Spatial Sonata had designed it as a contemplative space, shaped by the landscape and by sensory design.

That is an odd building to make. Salt is a material most people meet in a shaker or in the sea, not holding up a wall. And once it is a wall, it raises the obvious question: what was the room for?

The answer was mostly things you cannot see.

Inside, the pavilion held fragrances and botanical drinks. The stated aim was to engage all the senses. So the content of this room was smell and taste, inside walls of edible mineral, half sunk into a beach.

It was not the only pavilion working that way. Another laid sand across the ground, meant to carry both strength and fragility underfoot, and let water fall from rain chains. A third ran a perforated timber facade so air moved freely through the space, with trees pushing up through the roof and rocks serving as seating. Across the festival, a striking amount of the design was aimed at the feet, the nose and the skin.

The festival itself was brief. The fifth edition of Jia Curated ran for five days, from 13 to 17 August, at Pengembak Beach in Sanur on Bali's eastern shoreline.

What travelled afterwards was a picture of each pavilion, and a list.

The published account gathers eight of them and calls them the most interesting — the top eight. That is a selection, not a census, and selections are made by someone looking at something. What there was to look at, afterwards, was photographs.

A photograph of the salt room is a good photograph. It holds the walls, the low ceiling, the line where the structure meets the sand. It holds the shape of the thing exactly.

It holds none of what the room was for.

Read the list again and the salt walls change meaning.

They photograph well. That is why the room is on it. What the walls were for does not photograph at all — and so the room placed among the eight most interesting on the strength of the one part of itself a camera could hold.

Being measured accurately, on the wrong sense, is a familiar result. The number comes back and it is not wrong. The walls really are that shape. It is only that the part being measured was never the part that mattered, and there is no box on the form for the rest.

The salt was a surface someone could have put a tongue to, and found out what a wall tasted of.

In an image, it is stone.
