In 1992 the physicist Lee Smolin published a paper called "Did the Universe Evolve?" and, five years later, a book for general readers, The Life of the Cosmos. Both set out a single claim. When a black hole forms, the matter crushed inside it bounces into a new universe on the other side, one that inherits the physics of its parent with small random changes. Universes that make more black holes leave more offspring. Over many generations, the population comes to be dominated by universes tuned to produce black holes—which is why, on the odds, we should find ourselves inside one of them.

That is the mechanism. It is not exotic in structure: reproduction, inheritance, variation, selection, the ordinary machinery of Darwin applied to universes instead of finches.

What happened next is the thing the anchor account is really about. Smolin's paper was not refuted. It was left alone. Julian Gough, an Irish writer who took up the idea decades later, went looking through the literature and found that the theory "hasn't been engaged with properly at all." Kevin Kelly, who considered it while writing a book in 1992, allows that it can look like "kind of a crackpot thing at first." Twenty-five years passed with the idea neither killed nor built on. That silence is the fact needing an explanation.

Gough offers one. Physicists, he argues, are unfamiliar with evolutionary theory, so the Darwinian logic of Smolin's idea reads to them as noise rather than argument. In the rare cases the idea reached the public, physicists were the ones explaining it—which he calls poor P.R. The people best equipped to judge the Darwinian reasoning, evolutionary biologists, had barely encountered it at all. This is Gough's characterisation of the neglect, reported here, not an adjudicated finding. But read as a claim about routing, it is precise: the theory's fate has tracked which discipline's trained reading it happened to land in front of.

The James Webb Space Telescope changed where it landed. On July 8, 2022, four days before Webb released its first data, Gough posted a set of predictions on his Substack. His method, he says, was plain: "You're just applying Darwin to universes." If the earliest universes reproduced through the simplest available mechanism, then supermassive black holes should have formed very early—within roughly the first 100 million years after the Big Bang—and driven the rapid assembly of stars and galaxies around them.

Webb found galaxies forming earlier, faster and more orderly than standard models expected. NASA reported galaxies assembling about 100 million years after the Big Bang, and a later observation found an unusually massive black hole in a galaxy seen 470 million years out. What the prediction did, inside this account, was less about proof than about form. It gave the argument a testable claim and a public timestamp—something that could cross a disciplinary boundary the reasoning alone had not. Afterward the people who found the logic legible were the ones Gough's routing account would predict: Johannes Jäger, an evolutionary systems biologist, who notes that Gough "actually predicted" the early structures, and the biologist Michael Levin, who says it is "worth exploring."

The evidence itself remains thin, and the account says so. The cosmologist Stephon Alexander calls the theory "a beautiful idea" but warns that standard models may also account for early black holes, and that the theory still has to be "embedded" in known physics and shown consistent with it. Gough calls what Webb offers "excellent circumstantial evidence," which is a careful phrase. Only a handful of named individuals, not a field, have found it worth their time.

So the account does not show the theory is right. It shows that whether the same claim read as noise or as signal has depended on whose trained equipment it reached, and that the Webb predictions mattered less as confirmation than as the form that finally made an unparsed argument legible past the boundary it had been stuck behind.
