Lily-Grace Conradie's student proposal at the University of Nottingham is called "Anguilla." The source describes it as a landscape-led environmental project that responds to the critical decline of the European eel and reimagines conservation as a public experience. It names itself a European eel conservation and education centre. That is the thing to hold onto before reading anything into it: a building meant to conserve a species and teach people about it.

A centre of that kind has an obvious job. It informs. Exhibition, education, community interaction — these are ways of putting a fact about the eel in front of a visitor and letting the visitor learn it. Read that way, the eel's plight is the content and the building is the container.

The source does not quite let that reading stand. It says the project is "informed by the eel's extraordinary lifecycle and the barriers that threaten its migration." The brief takes its cue from movement, and from movement that gets stopped. Not from a static fact to be displayed, but from a journey with obstacles in it.

Why that matters is a matter of the eel's biology. The European eel hatches in the Sargasso Sea and reaches European rivers as a transparent glass eel only 8cm long, having already travelled 6,500km, according to ZSL. It then has to move upstream to feed and grow. But rivers get in the way. ZSL and the river obstacles survey guide both name barriers in freshwater — weirs, dams, sluices — as one of the principal threats preventing the species' recovery, and both put the fall in recruitment since the 1980s at 90 to 95 per cent. The key conservation action, ZSL states, is to restore migratory pathways by removing barriers, or where that is not possible, mitigating them. The eel's life is a passage, and the crisis is that the passage keeps being cut.

Take the brief's cue seriously and the design problem changes shape. It is no longer "how do we show people the eel's decline." It becomes "how does the building organise itself around a migration that must be able to complete." The source's own words fall into place under that logic. "Anguilla operates as ecological infrastructure — reconnecting habitats, communities and waterways." Read through the blocked journey, "reconnecting" is not decoration. It is the organising instruction. The building's job is to reconnect an interrupted route.

The education follows from that, rather than the other way round. The source says the project reveals the hidden complexities of the river system while creating spaces for restoration, reflection and learning. Learning is still there. But it sits inside the larger task of continuity of passage, not above it. The centre does not gather visitors in front of the eel; it arranges itself along the thing the eel has to get through.

The limit is worth stating plainly. The source names the barriers only as the theme the project is informed by, and stops. It does not describe the building's form, its site, or any actual device that restores migration. The eel passes and the Thames figures belong to ZSL's own conservation programmes, not to Conradie's proposal, and are here only to explain why eel migration is obstructed at all. Whether "Anguilla" contains anything that reconnects a real river, the source does not say.

What the source does let us say is narrower and enough. On its own wording, "Anguilla" shifts the job of a conservation centre from displaying the eel's plight to organising itself around an obstructed migration — treating architecture as the thing that reconnects an interrupted route rather than a container for information about it. What that looks like as a building, the source does not tell us.
