## Did the point land?

Yes, and precisely. Unprompted, the reader stated it as: *"the brake eases exactly where the
data is thinnest or capped, so the instrument goes quiet where suffering is least counted"* —
and added that the only genuinely new fact the project produced "wasn't about rent at all".
That is the lens, recovered from the prose by someone who was never shown it, with the
`IMPLICIT` realisation intact. The Crip Minds warrant is therefore **recoverable**, which was
the hardest thing to achieve here.

Would finish it: yes. Would send it to someone: yes, "with a caveat".

## The reader independently found the architecture's weakest joint

The ORDER hold — *the public-housing paragraph asserts more than it shows* — is the same
structural fault my own read found, arrived at independently. Beat `B5` gives the NYCHA easing
and Blinder's interpretation of it in one short beat with two facts and no figures, directly
after `B4`, which supplied three figures before drawing anything from them. The architecture
put an interpretation where it had no material to stand on.

Classified per section 22 as **STORY ORDER**, and it is a genuine editorial defect rather than
a safety one.

## Note on the reader's "unbelievable" flags

The reader flagged three passages as possibly overclaimed. Two are false alarms and are
correctly sourced: the Windsor Terrace rent figures (`F13`, StreetEasy via Gothamist) and the
one-in-four brake cables (`F25`, the maker's own written account). The third — Blinder's claim
that the easing "shows public housing is working" — is accurately attributed (`F15`) and is
his assertion, not the article's; but the reader's discomfort is well founded, because the
article passes it on without the figures that would let a reader weigh it. That is the same
ORDER fault seen from the other side.

The reader did **not** flag the sentence that actually fails, which is worth recording: the
fabricated ride through an unmeasured block group reads perfectly naturally.
