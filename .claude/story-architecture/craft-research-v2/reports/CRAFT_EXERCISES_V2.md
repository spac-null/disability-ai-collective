# Professional craft exercises v2

Collected from the teaching sources, with the skill each trains and which engine stage could
eventually learn from it. **Nothing here is an implementation proposal.** Several of these
exercises have no machine analogue and are listed because they clarify what the human gate is
actually doing.

| # | exercise | source | skill trained | why it matters for Crip Minds | stage that could learn from it |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Reverse outline.** Work through the draft one paragraph at a time and write down the single main point of each. Ignore what you meant; record what is there. If a paragraph has no main idea it is fluff; if it has two, split it. | GMU and Univ. of Oklahoma writing centres (CT-15, CT-16) | seeing your own structure from outside | This is the only exercise in the set that is directly mechanisable and directly diagnostic. A per-paragraph "one job" check is exactly the constraint the writer packet already states as a rule but never verifies. | Continuity Editor (as a post-draft report, not a gate) |
| 2 | **Reader-question mapping.** After each section write: reader now knows / reader now wonders / text gives next. Then judge whether the next material answered a natural question, created a better one, or interrupted curiosity with background too early. | Banaszynski via CT-03; independently the annotator method in every Storygram | ordering information by curiosity rather than by outline | The best-evidenced finding in the campaign (F-01). It also produces a *checkable* artefact, unlike `why_reader_wants_next`, which can be satisfied by assertion. | Story Architect |
| 3 | **Ladder of abstraction.** Take the story's concrete bottom rung and its single abstract apex; write the middle rungs that connect them. Choose one path up, early. If the piece stalls, change rung. | Hayakawa via Clark, Gravitz, Netburn (CT-04) | controlling concrete↔abstract movement as pacing | Names the variable the architecture currently has no field for. Also supplies the warning: *"Not every worm story is about justice"* — sometimes the apex is low, and forcing it is the defect. | Story Architect · Worth Gate |
| 4 | **Ending upstream test.** When the planned ending is not working, run your eye up the page and the page before. You may have finished before you thought you did. | McPhee via CT-02 | recognising the last necessary moment | Directly addresses "fewer manufactured paragraph punchlines". Cheap to implement as a report: would deleting the final paragraph remove a concrete referent introduced earlier? | a post-draft check |
| 5 | **Scene / summary discrimination.** Label each passage as scene, summary, exposition or reflection; look at the resulting string for long unbroken runs. | CT-03 (WSJ vs braided models) | seeing the texture of a draft | The architecture cannot currently say "this beat is explanation" versus "this beat is a scene", so it cannot notice that six explanation beats are consecutive. | Story Architect |
| 6 | **Outline-transition deletion.** Delete transitions that announce the article's structure, then read. Keep every transition that points at the material — head-to-tail echo, contrast turn, "But wait", a dated launch-pad after a section break. | CT-25, CT-03, CX-08 annotations 7, 16 | making the *scaffolding* invisible, not the transitions | **Rewritten after V1 review.** The V1 seed and this pass both originally had "delete every explicit signpost" — and CT-25, the source cited for it, contradicts that: it teaches four visible devices approvingly and uses "signpost" as praise. Delete outline transitions; keep content transitions. Still form-conditional: Bregman signposts nine times in one argument essay. | Continuity Editor, gated on form |
| 7 | **Minimum explanation.** For each technical term ask: is there a reason to care before the term? Is there an example or a mechanism before the term? Is the gloss within one sentence? Is it re-explained later (it should not be)? | CT-03 (De Chant), CT-04, SC-05, BR-14 | jargon timing | Produces four binary checks per term. Scientias and Bregman independently converge on placing the explanation *before* the label. | Story Architect (`definitions` timing) |
| 8 | **Nut graf drafting, and nut graf refusal.** Draft it early and refer back to it — then ask whether the story needs one at all. Sometimes a single well-placed quote is the nut. Sometimes a strong narrative needs none. Never let it become a mini-outline. | CT-11 (Robson, Fromartz, Kwok) | orientation without spoiling | Direct support for `lens_realization: EITHER`. Kwok's warning — mini-outline nut grafs "feel formulaic" — is a description of what the Jia drafts were doing. | Worth Gate · Story Architect |
| 9 | **Lede as its own tiny story.** Write an opening that has a beginning, middle and end, and that offers a promise and establishes tension — not necessarily a summary. | CT-01 (De Chant, Quammen) | openings that are not templates | Cuts against the current hard requirement for a concrete opening object. Quammen's praised lede is a question about whether we should go somewhere at all. | Story Architect |
| 10 | **Interview for narrative.** Ask the same question several times; ask past the talking points; ask how big, what colour, soft or rigid, cool to the touch; ask a source to narrate their day step by step; ask what kind of flower and where it came from. | CT-05 (Mullin, Rubiano, Wilke), CT-04 (Paliwal) | acquiring the material vividness requires | **The single most important exercise for Crip Minds safety.** It says vividness is an acquisition problem. Our engine cannot interview, but it can be told to *seek* this class of material and to report when the material is absent rather than compensating. | Research Pack |
| 11 | **Mine the methods section.** "The mice were exposed to auditory stimuli" may mean researchers clapped next to rodents in a startle chamber. Chase citations in the introduction for tangential detail. | CT-05 (Wilke, Imbler) | finding action in dry documents | An acquisition technique available to a machine, unlike exercise 10. Method sections are documents we already ingest. | Research Pack |
| 12 | **Chronology versus braid.** Lay the material out in time order; then try reordering by difference or by theme. Pick the one that keeps a reader oriented. | CT-03, CT-07 (Rowińska: three camps could not be told chronologically) | structure selection | The choice depends on the shape of the material, which the ledger already knows. | Story Architect |
| 13 | **Cutting.** Adee: finding good background "is like sculpting" — it involves carving away unnecessary information. Sokol's annotator: what a science journalist leaves out is as important as what they leave in. | CT-03, CX-05 annotation 23 | omission as a positive act | PR #62 already requires a non-empty `cut_evidence` list with declared reasons, and checks adherence afterwards. This is the most craft-aligned thing in the whole implementation. | already implemented |
| 14 | **Number conversion.** For each quantity, decide: raw, compared, translated to scale, attached to a person, attached to a time, or left alone. Then check that most are not raw. | CX-12 annotation 2, CX-11 annotations 5/19, BR-04, BR-14, SC-05 | making magnitude imaginable | Six-way coding, one per number. Cheap and specific. | Story Architect |
| 15 | **Unconventional protagonist search.** Before defaulting to the researcher or the affected community, ask whether a supporting character, a junior researcher, a contrarian, an animal, an object, a body part or the idea itself would carry the piece better. | CT-06 | escaping the default centre | Crip Minds' subjects frequently have no available human. The taxonomy gives legitimate alternatives that do not require inventing a perceiver. | Story Finder |
| 16 | **Limitations as a section.** Write what the work does not establish as consecutive short plain paragraphs, and place them before the consequence. Remove hedging adverbs from the rest of the prose. | SC-09, SC-05 (observed practice, not stated teaching) | honest uncertainty that does not read as evasion | The most transferable Scientias import. Also relevant to the negative-admission audit: a limitations passage is about the *world's* uncertainty, not about the evidence's silence. | Story Architect · negative-admission audit |
| 17 | **De-interpretation.** After stating a figure, write the sentence saying what it does not license, if a reader would otherwise over-read it. | SC-19 ×2, SC-14 | preventing a specific misreading | Distinct from evidence-state commentary; PR #62 has no category for it (F-17). | Story Architect |
| 18 | **The pub test.** Robson: while reporting, jot down "key attractions" — *"What one detail would I drop into the conversation to stop their eyes glazing over and get them to ask me more questions?"* | CT-11 | identifying what is actually interesting | Note the shape of the question: the goal is to make the listener ask for more, not to tell them the point. That is the Worth Gate's job stated in reader terms. | Worth Gate |
| 19 | **Write the objection.** Write the reader's strongest counter-argument as its own paragraph, then answer it there. | BR-02 ×4, BR-14 ×2 (observed practice) | handling resistance rather than curiosity | Argument forms only; absent from all twelve control exemplars. | Story Architect, argument forms |
| 20 | **Verb pass.** Highlight every verb in the draft; make each one active, specific and evocative. | CT-05 | prose energy at word level | The professional annotators spend more attention here than on anything else (F-25). We have spent almost none. | Continuity Editor |

| 21 | **Minimum explanation.** Ask what the least explanation is that lets the reader follow the whole piece. Then consider dispersing it, or narrating how the explanation was discovered instead of delivering it. | CT-24 (Carl Zimmer) | avoiding explanatory collapse | *"How little explaining can you get away with?"* Once answered, you have a target. And the alternative to a slab of exposition is a sequence of discovery events (F-26). | Story Architect |
| 22 | **Scene-provenance tiering.** For every proposed scene, name which tier its material comes from: direct observation, debriefed observer, or recorded material. If none applies, there is no scene. | CT-27 (Lauren Kessler), CT-29 (Junger) | writing unwitnessed scenes honestly | The most valuable exercise in the set for Crip Minds, because it converts a prohibition into a positive test against the ledger (F-27). | Research Pack · Story Architect |
| 23 | **Dramatic-material plainness pass.** Find the most dramatic passage. Strip adjectives, adverbs and metaphors from it. Read it again. | CT-27, CX-10 annotation 5 | not forcing drama into already-dramatic material | Two independent professionals state this rule (F-28). It is the safety-compatible counterpart to exercise 20's verb pass, and it should run *after* it. | Continuity Editor |

## Which of these the engine should NOT try to learn

- **Exercise 10** cannot be executed by the engine. It can only be respected: when the material
  it would have produced is absent, the correct outputs are a different form, more acquisition,
  or a hold — never compensation. This is the campaign's clearest statement of where craft and
  safety meet.
- **Exercise 6** must never be applied unconditionally, and its original form was wrong. As
  first written — by the V1 pass and by this one — it deleted taught craft. Applied to an
  argument essay it would also delete the load-bearing structure.
- **Exercise 20** is the one place where a machine may do harm fastest: "make every verb
  evocative" is an instruction to embellish, and the Jia autopsy already records four invented
  concrete details ("the ears", a "pink" wall, a floor that "sat lower"). The verb pass belongs
  to a stage with zero factual freedom, which is where PR #62 has already put the Continuity
  Editor — and even there it needs the semantic delta gate that already exists.
