ARTICLE A
=========
On a beach in Bali, someone built a room out of salt.

The walls were Himalayan salt brick, and the room was set partway into the ground, so it belonged to the beach rather than standing on it. The Jakarta practice Spatial Sonata designed it to be a contemplative space, shaped by the landscape and by sensory design.

That is an odd building to make. Salt is a material most people meet in a shaker or in the sea, not holding up a wall. And once it is a wall, it raises the obvious question: what was the room for?

The answer was mostly things you cannot look at.

Inside, the pavilion held fragrances and botanical drinks. The stated aim was to engage all the senses. So the content of this room was smell and taste, inside walls of mineral, on a beach.

Other pavilions worked the same way. In one, sand on the ground embodied both strength and fragility, and water fell from rain chains. In another, a perforated timber facade let air move freely through the space, trees pushed up through the roof, and rocks served as seating.

Read that list again and notice what kind of description it is. The sand *embodied strength and fragility*. Not: the sand gave underfoot, or held the heat, or made a sound when you crossed it. What the material stood for, rather than what it was like.

The festival ran five days, from 13 to 17 August, at Pengembak Beach in Sanur on Bali's eastern shoreline. Then it was over.

What remained was an account and a list. The published version gathers eight pavilions and calls them the most interesting — the top eight. Across all eight of those descriptions, none reports what any person perceived: they give the symbols, the materials and the stated intentions.

So go back to the sand.

The account keeps what the sand was said to mean and loses what it was to stand on it. That is the shape of the whole record. It holds the meanings and drops the encounters.

Which puts the salt room in an odd position. Its walls translate: they can be described and photographed. Its content does not. It sits on the list of the most interesting, described in terms of the part of itself that made it into writing.

A record decides what kind of perceiving it can carry. Then whatever it carried becomes the thing anyone downstream can weigh.

The salt was a surface someone could have put a tongue to, and found out what a wall tasted of.

The account has it as brick.


ARTICLE B
=========
The fifth edition of Jia Curated ran from 13 to 17 August 2026 at Pengembak Beach in Sanur, on Bali's eastern shoreline. The new venue set the theme, Nature Weave, and shaped eight pavilions each built to respond to the landscape. The materials are the first thing to name. Rice husks and recycled plastic. Salt bricks. Raw teak slabs and timber offcuts. These are physical things before they are anything else, and several of the pavilions were built to do their work through them rather than around them.

Look at what the source says each structure does, and a pattern shows up that has nothing to do with how the pavilion looks. In DDAP Architect's The Intelligence of Surfaces, made for the laminate manufacturer Lamitak, a perforated timber facade let air move freely through the space and doubled as a display for product samples. Natural rocks became seating. Trees came up through the roof. The air, the stone underfoot, the trees — these are routed into the walk, not applied to a wall.

The same logic runs through Nawangseta's Bhumi Lingsir, built with Living Designs Bali. A hundred traditional Balinese Topeng Buduh masks mark the many faces of human nature. Sand on the ground stands for strength and fragility. Water falls from rain chains. The sequence is meant to be walked, and part of what it delivers reaches the visitor through the feet and the sound of falling water. Alunan Pesisir, by Vivere Group and Eugenio Hendro, sets natural rattan against acoustic panels made from recycled materials to form its side barriers — a choice that shapes what a person hears inside as much as what they see.

The sharpest case is Spatial Sonata's Oaken Tiltroom. It was built from Himalayan salt bricks from Tuscany Stone Boutique and partly embedded in the ground. Inside, according to the source, it featured Oaken's fragrances and Stillroom's botanical drinks, with the aim of engaging all senses. Read that plainly: the core of this pavilion is something you smell, taste and feel. The salt is under your hands. The room is sunk into the earth. A photograph of it carries the shape and almost none of the content.

That is the thing worth saying. Several of these pavilions locate their content in channels that sight does not carry. A festival like this is consumed by walking through and photographing — that is the standard way its work travels afterward. But a walkthrough recorded as an image registers the packaging and misses the mechanism. When a room is built to be smelled, tasted and felt, the picture holds the surface and drops what the design is actually doing. Reading the Oaken Tiltroom as a visual object mistakes the wrapping for the work.

This is a reading, and it should be marked as one. The source gives the designers' stated aims — DDAP's air and rocks, Nawangseta's sand and water, Spatial Sonata's fragrance and salt. It does not report what any visitor experienced, and I am not claiming it does. Nor is any of this an accessibility claim: nothing in the source says these pavilions were made for, tested with, or reached by blind or low-vision visitors, and "engaging all senses" is not a credential of that kind. The claim stays about these specific structures and how they are described to work.

Held to that limit, the point still stands. Where a pavilion is built to be smelled, tasted, heard and felt, the photographable walkthrough that defines how a design festival is consumed records the surface and misses the design — as far, and only as far, as the designers' own stated intent, which is all the source gives.
