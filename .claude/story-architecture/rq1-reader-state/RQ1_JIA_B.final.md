For five days in the middle of August, a stretch of shoreline in Sanur, on Bali's eastern coast, was given over to a design festival. It ran from 13 to 17 August at Pengembak Beach. Afterwards, a published account of the festival put forward eight of the pavilions built there as the most interesting.

Eight is a selection. It is not a count of everything that stood on that beach during those five days. What the account offers is a shortlist of eight, and those eight entries are the ones that carry forward from the five days into everything that comes after them. The festival lasted less than a week. The eight descriptions of it do not have an end date, and they are what remains available to anyone who was not on that beach in August.

One of the eight is the Oaken Tiltroom. It was constructed from Himalayan salt bricks, stacked up into walls, and the structure was partially embedded into the ground. Whoever came to it came to a room whose surfaces were salt.

Inside, the pavilion held fragrances and botanical drinks. It was made by a practice based in Jakarta, which set out to create a contemplative environment shaped by landscape and sensory design. The stated aim was engaging all the senses. That is the aim as given, and it is the reason the room was assembled the way it was.

Another of the eight was built to let the site pass through it. A perforated timber facade allowed the free flow of air. Trees punctured the roof and carried on upward through it. Natural rocks were set down and became seating, so that whoever sat did so on stone, under branches that belonged to the beach rather than to the pavilion. Air, trees and stone were each given a way through.

In a further pavilion, water came down rain chains and fell onto ground of sand. The sand embodied both strength and fragility. That is the meaning assigned to it: a material that holds and a material that gives way, present at once in the same surface, with water arriving on it from above.

Read across all eight of the entries, a pattern in what they contain becomes plain. Each one carries what its materials were said to mean. None of the eight reports what any visitor perceived. The timber facade is credited with the movement of air. The rocks are credited with becoming seating. The sand is credited with strength and fragility. The descriptions reach the intention held in the material and then stop there, at the edge of the room, without going in. Eight pavilions, eight entries, and in each one the material speaks for itself and for nobody else.

So the sand's strength and fragility is the whole of what is on the record about that ground. It is a claim about what sand is, made beside a pavilion, and it travelled. The pavilion stood for five days on a beach and then came down; the account of it did not. Anyone who weighs these eight afterwards weighs the meanings the materials were given, because those are what the record kept. The standing there was not part of what it carried, and so it is not part of what can be weighed.

That limit sits heaviest on the first of them. The Oaken Tiltroom was made of Himalayan salt, and salt is a material with a taste. A wall of it is a surface with a taste, and it is that whether anyone reached out to it or not. The account has a word for that surface. The word is brick.
