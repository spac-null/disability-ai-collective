A wall of Himalayan salt brick stood in a room set partway into the ground on a Bali beach. The whole pavilion was built from those bricks, and it was partially embedded into the ground. People went inside it.

The room was called the Oaken Tiltroom. The Jakarta practice Spatial Sonata designed it, aiming to create a contemplative environment shaped by landscape and sensory design. That was the stated aim, and the salt walls were the means.

Inside, the pavilion featured fragrances and botanical drinks. The stated aim was engaging all senses. So the room offered something to breathe and something to drink, held inside walls of mineral.

Other pavilions at the same festival were built on comparable terms. In one, sand lay on the ground and embodied both strength and fragility, and water fell from rain chains. In another, a perforated timber facade allowed free flow of air, trees punctured the roof, and natural rocks became seating.

Sand on the ground embodied strength and fragility. Rocks became seating. One is a claim about what a material stood for; the other is a claim about what it did. Both arrived in the same register, as though they were the same kind of statement.

The festival itself ran five days, from 13 to 17 August, at Pengembak Beach in Sanur, on Bali's eastern shoreline. After the fifth day the pavilions came down, and what remained was a written account and a list of eight. The Oaken Tiltroom is one of the eight pavilions the published account lists as the top eight.

The account presents these as eight of the most interesting, the top eight. A selection presented as the most interesting is a selection, not a count of everything shown. Eight is the size of the list, not the size of the festival.

What the eight descriptions carry is a particular kind of information. None of the eight reports what any visitor perceived. The sand embodied strength and fragility, which is a thing the sand was said to mean. The rain chains released water, which is a thing the rain chains did. Neither line reaches the fact that sand under a foot is a surface that gives, or that water off a chain lands somewhere. The meanings travelled. The rest stayed at Pengembak Beach and came down with the pavilions.

The sand's description, then, describes the record more than the sand. It was written to carry significance, and significance is what it carried. Anything downstream of the five days has that sentence and nothing beside it. A person weighing the pavilions a year later weighs what strength and fragility were said to amount to, because that is what was written down, and because what it was to stand on the stuff was not.

The same holds for the salt room, which is where the gap shows most plainly. A perforated facade can be described as letting air through, and the description does most of the work the facade did. Fragrance and drink are named as offerings, and the naming does very little of what an offering does. The account can say that the room was built from salt bricks and that it aimed at all the senses. Those two statements sit at different distances from the thing itself, and only the first arrives intact.

Salt is not only a material. It is a surface with a taste, and a wall built of it is a wall of that surface, in a way a timber screen or a natural rock is not. That much belonged to the five days and to the people who went in.

The record has one word for it. The word is interesting.
