Press & Background

Jascha Blume

Founder, Crip Minds — Amsterdam / Bologna

For press, curatorial, and festival inquiries.  ·  About the project →

Crip Minds is an experimental editorial publication created by Deaf artist Jascha Blume. It asks what disabled ways of seeing can reveal about ordinary subjects that the usual account has missed. Four fictional editorial personas give the publication continuity and character. Blume designs the voices, source rules, factual boundaries, and editorial process; AI works inside that authored system.

Blume was born and raised in Amsterdam. He is Deaf, Nederlandse Gebarentaal is his mother tongue, and he graduated from the Gerrit Rietveld Academie. His art has repeatedly examined what translation, timing, and format do to the reality a person receives.

At the academy, interpreter-mediated teaching made a practical tension visible: access to spoken information can depend on another person’s language and timing. In group exchange, translated information can arrive after the moment for response has already begun to move.

This tension appears in De Gebarentaaltolk en Ik, which works with interpretation, back-translation, and synchronized video. The versions remain related while showing that translation does more than carry an unchanged message.

Video is central to Blume’s practice because sign language unfolds through movement, sequence, space, and facial expression. Static notation cannot preserve all of that linguistic information. Retrieve My Time (2010) approached time and access through the act of catching up with what was not available when it first happened.

Deaf gatherings are another point of reference in his work: temporary places where sign language and visual attention organize communication from the beginning rather than being added later.

These works support a recurring question: who controls the form in which reality reaches another person? The answer may be an interpreter, a camera, a caption, an interface, or an institution.

Blume describes part of this as iets terughalen wat eigenlijk van mij is, maar op afstand van me is komen te staan: recovering something that is his but has come to stand at a distance. Terugeisen — reclaiming — is a thread rather than a complete explanation of every work.

In ordinary life, the same issue appears when contact information technically exists but cannot be used. Phone-only communication is inaccessible to Blume; written contact changes whether information is available in practice.

In Bologna, Blume’s work with L’Altro Spazio treated access as a starting point for a cultural space rather than an adjustment made after the main decisions.

This history matters to Crip Minds because it joins artistic form to practical participation. Information or access can exist on paper while failing in the moment someone tries to use it.

Crip Minds is the current project. Pixel Nova, Siri Sage, Maya Flux, and Zen Circuit are fictional editorial personas, not autonomous disabled people and not substitutes for testimony. Pixel has stronger factual roots in Blume’s documented Deaf practice. The others are constructed from disability knowledge, cultural history, scholarship, interviews, artistic practice, and authored constraints.

The personas are not fixed theories or subject departments. Crip Minds can begin with technology, labor, museums, cinema, furniture, bureaucracy, or an ordinary object. It asks what a disabled way of seeing makes knowable about the thing itself.

Behind the articles is a private editorial system called the Mind Engine. It looks for the detail that does not fit the obvious story, tests different ways of understanding it, and keeps bold interpretation accountable to the facts. Its exact mechanics remain part of Crip Minds’ working method.

Crip Minds separates factual ground from interpretation. A source supplies the floor; the editorial reading supplies the leap. A strong argument may go beyond what a source explicitly concludes, but it may not invent a new event, quotation, date, or biography to support itself.

The voices use disability-rooted ways of looking, but they do not claim human lives. Their first-person continuity is editorial construction, and no persona may fabricate personal memories.

Current production includes automated research, drafting, checking, and editorial selection. A fuller form of the Mind Engine is still being developed. The project does not present experimental work as already deployed or promise a fixed publication cadence.
